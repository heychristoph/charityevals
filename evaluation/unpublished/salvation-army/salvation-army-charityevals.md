### Summary

The Salvation Army is a church that runs the largest network of social services in the United States: food, shelter, housing placements, help with bills, holiday gifts, disaster relief, residential addiction recovery and community centres. Our model of its US work, for fiscal 2025, comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="salvation-army-charityevals">0.003</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. Its overseas work, about 2% of the programme spending, has a model of its own and comes to <span data-figure="headline" data-model="salvation-army-charityevals-international">0.3</span>. Weighted by spending, a gift to the Salvation Army comes to about 0.009 years of healthy life per $1,000, three-fifths of it from the overseas work.

That is far below the global-health charities on the site, and the reason is the scale, not the charity. The site values money and goods on one log-income scale. About $30 of help a person in a year, against a poor US household's consumption of about $18,000 a person, is a gain of under 0.2%; the same share of the consumption of a family living on two dollars a day is about a dollar a year. Of the US result, about half comes from food, goods, bills paid and services, a quarter from shelter, an eighth from the recovery programme, a tenth from housing placements and the rest from community programmes and worship.

None of the Salvation Army's programmes publishes a controlled evaluation of its own, so every part of both models is built from the research on that kind of work and counted at half (the site's shared slider for estimates resting on the literature or on judgment). The biggest uncertainties are what families would have got elsewhere, how well the charity's own counts reflect help delivered, how much the recovery centres change, and, abroad, how many pupils of the schools it helps pay for would otherwise not be in school, how much savings groups raise consumption, and how much of its support for poorer territories pays for services rather than worship.

### What the charity does

In the year to 30 September 2025 the four US territories, the national headquarters and the World Service Office spent $4.31 billion, $3.55 billion of it on programmes. The report counts 184 million meals, 15 million items of clothing, furniture and gifts, 1.4 million households helped with rent, utilities or medical bills, 10.3 million nights of shelter, 56,000 people placed in permanent housing, 155,000 people in substance-abuse and rehabilitation services, 4.8 million participants in community and Kroc centres and 208,000 church members, besides smaller counts for employment help, case management, veterans, prisons, clinics, anti-trafficking and disasters. It ran on 45,000 employees, 5,200 officers (its ordained ministers) and 1.55 million volunteers, most of them ringing bells at the red kettles.

The Adult Rehabilitation Centers, a six-month residential work-therapy programme for addiction, are paid for by the Salvation Army's thrift stores. The stores' sales ($553 million) pay their own running costs, and what is left funds the centres.

About $52 million goes abroad. The World Service Office (SAWSO) spent $19.7 million on programmes in 69 countries: $8.7 million of mission support for poorer territories, $3.5 million on livelihoods (savings groups, income generation), $3.1 million on disaster relief (mostly vouchers and goods for Ukrainian refugees), $1.6 million on education, $1.1 million on business development for church units (solar panels, rental space, a nursing dormitory), $1.0 million on health and $0.7 million on getting trafficking survivors home. The four US territories also sent £25.8 million (about $33 million) to International Headquarters in the year to March 2025, for the Self-Denial Fund, which pays maintenance grants to the territories that can't pay their own way, and for running the headquarters.

### How the impact happens

1. **Food, goods, money and services**: families get food, clothes, gifts, care and bills paid that they would otherwise have to buy or go without, and some earn more after job help or case management. We count this at what it would cost them, on the site's log-income scale at their own low consumption.
2. **Shelter and housing**: a bed or a home is worth a cheap room's rent to someone who would otherwise sleep rough. People sleeping rough die at almost three times the rate of people in shelters (Boston, 2000-2009), and homeless people at several times the rate of housed people; part of each gap is the roof itself.
3. **Recovery**: some residents stay sober who otherwise wouldn't, for a few years: less illness from alcohol or drug dependence and fewer deaths.
4. **Community and worship**: youth clubs, camps, seniors' groups and the congregation raise their regulars' life satisfaction a little.
5. **Abroad**: relief and social services reach poor households, for whom the same dollar is a far larger share of what they live on; savings groups raise consumption a little; a year of schooling raises later earnings; maternal and newborn care averts newborn deaths and stillbirths; congregations raise their members' life satisfaction a little; survivors of trafficking get home.

### How the models work

The [US model](/salvation-army/salvation-army-charityevals) follows the site's template for social services in rich countries, with six branches, each adjusted for its own evidence, for what would have happened anyway and for missing data, then bridged into healthy years by the site's shared conversions:

- **Cost**: all four programme lines, less the thrift stores' own running costs (we judge about three-quarters of the rehabilitation line is the stores, cross-checked three ways), less day-care fees (day care is valued net of them) and less the overseas work, plus management and fundraising spread over them. Donated food and goods are in the cost at their value, as the help they are; the volunteers' hours, at the US volunteer wage, lower the charity's share of the credit. The figures both models read sit in one shared place, so the two always split the accounts in full.
- **Food, goods, money and services**: every meal at the grocery cost of USDA's Thrifty Food Plan (about $2.70), items, day care and services at their cash-equivalent value, bills paid at face value (about $73 a household, from the Western Territory's audited cash assistance; veterans' temporary help at $500), job help and case management at the earnings they add, all spread over the households served and valued against their consumption, about 30% of the US average per person in 2025 dollars. Half of it, we judge, families wouldn't have got from SNAP, another pantry or another agency.
- **Shelter**: a year in shelter is worth $20 a night against consumption of 15% of the average; the deaths it averts are the gap between sleeping rough and sheltered death rates, of which we credit 30% to the shelter, for the half of nights that would otherwise have been spent without a roof.
- **Housing placements**: each placement adds about half a year housed against usual care (a Housing First trial for supportive housing, the Family Options Study for rapid re-housing and diversion), valued against the person's own alternative, plus a fifth of a homeless adult's death rate removed, for the Salvation Army's share (70%) of the resources behind the placement.
- **Recovery**: about 3 extra lasting remissions per 100 people counted, dropouts included, for three years each, at the shared disability weight of moderate alcohol use disorder, and the excess deaths of continued heavy use they avoid.
- **Community and worship**: small life-satisfaction gains for participants and members, mostly discounted for what they would have found elsewhere.

The [overseas model](/salvation-army/salvation-army-charityevals-international) follows the template for relief abroad, with one branch for each kind of work the money pays for, each on the chain its own family uses (consumption gains on the log scale, later earnings from schooling, deaths valued by age, life satisfaction). SAWSO publishes no count of the people its grants reached, so reach is spending over a cost per person from the literature or our judgment, and every branch is thin; every branch waits about a year and a half before the money is spent (SAWSO's reserves, International Headquarters' year of grants held in reserve, delivery):

- **Operating support** ($42.8 million of budget: SAWSO's mission support, its business development as the running costs it saves over the assets' life, and the territories' world-services money). We judge 55% of it ends in services and 45% in worship and church life, from the international Army's counts of corps, schools, clinics and relief. The services split into schools (35%), health care (25%) and social services (40%); where they reach follows International Headquarters' grants by territory: Africa 29%, South Asia and the Pacific 26%, Latin America 20%, Eastern Europe 8%, the rest richer countries.
  - **Schools**: about $150 a pupil-year; a quarter of the pupils would otherwise not be in school; each extra year of schooling raises earnings 9% over a working life (the global average return).
  - **Health**: SAWSO's health projects and the support for clinics and hospitals, at about $150 a birth, with the Cochrane review's 25% fewer newborn deaths and 19% fewer stillbirths, discounted to 40% because much of the work is other care.
  - **Social services**: meals, homes and relief at 80% of their cost to the people served, at the consumption of poor households in each region.
  - **Worship**: about $40 a member-year, valued as the US model values its congregations' life satisfaction.
- **Livelihoods** ($3.5 million): savings groups at about $40 a member; a 5.5% gain in household consumption for two years, from the meta-analysis of 24 trials of saving promotion in Africa, pulled down by the largest trial, which found none.
- **Education projects** ($1.6 million): about $400 a child, the median learning gain of evaluated programmes in poor countries (0.1 standard deviations), 40% of it lasting into adult life.
- **Relief** ($3.1 million) and **anti-trafficking** ($0.7 million): goods and vouchers at their cash value to refugee households; survivors' life satisfaction for the share of their repatriation SAWSO paid for.

### What drives the result

- **How much we count a programme whose results aren't measured**, a shared slider (10-100%): every branch of both models is thin, so both move one for one with it.
- **The exchange rate between healthy years and income**, a shared slider: almost everything is income, so both results move nearly in inverse proportion.
- For the US work: **how well the counts reflect help delivered** (60-100%), **what families would have got anyway** (30-80%), **how poor the clients are** and the recovery programme's effect, each moving the result by up to a quarter.
- For the overseas work: **what a school place costs and how many pupils would otherwise not be in school** (each moving the result by up to half), **the savings groups' effect on consumption** and cost per member, and the shared discount rate (most of the schooling's value comes decades later).

### Biggest uncertainties

- No study measures what Salvation Army clients would have got without it; US families in need use SNAP and several pantries and agencies.
- The recovery centres and the housing placements have no controlled evaluation of their own; the anchors are completion rates, a 1978 follow-up without a control group, the residential-treatment literature, a Housing First trial and the Family Options Study.
- The split of the rehabilitation line between the stores and the programme isn't published; three cross-checks put the programme at 12-34%.
- Abroad: SAWSO's spending by sector and the territories' contributions to International Headquarters are published, but nothing on reach or results; what the operating support turns into is a judgment from the international Army's counts of its services.
- The service counts are touchpoints, not unique people, and the national summary is a combination of audited statements that is not itself audited.

### What isn't modelled

In the US: wellbeing beyond the money value of help for survivors, prisoners and disaster victims, spiritual care and community ties (+), the overdose risk after relapse and funging inside the organisation (-); we judge they roughly cancel. Abroad: the savings groups' gains in women's say over household decisions and in business, maternal deaths averted, the nursing graduates' later care and the territories' addiction, prison and elderly-care work beyond its cash value (+), and funging inside the international Army (-), together about +5%.

### How this model was checked

The mechanical audit (lint, invariants, shared figures) passes for both models. Building the first version showed that the engine's discounted-years function counted a whole first year for effects shorter than a year; it now counts the fraction (with a test).

The first version (3 November 2026) went through three rounds of independent audit (1, 1 and 1 high findings; 7, 3 and 1 medium), each fixed: housing placements were added as a branch, programmes with counts were put on the chain, meals priced at grocery cost, the placement branch's double counting of usual care removed, bill help derived from audited cash assistance, the shelter and food counterfactuals made consistent, and the overseas work separated from the US help. The last round's fixes had not been re-audited.

The rebuild (4 November 2026) follows the site's new rule for missing data: no programme is valued at other models' result any more, and every branch carries the shared missing-data discount. The overseas work, which the first version valued at the median of three other charities' models and which then carried four-fifths of the result, now has its own model built from what SAWSO's annual report and the International Trust's accounts say the money funds and from the research on each kind of work. Every US branch, none of them evaluated, is now discounted too, which halves the US result. Two points the first version left open are fixed: US consumption is now in 2025 prices, and the summary adjustment rows are weighted by each branch's outcome instead of a simple average.

The rebuild's first independent audit found 1 high, 2 medium and 6 low issues in the US model and 5 high, 6 medium and 6 low in the overseas model. The US high: the job-search help's earnings were a typed present value; they are now a yearly gain over its years at the shared discount rate. The overseas highs: the operating support, four-fifths of the money, was valued only as cash-equivalent services; it is now split into schools, health, social services and worship, each on its own chain. The savings-group effect, the largest driver, was a judgment not derived from its evidence; it now comes from the meta-analysis of 24 trials weighed against the largest trial. The relief branch used no shared PPP factor; it now reads Ukraine's and Moldova's (new facts), and the social services read each region's. Money waiting in SAWSO's and International Headquarters' reserves is now a discounted wait in every branch instead of a share of unmodelled benefits. The fifth high, that development work sits as branches of a relief model when the build rules want one model per family, is answered by keeping one overseas model whose branches each follow their own family's chain and shared rows: splitting $1-4 million lines into four models would cost more than it tells. Of the mediums, credit is no longer shared twice (reach is already the US money's share), administration is no longer discounted twice, the territories' support is read from the International Trust's accounts ($32.9 million instead of a $20 million estimate), where the lives are follows the grants by territory, the nursing dormitory is under business development, and the costs per child and per birth have reference classes. In the US model the recovery programme's anchor now cites the reviews, count quality is anchored on the site's other US service models, and a small wait for funds held is in every branch. One low was checked and not taken: the Western Territory's world-services support sits in the other-social-services column of its functional expenses, not the rehabilitation column, so the thrift-store adjustment doesn't remove it.
