### Summary

Habitat for Humanity International (HFHI) helps low-income families in more than 60 countries build, extend and repair their homes through Habitat national organisations, and works with lenders and building-material firms so that poorer households can buy housing loans and products. In the year to June 2025 it spent $75.5 million on this international work and $24.3 million on public awareness and education. Our estimate of a dollar given to HFHI for these two programmes is **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals-international">0.10</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. HFHI's US affiliates work, its largest programme, is modelled separately ([US model](/habitat-for-humanity/habitat-for-humanity-charityevals)) and comes to <span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals">0.008</span>.

It is well below cash transfers because only about 18 cents of each dollar reaches families as housing, half the families are in middle-income Latin America and Europe and better off than cash recipients in East Africa, and a home improvement is an in-kind transfer. Public awareness and education publishes no outcome data and is valued at the US result, discounted.

### What the charity does

Outside the US and Canada, most of Habitat's direct work is incremental: a floor, a room, a roof, a latrine at a time (265,000 people in FY2025), plus repairs (178,000 people), a few new or rehabilitated homes (30,000) and disaster shelter. HFHI's money reaches it as grants to national organisations ($31 million) and through its area and country offices' staff ($28 million). Its Terwilliger Center for Innovation in Shelter, MicroBuild and the national offices' market programmes work with microfinance institutions and firms; Habitat counts 2.4 million people in households that bought from these partners.

### How the impact happens

1. A family gets better housing than it would have built itself that year, paid for by Habitat net of what the family repays: a transfer, enjoyed over the years the improvement lasts.
2. Some improvements (cement floors, latrines, stoves) also improve health.
3. Market partners sell housing loans and products on better terms than the families' alternatives; the surplus over the next-best option is the benefit.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's cash-transfer template with two branches:

- **Direct housing.** HFHI's audited transfers to international partners ($31.6 million), times the share of them for direct housing (80%, a judgment: HFHI doesn't split its spending) and the share national organisations pass on to families as materials, labour and subsidy (72%), divided by a net subsidy of about $1,500 a family, gives the families HFHI's own transfers reach. The subsidy is spread over the improvement's life and valued per person on the site's log-consumption scale at each region's consumption (the World Bank's regional averages, put on the household-survey scale, times how far below the survey mean Habitat's families are), weighted by where the families are. HFHI is credited with 80% of the resources behind that housing (national organisations' own staff, volunteers and sweat equity share the rest). Spillovers to local builders and a small share for child deaths averted are added.
- **Market development.** Habitat's count of 2.4 million people reached through partners (as low as the Form 990's 300,000 Terwilliger households in the range), a surplus of about $100 a household, counted once, at somewhat higher consumption, with only the share of partners' sales due to Habitat (30%), a discount for the reliability of the count (80%) and HFHI's share of Habitat's market work (70%).
- **Public awareness and education** ($24.3 million): no outcome data, so it is valued at the nearest evaluated programme's result, Habitat's US affiliates work, read live and counted at the shared discount for programmes we could only estimate from similar ones.

### What drives the result

- **The value of a healthy year against a doubled income** (a site-wide slider).
- **The market surplus per household** and **how many partners' sales Habitat caused**: the market branch is about two-fifths of the result.
- **The share of the transfers national organisations pass on to families**, and the families' consumption in each region.

### Biggest uncertainties

- HFHI publishes no split of its international spending between direct housing and market work, and no average subsidy per family.
- The market branch rests on Habitat's own count of partners' customers and on judgments of what Habitat added.
- Health effects are measured for cement floors in Mexico, not for Habitat's mix of work.

### What isn't modelled

Tenure security from land and titling help, disaster resilience of the homes, and Habitat's Home Equals advocacy for informal settlements are a positive share on top, not modelled one by one.

### How this model was checked

Not published: three rounds of independent review each found a high finding. Round 1 (1 high, 6 medium, 5 low): the share of spending reaching families was applied to the wrong cost base (rebuilt on the audited transfers line). Round 2 (1 high, 2 medium, 6 low): families' consumption was on the national-accounts scale (moved to the survey scale). Round 3 (1 high, 3 medium, 7 low): the survey ratio came from stand-in countries but multiplied richer regional averages; fixed after round 3 by taking each region's level from the stand-in countries' own survey means (result 0.096 to 0.12), not yet re-reviewed. Open mediums: no Habitat national organisation's accounts anchor nso_delivery_share; the market surplus treats product buyers like borrowers. Move this file and the model back to writeups/ and models/global-health/, and re-add the programme to charities/habitat-for-humanity.json (weight $99,806 thousand), once a fourth review finds no high.
