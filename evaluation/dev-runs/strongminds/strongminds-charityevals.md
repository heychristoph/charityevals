### Summary

StrongMinds treats depression with six weeks of group talk therapy, mostly for adolescents and adult women in Uganda and Zambia. Our own estimate of its 2025 work comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="strongminds-charityevals">3.2</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**.

The result rests on how big and how lasting the effect of the therapy is once it runs at scale. The literature on talk therapy in non-Western countries finds large effects. The one randomised trial of StrongMinds' own delivery, with adolescent girls in Uganda, found an effect about a third that size, gone within two years. We weight the two equally. The plausible range is wide. Most of it comes from that question and from how a point of life satisfaction compares with a year of healthy life.

### What the charity does

StrongMinds runs group interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT-G): groups meet weekly for six weeks with a trained lay facilitator to talk through what is behind their depression and to support each other. In 2025 it treated 745,224 people, up from 426,642 in 2024. Most were in Uganda and Zambia, and the rest were treated through partners in Ethiopia, Kenya, Malawi and Nigeria. About a third (some 267,000) were adolescents aged 15–24, and only 3% were treated by StrongMinds' own staff.

Fewer and fewer of the groups are run by StrongMinds' own staff. Most are now led by volunteers recruited, trained and supervised by government staff, who in 2025 covered about 9% of program costs in Uganda and Zambia. NGO partners run others, and in Zambia so do peer facilitators who are former clients. StrongMinds spent $11.4 million in 2025. A US programme, StrongMinds America, closed at the end of 2024 because it didn't work.

### How the impact happens

1. People screened as depressed (recruited through community sensitisation sessions) join a therapy group and attend some or all of the six sessions.
2. Their depression lifts by the end of therapy, and the gain fades over the following two years or so.
3. Other people in their household are a little better off too.
4. A few suicides are averted while the effect lasts.

### How the model works

- **Cost.** StrongMinds' audited 2025 program spending ($7.32 million), with management and fundraising spread over it. That multiplier is ×1.56, because administrative costs are a large 27% of spending. It comes to about $15 per person treated, the same as StrongMinds' own figure.
- **Effect.** An even blend of two sources. One is the six trials at low risk of bias in a meta-analysis of psychotherapy for adult depression in non-Western countries (Cuijpers et al. 2018: 0.51 standard deviations). The other is StrongMinds' own randomised trial (Baird, Özler et al. 2024): 0.73 points on the PHQ-8 depression scale among adolescents who attended, about 0.17 standard deviations.
  - The weight starts from how precise each source is, allowing for how much trials differ from one another. It is then lowered because the trial was an untypical first attempt.
  - The blend is 0.34 standard deviations at the end of therapy, fading to zero over 2.6 years, the pace of the trial's 12- and 24-month follow-ups among attenders.
  - StrongMinds' own before-and-after figure, a 12-point fall on the PHQ-9, is not used: it has no control group, and in the trial the control group improved a lot too.
- **WELLBYs.** Standard-deviation-years of depression become WELLBYs at the site's shared rate. The household's other members gain the site's shared share of the effect (household size 4.7 in both Uganda and Zambia). The WELLBY rate then turns them into healthy years, calibrated so a year of depression counts the same as through the Global Burden of Disease weights.
- **Adjustments.** Together they keep a little over half of the effect (×0.54):
  - a 25% discount for a short, scaled programme run by volunteers (six sessions, against the trial's 14) and for self-reported outcomes;
  - 84% of the credit to StrongMinds, since governments, NGO partners and volunteer facilitators contribute too;
  - a 15% discount for clients counted who didn't get the therapy as studied.

  Nothing is taken off for treatment people would have had anyway, because the trials' control groups already had it. After everything, the effect counted per client is about 1.1 times what StrongMinds' own trial found among attenders.
- **Suicides.** People with major depression die by suicide about 7.6 times as often as the population as a whole (we apply it to women's lower rate). If the therapy removes 15% of that excess risk while it lasts, it averts about one suicide per 30,000 people treated, valued like every death on the site. That is about 2% of the result.

### What drives the result

- **The value of a point of life satisfaction** (a shared slider) moves the result most.
- **How long the effect lasts**: 1.5 to 4 years moves the result between about 1.9 and 4.8.
- **StrongMinds' trial result, the evidence discount and the weight on the trial** each move it by between a fifth and nearly two-fifths either way.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether StrongMinds' trial or the literature is the better guide.** The trial was StrongMinds' first attempt with adolescents and through a partner organisation, therapy ended just before COVID-19, and attendance was low. StrongMinds has since changed its adolescent model. Adults treated by its own staff may do much better, as the older Ugandan trials of the same therapy suggest. StrongMinds says it is investing in a new trial of its current six-week model, which would settle much of this.
- **Quality at scale.** Most clients are now treated through government volunteers and partners, who are less closely supervised. The client count, which tripled in two years, is StrongMinds' own and isn't independently verified.
- **Household spillovers** rest on few studies.

### What isn't modelled

- Effects beyond depression that StrongMinds reports (food security, children's school attendance, work), except as they show up in depression scores.
- StrongMinds' work to get governments to take on and pay for the therapy, which could make future treatment cheaper.
- Any benefit of the community sensitisation sessions (about a million people in 2025) for people who don't go on to join a group; their cost is counted as part of recruiting clients.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits, each by a separate reviewer with no access to our notes:

- **Round 1** (1 high, 2 medium, 8 low). The high finding was that the model used 2024 when StrongMinds' 2025 audited accounts were already published. The model was rebuilt on 2025's costs, clients and staff pay, which raised the result by about half, mostly because the cost per person fell from $23 to $15. The weight on StrongMinds' own trial now starts from a calculation of each source's precision (40% to 50%). The effect's duration now uses both of the trial's readings (2.5 to 2.9 years). The deduction for treatment people would have had anyway is gone, since the trials' control groups already had it. Suicides now use women's suicide rate and are discounted. Labels and citations were corrected.

- **Round 2** (0 high, 1 medium, 4 low). The duration's second reading rested on a wrong argument from round 1 (the intention-to-treat estimates stay flat only because more attenders were reached at 12 months), so it is back to the per-attender shape, 2.6 years (−10%). StrongMinds' share of the credit is now worked out in dollars for each contributor (85% to 87%). Explainers and the write-up now give the adolescent share, the six sessions against the trial's 14, and the 3% of clients treated by StrongMinds' own staff.
- **Round 3** (0 high, 0 medium, 2 low). The volunteer facilitators' time is now priced in StrongMinds' share of the credit, and the NGO partners' cost per client has an anchor (87% to 84%, −3%). One range in this write-up was corrected.
