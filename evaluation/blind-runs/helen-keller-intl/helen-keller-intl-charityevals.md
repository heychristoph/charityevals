### Summary

Helen Keller Intl works with ministries of health in Africa and Asia on nutrition, eye health and neglected tropical diseases. This model covers its two largest measurable programmes in the fiscal year to June 2025: twice-yearly vitamin A for 47 million children under five in 14 African countries, and mass treatment of 32 million people for neglected tropical diseases in seven African countries. Together they come to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="helen-keller-intl-charityevals">14</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**; GiveDirectly comes to about <span data-figure="headline" data-model="givedirectly">4</span>.

This is charityevals' own estimate. It is built from Helen Keller's audited accounts, Form 990 and reports, the Cochrane review of vitamin A, the long-run deworming trial in Kenya and UN mortality estimates. It doesn't use any evaluator's analysis of Helen Keller.

### What the charity does

In cash programme spending of $94.3m:

- Nutrition, vitamin A included: $76.9m.
- Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs): $13.1m, plus $10.5m of donated Mectizan.
- US vision screening: $3.6m.
- Eye health abroad: $0.7m.

### How the impact happens

1. **Vitamin A.** Helen Keller pays for training, transport, supervision and campaign days. Government health workers and volunteers give the capsules, which UNICEF and others supply.
2. Children given vitamin A every six months die less often from infections. The Cochrane review of 19 trials (1.2 million children) finds 12% fewer deaths from all causes (95% interval 7% to 17%).
3. The model applies that cut to today's death rate of children aged 6–59 months in the countries reached. That rate comes from UN IGME's 2023 under-five, infant and newborn mortality, weighted by children reached: about 1 death per 95 children a year.
4. Each death is valued at the site's moral weight for an under-five death. The saved children's later income is added by the site's shared ratio of income value to the value of deaths averted.
5. **NTDs.** Community drug distributors give ivermectin, albendazole, praziquantel and azithromycin against river blindness, lymphatic filariasis, schistosomiasis, intestinal worms and trachoma.
6. Children dewormed at school age earn more as adults. The Kenya trial's 20-year follow-up found 14% higher consumption after two to three extra years of treatment twice a year.
7. The model scales that gain three ways: to the share of moderate-to-heavy infection here against the trial's 37%, to Helen Keller's mostly annual rounds, and to the share of the treated who are school-age children.

### How the model works

The model has one branch per programme, each with its own adjustments:

- **Evidence.** Vitamin A takes the shared trial-quality figure, times a judgment that today's effect is three quarters of the trials'. Deaths from measles and diarrhoea, the causes vitamin A acts on, are a smaller share now. Deworming's single trial gets 0.6.
- **Counterfactual.** Children who would have been treated anyway. For vitamin A, that is through routine clinic visits. Routine delivery reached 13% of children in Tanzania against 76% with campaigns, and fixed sites reached 63% against 91% door to door in 13 African countries. For NTDs, it is the site's shared figure.
- **Credit by share of resources.** For vitamin A, about 43%. That is Helen Keller's cost per child against the whole campaign's, which Helen Keller puts at a little more than $1 a dose. The rest is ministries' staff time, volunteers' hours and capsules. For NTDs, Helen Keller's cash against the donated Mectizan, other donated drugs and the distributors' time, about 41%.
- **Delivery.** Tally sheets against swallowed doses.
- **Funging.** Other funders who might have paid for the campaigns anyway.
- **Waiting time.** Restricted vitamin A funds at year end are about 2.5 years of vitamin A spending. They earn about 1.6% interest meanwhile, and the wait is discounted at the shared rate.

Cost is each programme's spending plus its share of management and fundraising: about $0.90 per child for a year of vitamin A and $0.50 per person treated for NTDs. Vitamin A's share of nutrition spending (45%) is anchored on Helen Keller's pledge of at least $35m a year for about 40 million children. Staff pay is 40% of expenses, from the accounts, at the shared pay gap for health and field staff.

### What drives the result

- **The vitamin A effect.** That covers the trials' mortality cut, how much of it holds today and how many children routine care would have reached anyway.
- **Partners' share of the vitamin A campaigns' cost.** Helen Keller's "a little more than $1 a dose" for the whole campaign means partners pay a bit more than Helen Keller does.
- **The worm burden.** Recent surveys in Burkina Faso and Sierra Leone find under 1% of school-age children with moderate-to-heavy infection, against 37% in the Kenya trial. The model takes 2% to allow for schistosomiasis hotspots and reinfection. The trial's consumption effect also matters.
- The shared sliders: the value of a healthy year, the discount rate and the value of a life.

Vitamin A is about 84% of the value. Per dollar, the NTD branch comes out at about half of vitamin A's, before counting adults' illness.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Reach is Helen Keller's own administrative count, not an independent coverage survey.
- Helen Keller doesn't split NTD treatments by disease, so the NTD branch rests on judgments with wide ranges.
- The year includes the end of US government funding, which cut NTD spending from $22m to $13m. The next dollar's NTD work may not look like this year's.

### What isn't modelled

- **The rest of nutrition**, about $42m or 45% of cash programme spending. It covers treatment of acute malnutrition (61,500 children), maternal supplements, home gardens, food fortification and breastfeeding support. Some activities have counts, but none has a spending split, so the result covers vitamin A and NTDs only, about half of cash programme spending.
- **Adults' illness from river blindness, filariasis and trachoma**: blindness, lymphoedema and trichiasis. The site has no shared value for disability in its units, so the NTD branch understates. A rough judgment puts it at a few per cent of the result, perhaps 1–8%. Mass ivermectin against river blindness across Africa averted an estimated 8.2 million DALYs in 1995–2010 (Coffeng et al. 2013), on the order of 0.01 a treatment or less.
- US vision screening and eye health abroad, each under a tenth of spending.

### How this model was checked

Three independent audits (audit-cea, each by a fresh reviewer without the builder's notes) and the site's mechanical checks (lint and every normalisation invariant: no violations). No round found a high-severity problem.

- **Round 1** (10 medium, 6 low) led to these changes:
  - Vitamin A credit is now built from the campaign's whole cost.
  - The saved children's later income is counted through the site's shared ratio.
  - The deworming effect is scaled for annual rather than twice-yearly rounds.
  - The worm burden is built from the trial's sourced prevalence.
  - Adults' NTD illness is no longer a share of children's income.
  - The external-validity discount no longer counts the DEVTA trial twice.
  - Vitamin A's share of nutrition now treats the pledge as all-in.
  - The counterfactual and funging questions were separated.
- **Round 2** (3 medium, 6 low) led to these changes:
  - Partners' cost per child now comes from Helen Keller's "a little more than $1 a dose".
  - The worm burden is anchored on recent surveys in Burkina Faso and Sierra Leone (2% instead of 5%).
  - Smaller fixes to the waiting time, the income wait and the illness share.
- **Round 3** (6 low) led to these changes:
  - The routine-care counterfactual is anchored on coverage studies (0.65 instead of 0.75).
  - Source details and wording.

Together these took the result from 25 to 14 years of healthy life per $1,000.
