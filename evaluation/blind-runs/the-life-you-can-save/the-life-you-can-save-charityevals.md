### Summary

The Life You Can Save recommends about thirty global health and poverty charities and gets people to give to them: through its own giving channels in the US and Australia, and by influencing gifts made to the charities directly. In our estimate each dollar it spends does **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="the-life-you-can-save-charityevals">68</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**, by way of the charities it moves money to.

This is charityevals' own estimate, built from the charity's 2025 money-moved report, its 2025 financials summary and its Form 990 for 2025. It comes out more than ten times the median global-health charity on the site because each dollar moves about nine dollars to charities that are themselves among the site's better results. Its result is a multiple of theirs. It is not a separate intervention.

### What the charity does

The Life You Can Save grew out of Peter Singer's book of the same name. It researches and recommends charities, almost all in global health and poverty, and runs outreach to get people to give to them: the book (about 20,000 copies given away in 2025), its website and newsletters, workplace and online giving, and donor services. Donations can go through it: the US entity grants them on, and the Australian entity (a separate registered charity) does the same for tax-deductible gifts from Australia. They can also go to the charities directly, and the charities, donors or giving platforms then report that it influenced the gift. In 2025 it also ran a Rapid Response Fund with Founders Pledge to fill gaps left by the cuts to foreign aid.

### How the impact happens

Money moved → the recommended charities' programmes → healthy years. The charity has no outcome of its own. Its value is what the money it moves achieves, less what that money would have achieved anyway.

### How the model works

- **Cost.** The operating expenses of both entities in calendar 2025, the latest year with a filed Form 990 and a published money-moved report. That is $2,186,513 in the US (the 990's total expenses less the grants it passed on) and A$653,207 in Australia, converted at the IRS's 2025 average: $2.61 million in all. The grants passed on are the money moved. They are not the cost of moving it. All operating work serves the one programme of moving money, so the overhead multiplier is 1.
- **Money moved.** The six channels of its money-moved report add up to $24.1 million by its own count, or $23.9 million with the Australian gifts at the site's rate. The three channels that rest on someone reporting the gift ($4.5 million) are scaled up by a quarter (our judgment, 1–2×). The charity says only about half its charities report such gifts and fewer than 100 donors did, so they are undercounted. Founders Pledge, which co-ran the Rapid Response Fund, takes a quarter of the $3.1 million of that fund the charity counts (our judgment, 0–50%). That leaves $24.3 million, about $9.3 per dollar of cost.
- **Donated services.** The audited statements count $250,000 of donated services in the US. They stay out of the cost and lower the charity's share of the credit to 91%, as for every model on the site.
- **Value of a dollar moved.** This uses the site's one method for meta charities (docs/normalization.md 2.8). The destinations are the charity's own split of 2025 money moved by charity, each charity at its live result on this site. Half of the money is new money (the shared figure, 35–80%). The other half counts only for the gain over where it would otherwise have gone (GiveDirectly's live result times the shared multiple, 1×).
- **The next dollar.** This is a past average: one year's money moved over one year's cost. So the next dollar is valued at the shared share of the average (the slider "How much the next dollar does compared with the average dollar so far", 50%).
- **Staff pay.** Its staff pay is 82% of its US operating cost, from the 990. The pay gap is the shared one for research and advisory staff. Both count only with credit by share of resources on, as do the donors' own new money.
- The template's policy and career branches are 0, because it does neither.

### What drives the result

- How much the next dollar does compared with the past average (the slider): from 18% to 100%, the result runs from about a third of the headline to twice it.
- Credit by share of resources: when it's on, the donors who give the new money share the credit, and the result falls to about a sixth.
- The value of a healthy year against a doubled income, the discount rate and the value of a child's life all move the destination charities, so they move this model too.
- Where shifted money would have gone moves it by up to a third: down at 3× GiveDirectly, up a sixth at 0. The new-money share (35–80%) moves it from 5% lower to 10% higher.
- How complete the reported gifts are (0.9–2× what was reported) moves it from 6% lower to 14% higher.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **New money.** The site's 50% is one figure for every meta charity. Two features of this charity cut both ways. Much of its money passes through its own giving channels, and some of those donors would have given to the same charities anyway (for example Australians looking for a tax-deductible route).
- **Whether the next dollar does as well as the average.** Spending rose 43% in 2025 (staff, systems and research), and money moved rose 18%.
- **Destinations without a model.** Fourteen recommended charities and the Rapid Response Fund ($10.7 million, 44% of the by-charity total) have no model on the site, among them the Fistula Foundation, Sanku and Educate Girls. They are valued at the average of the modelled ones.
- **Self-reported influence.** Gifts the charities or donors attribute to it rest on "where did you hear about us?" answers, not on a measured counterfactual. They are also undercounted by its own account (only about half its charities report them, and fewer than 100 donors reported theirs), and a gift reported by both a charity and its donor may be counted twice. The completeness judgment covers both, from 0.9× to 2× what was reported.

### What isn't modelled

- Pledges and future giving it inspires in 2025. Only gifts made in 2025 are counted.
- Its influence on how other people and organisations think about giving (the book's ideas, media). That is not measurable here.
- Gifts it influenced that went through GiveWell, which the charity itself excludes.
- Where the confidential gifts went. The by-charity table excludes them by its own note, yet it sums to $24.11 million, slightly more than the channel total of $24.07 million, so the two don't reconcile exactly. The split is used only for shares.

### How this model was checked

Mechanical checks (lint, every invariant of docs/normalization.md) and independent audits by a separate reviewer with no access to the builder's notes, each of which re-opened every cost and reach figure in the charity's documents.

- **Round 1** (1 high, 1 medium, 6 low). Fixed: the $250,000 of donated services in the audited statements now lowers the charity's share of credit (Convention 2). The self-reported channels of money moved got a ranged completeness judgment, since the charity says they undercount. The career branch was restored at 0. Four explainer and write-up corrections were made, including the count of unmodelled destinations in the shared moved-money row (14 charities and the Rapid Response Fund).
- **Round 2** (0 high, 0 medium, 5 low). Fixed: leftover template sources were removed from the unused career rows. The completeness judgment's low end now allows for gifts counted twice (0.9). The summary and write-up wording was corrected (credit shares, sensitivities, the by-charity reconciliation).
