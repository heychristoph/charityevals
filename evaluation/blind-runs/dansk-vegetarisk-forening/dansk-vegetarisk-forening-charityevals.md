### Summary

Dansk Vegetarisk Forening (DVF), Denmark's vegetarian society, founded in 1896, campaigns for more plant-based food in Danish policy, kitchens, shops and education. This estimate covers its domestic policy work, the one part of its work with an outcome that can be followed to animals: Denmark's Fund for Plant-Based Foods, which DVF lobbied for from 2020, won in the 2021 agricultural agreement (675 million kroner for 2023-2030) and helped top up by 420 million kroner and make permanent in the green tripartite agreement of November 2024. Set against five years of DVF's policy spending, its share of the meals the fund's grants are expected to replace comes to about **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="dansk-vegetarisk-forening-charityevals">0.28</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. That is well below most animal charities on the site, and the result is mostly uncertainty: almost every step from a krone of fund grants to a meal not eaten is a judgment.

### What the charity does

DVF had 24-27 staff in 2024 and spent about 13.0 million kroner ($1.9 million) that year, up from 3.8 million in 2020. It reports six programme areas: policy (the plant-based food fund, the national action plan for plant-based foods, the right to a plant-based meal in public kitchens, lawsuits against greenwashing), business (the Green Heart and V-label labels on about 600 products, the Green January campaign with 53 companies, advice to supermarkets and caterers), education (training for kitchen and health professionals, the Plant-Based Knowledge Centre it runs with Økologisk Landsforening), inspiration and information, cooperation, and international work (from 2025 a project to spread the Danish fund model to other EU countries). Much of its project work since 2022 is itself funded by public funds, including about 3 million kroner from the plant-based food fund in 2024.

### How the impact happens

Politicians decide to spend public money on plant-based food; the fund grants it to kitchens, caterers, retailers, producers and researchers; their projects put more plant-based meals on menus and plant-based products on shelves; fewer animal-product meals are eaten, so fewer animals are farmed. DVF's contribution is at the first step, alongside other green groups, the plant-based industry association and the parties that negotiated the agreements.

### How the model works

- **Cost**: DVF's audited spending, 2020-2024, in 2024 prices (World Bank consumer price index), less money set aside in provisions for later years, less administration; times domestic policy work's share, a judgment of 25% (15-40%) from the policy team's headcount, year by year; plus overhead. This is a past average: five years of policy work against the two wins they produced, so the shared slider for how much the next dollar does compared with the average applies.
- **Wins**: the fund's yearly budget from both agreements, about 154 million kroner a year in 2025-2030, with a fifth of it credited to each campaign year. Each year of grants won counts for the site's years a won reform counts before it would have come anyway, after a wait of about 1.7 years from each win to its first grants, discounted like every future benefit. The fund's permanence is what makes the slider's higher settings meaningful.
- **Effect**: animal-product meals replaced per krone of grants during the projects, a judgment of 0.025 (0.0025-0.125), worked from the fund's largest school-food grant (1.44 million plant-based meals targeted for 10.5 million kroner) and the share of the fund spent on comparably direct projects; as many again after the projects end (0-2 times), each stream discounted to its middle. Each replaced meal is valued at the site's shared figure for a replaced meal, with the shared production response and the meal-mix ratio for Denmark (1, from half to double).
- **Adjustments**: 0.7 for diners eating animal products elsewhere instead and self-assessed reporting; 0.7 for projects that would have been funded anyway; DVF's share of the advocacy behind the fund, 25% (10-50%), worked from staff-years of the organisations involved; and 8% (0-20%) for the campaign's other wins: the 580 million kroner of EU farm support for food crops, the national action plan and research funding for plant-based food.
- **Your dollar**: DVF's own staff costs (two-thirds of spending, from its accounts) and the shared pay gap for campaigners; then the species step, which splits a replaced meal's suffering between fish, chickens and pigs and weighs each against a chicken.

### What drives the result

The meals a krone of fund grants replaces is the largest of DVF's own uncertainties: the fund began measuring effects only in 2025, and much of its money goes to product development, export and research whose effect on Danish meals is slow. Next come the shared figures for a replaced meal and for fish, the years a won reform counts, DVF's share of the credit and the policy share of its spending.

### Biggest uncertainties

- No project funded by the fund has yet reported meals replaced; the per-krone figure is a worked judgment, not a measurement.
- DVF's share of the credit: the fund came out of negotiations in which DVF was one voice among several organisations and parties, though the one that made it a priority throughout.
- DVF publishes no spending by programme, so the cost of its policy work is a headcount-based judgment.

### What isn't modelled

- DVF's other five programme areas, about 75% of its programme spending, including the EU project staff listed under policy: labels, Green January, advice to supermarkets and caterers, the Plant-Based Knowledge Centre's kitchen projects, training, public information, and its international work. None publishes outcomes in meals or animals, so the result covers its policy work only, and a donation's effect through the rest is unknown rather than zero.
- Other policy wins beyond a small share (8%) for the food-crop premium, the action plan and research funding for plant-based food: the lawsuits on vegan meals in public institutions and on Danish Crown's climate claims, and any effect of the fund's model spreading abroad.
- Effects of the fund outside meals (climate, health, farm incomes).

### How this model was checked

Three independent audits (audit-cea, each in a fresh context, blind to evaluators' analyses of DVF) checked every source and the site's standard steps.

- Round 1 (1 high, 4 medium, 2 low groups): the benefit of menus lasting after a project was not discounted (split into meals during the project, a persistence multiple and the project's length); the policy share was cut from 30% to 25% after the staff page showed most of the policy staff work on the EU project; DVF's credit share was worked from staff-years; the campaign's other wins were added as a share; the wait was lengthened; sources added for the 2026 round and the counterfactual; the money-moved and career branches, unused, were removed.
- Round 2 (1 high, 2 medium, 4 low): both meal streams are now discounted to their middle and the wait runs to the first grants (1.7 years); persistence is anchored on the Danish worksite-canteen study; research-funding wins added to the other wins (8%); the evidence adjustment split into compensation and reporting with anchors.
- Round 3 (0 high, 1 medium, 4 low): wording and citations only (the persistence anchor's citation and working, the fund's dates and web address, this write-up's list of other wins). The remaining medium is the lint's note that the result is under a tenth of the animal median, which the summary explains.
