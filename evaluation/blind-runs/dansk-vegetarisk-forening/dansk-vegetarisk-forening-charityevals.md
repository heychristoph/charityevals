## Summary

Dansk Vegetarisk Forening (DVF, the Danish Vegetarian Society) is a Copenhagen-based association of about 4,100 members and 27 staff that works for a more plant-based Danish food system. **This model covers only its grant-funded work with professional kitchens and foodservice in 2024**, about a quarter of its spending, most of it run through Plantebaseret Videnscenter (the Plant-Based Knowledge Centre DVF founded with Økologisk Landsforening). It is not a rating of the whole charity, and it doesn't describe what a private gift funds.

Best guess: about **0.009 suffering-adjusted days (SADs) averted per dollar, about 0.002× GiveDirectly**. That is far below the other diet-change charities on the site, for three reasons: DVF advises and trains kitchens rather than securing meal commitments, and measures no meals; we credit only part of any change to DVF; and Danish canteen meat is mostly pork and beef, which carry fewer SADs per meal than chicken, while some replacement dishes contain egg and cheese. The range is enormous: the effect of a one-day workshop on what a kitchen serves is our judgment, not a measurement.

DVF's best-known work is political: it campaigned for Denmark's national action plan for plant-based foods and its plant-based food fund (Plantefonden: 675 million kroner for 2022–2030, since raised, plus 420 million agreed in 2024 and made permanent). That work is probably its largest impact and isn't in this model.

## What the charity does

DVF names six programme areas: policy, business, education, public information, partnerships and international work. In 2024 its kitchen and foodservice projects were:

- **Grøn køkkenrejse** (green kitchen journey): one-day regional workshops that helped school and college canteens (44 institutions over three rounds, two of them held in 2024) write action plans for greener canteen food.
- **Plant-rich food for older people and patients**: recipes richer in legumes and nuts tested in three care-home kitchens and one hospital kitchen, with DTU and Herlev-Gentofte Hospital.
- **Plant-based conference food**: nudging workshops in four hotel and conference kitchens, with MeetDenmark.
- **Festivals and attractions**: workshops for caterers at festivals, concert venues and theme parks.
- **Food on the go**, a **wholesaler project** (inspiration sheets delivered to 5,000 eateries with Cater Grønt's deliveries), the **Foodservice Network** and the **Green January** campaign (53 companies).

Since late 2024 it has also helped boarding-school kitchens (50 schools in 2025) serve two plant-based meals a week; that project spent almost nothing in 2024 and belongs to a 2025 model.

## How the impact happens

Kitchens that serve more plant-based meals replace meals with meat, fish, eggs or dairy. Fewer animal products bought means fewer animals bred and raised in farms, after allowing for the fact that production falls by less than one for one when demand falls. The change lasts as long as the kitchen keeps its new menus.

## How the model works

- **Cost.** DVF's audited 2024 accounts are split by type of cost, not by programme. We take the grants from Plantefonden (3.07 million kroner) and the fruit and horticulture levy fund (0.35 million), keep the share spent on kitchen and foodservice projects (93%; the retail benchmarking project is excluded), take out the part that may reimburse overhead (12%, since Plantefonden allows an overhead allowance on wages), and add about one person's time from DVF's own funds: about 3.1 million kroner, $456,000. Spreading management and fundraising (about 15% of spending, a judgment because the accounts have no functional split) gives **$536,000**. A 2.7 million kroner transfer to restricted provisions is excluded from total spending: it is money set aside, not spent.
- **Reach.** No meals are counted, so we estimate them in two branches:
  - (a) about 41 institutional kitchens × 40,000 meals a year × 7% of meals switched to plant-based, where a kitchen acts: about 115,000 meals a year; half the kitchens act;
  - (b) commercial outlets reached by light-touch outreach selling about 6 million meals a year × 2% switched, where an outlet acts: about 120,000 meals a year; 15% of outlets act.
  Together, a meal-weighted 32% of the 235,000 potential meals change: about 75,000 meals a year.
- **Effect.** The shared figure of 0.90 SADs per reference meal, scaled to the Danish species mix (0.28, below) and net of eggs and dairy in the replacement dishes (a quarter of the saving given back): 0.19 SADs per meal. Production response 0.76. Persistence 3 years for institutional kitchens and 1 year for commercial outlets (2.4 discounted years, meal-weighted).
- **Adjustments.** Evidence 0.85 (compensation at other meals, publication bias); half the change wouldn't have happened anyway; DVF gets half the credit, shared with its project partners; the delivery share above (0.32). Combined: 0.068.
- **Your dollar's share.** Staff pay is 66% of spending; with a 20% pay gap, cash donations pay for 86% of the full cost.

Result: about 5,600 SADs for $536,000: 0.010 SADs per dollar, or 0.009 counting staff's salary sacrifice.

**What the replaced meals contained.** The shared SADs-per-meal figure describes a chicken- and fish-heavy canteen meal. Danish households' fresh-meat purchases are 42% pork, 26% beef, 2% veal and 28% poultry (GfK 2015, via Landbrug & Fødevarer; canteen meals aren't covered, and no canteen mix is published). Per kilogram, chicken involves about 22 days of animal life, pork 2.8 and beef 1.9 (from the lifespans and yields in Brian Tomasik's analysis). Weighted by Rethink Priorities' welfare ranges, a kilogram of pork carries about a fifth of the welfare-weighted animal-days of a kilogram of chicken, beef about an eighth; allowing that a day is worse for a fast-growing broiler than for a pig or a beef animal (Tomasik rates suffering per day at 3, 2.5 and 1) brings these to about 0.17 and 0.04. DVF's climate-framed work probably cuts beef more than average, so we scale the reference meal by 0.28 (0.15–0.6). Some of the modelled projects promote plant-rich rather than fully plant-based food, and egg is the most animal-day-intensive food per kilogram, so we assume a quarter of the saving (0–60%) is given back by eggs and dairy in the replacement dishes. We don't model kitchens switching beef to chicken, since DVF promotes legumes, not poultry, but that risk is why the species-mix range goes low.

## What drives the result

- **What the replaced meals contained** (the shared SADs-per-meal figure, 0.08–1.7): 0.0002× to 0.004× GiveDirectly; plus the Danish species-mix scale (0.15–0.6): 0.001× to 0.005×; and eggs and dairy in the replacement (0–60%): 0.003× to 0.001×.
- **The share of a kitchen's meals that changes** (2–20%): 0.001× to 0.006×.
- **Kitchen size** (15,000–100,000 meals a year): 0.001× to 0.005×.
- **How long an institutional kitchen's change lasts** (1–6 years): 0.001× to 0.004×.
- The shared exchange rates between animal suffering and human wellbeing move the result as much as any of these.

### Why this is so far below Sociedade Vegetariana Brasileira

The closest model on the site is ACE's model of Sociedade Vegetariana Brasileira (SVB), about 1.75× GiveDirectly, some 750 times this model's result.

| | SVB (ACE) | DVF kitchens (this model) | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meals per dollar a year, before adjustments | about 28 (2.5 million meals in committed institutions for about $90,000) | about 0.45 (235,000 potential meals for $536,000) | ~60× |
| Share of those meals that change | 1 (commitments counted as delivered) | 0.32 (kitchens acting on advice) | ~3× |
| Years credited | about 5 | about 2.4 | ~2× |
| Other adjustments | 0.78 (share of programme cost not falling in future years) | 0.21 (evidence × counterfactual × attribution) | ~3.7× |
| Production response | not applied | 0.76 | ~1.3× |
| SADs per meal | 0.084 | 0.19 | ~0.45× |
| Share paid by cash donations (salary sacrifice) | 0.74 | 0.86 | ~0.86× |

The ratios multiply to roughly 750×, the gap per donor dollar.

Most of the gap is reach per dollar: SVB's model counts meals in institutions that have committed to plant-based days, while DVF's kitchen work gives advice without commitments and its grants also pay for festival, takeaway and network activities whose effect on meals is small (and the meals that Green January and the Foodservice Network may change aren't counted). The rest is the adjustments, which are judgments; with every DVF-specific judgment and shared fact at its most favourable end, and the shared exchange rates at their defaults, the model reaches about 1.9× GiveDirectly.

## Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether the advice changes menus.** DVF publishes no purchasing data, plant shares or meal counts for any kitchen project, and the projects' own pages report goals and activities, not outcomes. The model's reach rests on anchors from elsewhere: a Cambridge cafeteria trial where doubling vegetarian options raised vegetarian sales by 41–79% (Garnett et al. 2019), and Skanderborg's four-year municipal programme, which cut beef purchases by 61%. Both are more intensive than a one-day workshop. A recent meta-analysis finds that rigorous studies of meat-reduction interventions show small effects (Green, Smith & Mathur 2025).
- **What the replaced meals contained, and what replaced them.** If kitchens drop beef and keep chicken, few animals are spared; if they switched beef to chicken, or to egg-based dishes, the change could add suffering. DVF promotes legume and vegetable dishes, so we don't model either switch, but they are why the species-mix range goes down to 0.15 and the replacement offset up to 60%.
- **Whose money this is.** Almost all of the modelled work is paid for by public grants, which DVF wins in competition with other applicants. A private donor's gift doesn't directly fund it; it funds DVF's unrestricted work (policy, communication, the capacity to win and run grants). The model reports the cost-effectiveness of the programme per dollar spent, as the template does. It doesn't model what a marginal private dollar does, nor what Plantefonden's money would have done with another grantee.
- **Counterfactual.** Danish public kitchens are already shifting, helped by dietary guidelines that recommend less meat and by the very fund DVF campaigned for, and kitchens that sign up are self-selected.
- **The policy work.** If DVF's campaigning made Plantefonden happen, its impact runs through more than a billion kroner of grants to kitchens and producers. That would need a policy model (probability that DVF's work changed the outcome, and the value of what the fund pays for), which this model doesn't attempt.

## What isn't modelled

- **Policy and legal work**: the plant-based action plan, Plantefonden, the EU "Danish Plant-Based Diplomacy" project and EU Plant Food Summit, lawsuits for the right to vegan food in public institutions and against Danish Crown's greenwashing.
- **Retail and products**: benchmarking supermarkets, the Green Heart and V-label (409 and 190 products in 2024).
- **Health professionals and public information**: nutrition conferences, health guides, books to all 384 regional and national politicians, Plant Food Festival, the magazine, newsletter and social media.
- **Indirect effects of the kitchen work**: training that kitchen staff carry to other jobs, networks and norms in the foodservice sector, and DVF's role around the Green Food Craftsman course at Hotel- og Restaurantskolen.
- **Environmental and health benefits** of the diet changes, which matter to DVF but aren't in the SAD unit.

## How this model was checked

- **Mechanical audit** (`scripts/audit-model.ts`) before each round: no high findings; two medium findings remain by design (the result is an outlier against the other diet-change models, decomposed above; the joint range is very wide, which the summary says).
- **Round 1** (independent auditor): 1 high, 5 medium, 4 low. Changes: added a Danish species-mix scale to SADs per meal (high); corrected the grant share after the auditor found that "Strategiprojektet" is the school-canteen project (0.85 → 0.93); split persistence by branch (commercial outlets 1 year); narrowed the evidence adjustment to what the reach rows don't already cover (0.7 → 0.85); added the SVB decomposition and said in the summary and output label that the model covers only the grant-funded kitchen work; widened the low end of institutions reached (25 → 15); took a possible grant overhead allowance out of programme spending; cited the 40 million day-guests figure; marked the meta-analysis as a preprint. Net effect: 0.040 → 0.015 SADs per dollar.
- **Round 2** (new independent auditor, with round 1's findings): all round-1 findings resolved; 1 high, 1 medium, 7 low. Changes: added a replacement offset for eggs and dairy in plant-rich replacement dishes (high); added per-day suffering intensity to the species-mix scale (0.35 → 0.28, medium); noted that the meat-purchase source excludes canteens; cited Plantefonden's 2024 call for the overhead rules and raised the high end of the overhead share to 0.23; cited DVF's project overview directly for the school-canteen project (its grant is 0.79 million kroner, not the 2.08 million the round-1 auditor reported, which belongs to the next line, a Bispebjerg Hospital project); explained keeping the shared production response; noted that Green January's and the network's meals aren't counted. Net effect: 0.015 → 0.009 SADs per dollar.
- **Round 3** (new independent auditor): all round-2 findings resolved; 0 high, 0 medium, 5 low. Changes: corrected the SVB comparison table (SVB's future-cost factor and donor share were missing); cited Tomasik's per-day suffering rates (chicken 3, pork 2.5, beef 1), which give the same 0.28 scale; noted that the care-home recipes also cut chicken and mixed mince. Not changed: the replacement offset stays a judgment (the auditor found DVF and DTU's care-home recipes broadly consistent with it). The result is unchanged at 0.009 SADs per dollar.
