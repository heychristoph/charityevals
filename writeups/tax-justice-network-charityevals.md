### Summary

The Tax Justice Network researches tax havens and campaigns for international tax rules. Our model counts its work since 2003 against two outcomes: the transparency standards it first proposed, now in force worldwide, and the UN tax convention now being negotiated. It comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, because the outcome is public revenue for lower-income governments, a large sum valued at what government spending does there. It is a very uncertain figure: the network's share of the credit, the years a win counts and what the convention will recover are our judgments.

### What the charity does

The Tax Justice Network is a UK non-profit company limited by guarantee, not a registered charity, with a remote team of about 33 people in 14 countries. It spent GBP 2.09 million in 2025, most of it on staff. In 2003 it proposed three measures: automatic exchange of tax information between countries, registers of who really owns companies, and country-by-country reporting by multinationals. All three are now international standards. It publishes the Financial Secrecy Index, the Corporate Tax Haven Index and the State of Tax Justice reports, and works with tax authorities in Africa and Europe on their own data. In 2025 it worked mainly on the UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation, whose text is due for agreement in 2027. Its funding has never reached US$3 million a year, and it cut a quarter of its core staff at the end of 2025.

### How the impact happens

Tax havens let multinationals and wealthy people avoid tax. When tax authorities can see foreign accounts and who owns companies, they find hidden income and people declare more. Governments then have more to spend. In poorer countries that spending pays for health care and schools, and the people who paid the tax are mostly rich, so the money does much more good than it costs them.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** an average year of spending since 2003, from the published accounts of 2005 to 2025 (hosted bodies left out) in 2025 prices, with a judged GBP 60,000 a year for 2003-2004, before the company had accounts. That comes to 45% of 2025's GBP 2.09 million.
- **Transparency standards (about 54% of the result):**
  - tax authorities report EUR 4 billion a year of extra revenue from the transparency standards (2024, the OECD Global Forum), 70% of it in developing countries;
  - we judge 35% of the developing countries' share is in low and lower-middle income countries, 35% of that in India. That revenue is valued as GiveWell values a dollar of government spending: about 1.5 times cash in its African programme countries, a quarter of that in India. Revenue in upper-middle and high-income countries is valued as money spread over everyone's consumption, much less per dollar;
  - each dollar is net of what it costs the people who pay, judged to consume about four and a half times the UK average (a blend of multinationals' shareholders, at the shared 2.5 times, and offshore wealth's owners, at 20 times), with the shared extra cost of raising public money;
  - the win counts for the years a won reform counts on the site, less the shared yearly chance that such a reform is repealed;
  - the network gets 8% of the movement's credit in the years the standards were won, and civil society 35% of the campaigners' credit: the governments that pushed for the standards on their own account and the journalists who published leaked documents did the rest. Exchange of information on request, which predates the network's proposals, gets half that credit. That makes {{row:a_attribution_share_shared}};
  - we trust the revenue figure at 70%, and judge 60% of the identified revenue is collected;
  - side effects: tax people now pay because evasion is harder adds half again, and revenue from the other two standards adds 10%; banks' reporting costs (the standards' compliance costs, counted in full) take away 2%.
- **UN convention (about 46%):**
  - lower-income countries lose US$47 billion a year to cross-border tax abuse (the network's own 2023 estimate); all countries lose US$480 billion;
  - the chance it comes into force with substance is the site's shared 40% for a contested policy issue. If it does, its rules would recover 5% of the losses if applied in full (an upper anchor: the OECD expected its global minimum tax to raise about a third as much as the losses), starting in about five years, and 60% is applied;
  - civil society gets 50% of the campaigners' credit (the African Group of governments leads), and the network 12% of that: 6%. Two thirds of the network's work on it is done; only that share is counted against past spending;
  - we trust the loss estimate at 60%;
  - side effect: the shared 5% for counter-measures such work provokes.
- **Both branches** are spread over the 23 years of work since 2003, and both are discounted by the shared missing-data slider, as no study measures the network's part.
- **The next dollar** is valued at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of the average: money is short, but the main standards are already won and new money goes to the harder convention work.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts, the missing-data slider, the next dollar's value and the share of losses the convention recovers move the result most. The network's and civil society's shares of the credit come next.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Credit:** no one has measured how much the network's ideas and advocacy mattered against the G20, the OECD, FATCA and the leaks. Our {{row:a_attribution_share_shared}} for the standards could be a third or twice that.
- **Where the revenue is:** no split of the Global Forum's revenue by income group or country is published. Lower-income revenue counts about 17 times more per dollar than upper-middle income revenue, so this split matters.
- **The convention:** its text, its protocols and who will ratify them are all unknown. The losses it addresses are the network's own estimate.

### How this model was checked

Audit rounds are listed in audits/tax-justice-network-charityevals/. Round 1 found one high and eight medium issues. The convention's chance now reads the shared figure for contested issues. India's revenue is valued at GiveWell's figure for India's government. Only the share of convention work already done is counted. Won standards can now be repealed. Backlash and the cost of raising public money read shared figures. The average year's cost is built from each year's accounts. The network's credit is split by period and by kind of information exchange. Together these took the result from 2.0 to 1.1. Round 2 passed; its fixes (2015 and 2017 spending, the accounts back to 2005, Latin America's 2024 revenue in the income split, an anchor for what the convention recovers) took it to 1.3, checked in a verification round.
