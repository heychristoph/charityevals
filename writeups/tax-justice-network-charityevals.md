### Summary

The Tax Justice Network researches tax havens and campaigns for international tax rules. Our model counts its work since 2003 against two outcomes: the transparency standards it first proposed, now in force worldwide, and the UN tax convention now being negotiated. It comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="tax-justice-network-charityevals">2</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, more than ten times the site's global-health median, because the outcome is public revenue for lower-income governments, a large sum valued at what government spending does there. It is a very uncertain figure: the network's share of the credit, the years a win counts and what the convention will recover are our judgments.

### What the charity does

The Tax Justice Network is a UK non-profit company limited by guarantee, not a registered charity, with a remote team of about 33 people in 14 countries. It spent GBP 2.09 million in 2025, most of it on staff. In 2003 it proposed three measures: automatic exchange of tax information between countries, registers of who really owns companies, and country-by-country reporting by multinationals. All three are now international standards. It publishes the Financial Secrecy Index, the Corporate Tax Haven Index and the State of Tax Justice reports, and works with tax authorities in Africa and Europe on their own data. In 2025 it worked mainly on the UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation, whose text is due for agreement in 2027. Its funding has never reached US$3 million a year, and it cut a quarter of its core staff at the end of 2025.

### How the impact happens

Tax havens let multinationals and wealthy people avoid tax. When tax authorities can see foreign accounts and who owns companies, they find hidden income and people declare more. Governments then have more to spend. In poorer countries that spending pays for health care and schools, and the people who paid the tax are mostly rich, so the money does much more good than it costs them.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** an average year of spending since 2003. The 2025 audited accounts give GBP 2.09 million. Earlier years were smaller (GBP 0.57 million in 2014), so an average year is judged at 55% of 2025's in real terms.
- **Transparency standards (about 55% of the result):**
  - tax authorities report EUR 4 billion a year of extra revenue from the transparency standards (2024, the OECD Global Forum), 70% of it in developing countries;
  - we judge 30% of the developing countries' share is in low and lower-middle income countries. That revenue is valued as GiveWell values a dollar of government spending, about 1.5 times cash. Revenue in upper-middle and high-income countries is valued as money spread over everyone's consumption, much less per dollar;
  - each dollar is net of what it costs the people who pay, judged to consume five times the UK average;
  - the win counts for the years a won reform counts on the site;
  - the network gets 12% of the movement's credit, and civil society 15% of all credit: governments, the US FATCA law and leaked documents did most. That makes 1.8%;
  - we trust the revenue figure at 70%, and judge 60% of the identified revenue is collected;
  - side effects: tax people now pay because evasion is harder adds half again, revenue from the other two standards adds 10%, and banks' reporting costs take away 2%.
- **UN convention (about 45%):**
  - lower-income countries lose US$47 billion a year to cross-border tax abuse (the network's own 2023 estimate); all countries lose US$480 billion;
  - we judge the convention has a 40% chance of coming into force with substance. If it does, it recovers 5% of the losses, starting in about five years;
  - civil society gets 25% of the credit (the African Group of governments leads), and the network 12% of that: 3%;
  - we trust the loss estimate at 60%, and judge 60% of the new rules is applied;
  - side effect: US opposition takes away 5%.
- **Both branches** are spread over the 23 years of work since 2003, and both are discounted by the shared missing-data slider, as no study measures the network's part.
- **The next dollar** is valued at 54% of the average: money is short, but the main standards are already won and new money goes to the harder convention work.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts, the missing-data slider, the network's share of the movement's resources and the next dollar's value move the result most. The share of losses the convention recovers and civil society's share of the credit for the standards come next.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Credit:** no one has measured how much the network's ideas and advocacy mattered against the G20, the OECD, FATCA and the leaks. Our 1.8% could be a quarter or twice that.
- **Where the revenue is:** no split of the Global Forum's revenue by income group is published. Lower-income revenue counts about 25 times more per dollar than upper-middle income revenue, so this split matters.
- **The convention:** its text, its protocols and who will ratify them are all unknown. The losses it addresses are the network's own estimate.

### What isn't modelled

- Effects of the indices and the State of Tax Justice reports on public debate, except through the two branches.
- Revenue from beneficial-ownership registers used against corruption and money laundering, beyond the 10% side effect.
- Any loss of investment in the countries because taxes are higher.
