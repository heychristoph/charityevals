### Summary

FairVote researches and campaigns for new ways of electing American governments: ranked-choice voting and proportional representation. Our model of its 2024 work comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="fairvote-charityevals">0.02</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, about a third of the site's global-health median. It is a very uncertain figure: whether a new voting method changes how well people are governed, and by how much, is our judgment.

### What the charity does

FairVote was founded in 1992. Its 501(c)(3) spent $6.4 million in 2024, $4.3 million of it on its programme. It publishes research, such as its forecasts of which US House seats are safe for one party. It supports ballot campaigns with research, education and media work: four state and four city campaigns in 2024. It monitors elections that use ranked ballots, twelve cities' in 2024. Most of its work is for ranked-choice voting for single offices. About a fifth is for proportional representation: the Fair Representation Act for the US House, and cities electing councils with several members per district. Its sister 501(c)(4), FairVote Action, lobbies and files separately.

### How the impact happens

Ranked-choice voting now reaches about 17 million Americans (14 million voters), up from ten cities in 2016. New York City, Maine and Alaska are the largest. Studies find campaigns under it are less negative and more women and people of colour stand and win. Whether it changes what governments decide is not yet known. FairVote is one of many groups behind these wins, and the ballot campaigns are paid for mostly by others.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** the 2024 Form 990 (audited statements, which the return says exist, aren't public). Management and fundraising are spread over the programme.
- **The past record:** a dollar to FairVote is counted as doing what the average dollar behind each movement did.
- **Ranked-choice branch (about four fifths of spending):**
  - about 1.4 million more people a year under ranked-choice voting in 2016-2025, net of repeals;
  - they use it for only some of their governments' elections (New York City its primaries, cities their own offices): counted at 44% of a full switch;
  - the gain: a judged 2% of the income becoming a democracy brings (about a fifth more income per person in the long run), counted at 0.5 for doubt that the effect is real;
  - the movement spent about $19 million a year, ballot campaigns included.
- **Proportional-representation branch (about a fifth):** about 103,000 people a year in 2016-2025, most of them in Portland, Oregon, which adopted it in 2022. A city's own elections count as half of a full switch. The gain is a judged 5% of democracy's, at 0.6 for doubt. The movement spent about $3.4 million a year. The Protect Democracy model shares these figures.
- **Years:** the gain builds over about 25 years. A win counts as brought forward by the site's five years, with a 3% yearly chance of repeal.
- **Both branches** are discounted by the missing-data slider. Ranked-choice voting gives about four fifths of the result.
- **Side effects:** the good of fairer elections beyond income (+20%) and the state bans the wins provoke (−10%), shared with the Protect Democracy model.
- **Next dollar:** 42% of the average. The easier wins came first, every statewide ranked-choice measure in 2024 failed except Alaska's defence of its own, and nineteen states now ban it.

### What drives the result

1. The years a won reform counts (1 to 20).
2. The missing-data slider.
3. Ranked-choice voting's share of democracy's income gain (0.3% to 6%).
4. Democracy's long-run income gain (7% to 35%).
5. The next dollar's share of the average (20% to 79%).
6. The ranked-choice movement's yearly spending ($9 million to $39 million).
7. Doubt that ranked-choice voting changes how places are governed (0.25 to 0.85).
8. The yearly chance a won reform is repealed (1% to 8%).

### Biggest uncertainties

- No study measures how much a voting method changes how well a place is governed. The shares of democracy's gain are judgments.
- The movement's spending is a judgment from one year's count of ballot money.
- Proportional representation's record is a handful of cities, Portland, Oregon most of it.

### What isn't modelled

- FairVote Action's spending and lobbying.
- Any value per voter reached or report published: these count only through the reforms won.
- Effects on turnout: studies find them mixed.

### How this model was checked

Built from the template and audited by independent reviewers (rounds in audits/fairvote-charityevals/). Round 1 moved proportional representation from a forecast to the 2016-2025 record, counted against the movement's average spending over the same years, and shared its figures and side effects with the Protect Democracy model. Round 2 counted a city's own elections as half of a full switch and built the share of elections ranked ballots cover from the places themselves, lowering the result by about 30%; a last round checked those changes.
