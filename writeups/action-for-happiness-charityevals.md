### Summary

Action for Happiness is a small UK charity that teaches people what research says makes for a happier life, through volunteer-led courses and local groups and a digital programme of daily actions. Our model of its 2025 spending comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**.

Its course is one of the few wellbeing courses for the general public tested in a randomised trial, which found life satisfaction about a point higher (on a 0-10 scale) at the end. But today's course is a shorter successor that hasn't been trialled, and most of the result comes from the digital programme, where nobody has measured the effect: it rests on the research on self-help exercises and on our judgment of how many of the 190,000 people the charity counts actually follow a programme. That share, how long the effects last, and how much the next dollar adds to an audience built over fifteen years are the biggest uncertainties.

### What the charity does

In the year to 31 December 2025 Action for Happiness spent £551,274: £539,274 on its charitable activities, reported as one activity, and £12,000 on fundraising. Two employees and four subcontractors (six full-time equivalents) run it, with volunteers delivering the courses and groups. In the year it:

- ran 160 Happiness Habits courses: six weekly two-hour sessions, online or in person, for about 12 people, led by pairs of trained volunteers (it recruited 1,065 new volunteers);
- supported 115 volunteer-led local groups meeting monthly;
- ran a digital programme: monthly calendars with daily action ideas, an app (270,000 registered users), the 10 Days of Happiness online programme (25,000 new participants), 13 live talks (35,000 participants), social media and resources for schools and organisations.

It says it supported over 190,000 people to take at least one measurable wellbeing action.

### How the impact happens

Participants learn and practise habits linked to wellbeing (gratitude, mindfulness, kindness, connecting with others, setting goals) and talk them over in a group. The trial of the earlier course found higher life satisfaction and happiness, lower depression and anxiety scores, and more compassion and social trust. The digital programme prompts the same kinds of actions at a much lighter dose.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's template for wellbeing, with three branches:

- **Cost**: all charitable spending, about $726,000 with fundraising. The accounts' direct-cost lines split it: course costs, community development (volunteer training, groups and hubs) and a judged 30% of staff time go to the courses and groups, about half of the total; the rest to the digital programme, talks and campaigns.
- **Courses**: 160 courses of about 12 participants (the charity's own figure), some 1,900 people. The effect blends the randomised trial of the predecessor course, Exploring What Matters (146 London participants; life satisfaction up 0.63 standard deviations against a wait-list), with the meta-analysis's group-delivered positive psychology courses (0.38), weighting the trial by its precision and a little less because today's course is shorter and mostly online: about 0.53 standard deviations, fading over about a year. The volunteers' time (leading, hosting and training), valued at the UK volunteer wage, lowers the charity's share of the credit to about 43%.
- **Local groups**: about {{row:group_people}} regular attendees (judged), each year of monthly meetings taken at 30% of a course's effect, with the courses' adjustments.
- **Digital programme**: the remaining 187,000 people the charity counts. The effect is the meta-analysis's self-help exercises (0.33 standard deviations), fading over about a year, for the 15% judged to use something like a programme (10 Days of Happiness and regular app use); a single action from a calendar or a post is not counted. This reach comes from an audience built since 2011 (780,000 members, 76,000 of them new in 2025) at mostly fixed cost, so a year's spending over it is a past average: the branch counts at the charity's own figure for how much the next dollar does against that average, {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} (its room for more funding, what the next dollar buys and how it is run).
- **Everyone**: household members gain the site's shared share of the participant's effect, and a few suicides are averted among the participants with depression, valued as every death on the site is, by the ages of suicides in England and Wales.
- **Adjustments**: the evidence (the courses 0.65, the digital programme 0.5: unblinded, self-reported trials of self-selected people, mostly students in the self-help trials, and a literature whose wellbeing effect shrinks by about a quarter when small-study bias is corrected), what people would have got elsewhere (95% and 90%: only free apps that have spread since the trials), the charity's share of the credit, delivery (fidelity of the volunteer-led courses), and the missing-data discount on every branch, since neither today's course, the groups nor the digital programme has been evaluated.
- **Side effects**: the leaders' own wellbeing and kindness towards others (+8% of the courses' value), and the digital programme's wider reach against screen time (+5%).

### What drives the result

- **The shared discount for missing data**, which applies to every branch.
- **How long the digital effect lasts** (about five months to two years), **how many of the 190,000 follow a programme** (5% to 30%) and **the next-dollar figure** (18% to 94%): each moves the result three- to fourfold.
- **How far to trust the self-help literature** and its effect size.
- **The exchange rate between WELLBYs and healthy years**, a shared slider.

### Biggest uncertainties

- The charity's count of 190,000 is of people who took at least one action; how many took a programme's worth is our judgment, and nobody has measured what its calendars, app or emails do.
- The one trial is of the eight-week in-person course of 2016-2017; today's six-week, mostly online course keeps its structure but is untested. The trial was small, unblinded and self-reported, and one of its authors co-founded the charity.
- No study follows participants beyond two months after the course, and the wider literature pools follow-ups only to six months (a few trials ran to a year).
- Course size and the local groups' attendance are not reported.

### What isn't modelled

The volunteers' and partners' own satisfaction beyond the share counted, and any effect on policy from the charity's campaigns (the International Day of Happiness, the World Happiness Report launch) beyond a small share. The members (780,000) and social media reach (36 million) count only through the people who take an action.

### How this model was checked

The mechanical audit (lint, invariants, shared figures) passes. Round 1 of the independent audit found 1 high, 2 medium and 11 low issues: the effect sizes' ranges are now read as the sources' 95% normal intervals; the digital counterfactual counts only free apps that have spread since the trials (80% to 90%); the ages of suicides averted come from the ONS's 2025 counts, now a shared fact; and the rest (the prior's spread, volunteer training time, course size, attendance not discounted, the suicide base rate, beneficiary countries, wording) were fixed. Round 2 found those in place and 1 high, 2 medium and 2 low issues: the digital programme's reach is an audience built over years, so its result now reads the charity's next-dollar figure; the digital effect fades over about a year, as the follow-up evidence implies, not nine months; both evidence adjustments are anchored on the corrected subjective-wellbeing effect rather than all wellbeing outcomes; and the prior's standard error is worked out live. Round 3 passed with 1 medium and 3 low issues, all fixed: the digital evidence adjustment now also counts that most self-help trials recruited students, whose effects run larger than those of people recruited online (0.6 to 0.5); a reason for the next-dollar figure that the evidence adjustments already counted was dropped; the beneficiary countries follow the branches' new shares; and a comparison left the cost explainer. A scoped verification checked those changes. The result moved from 0.18 to 0.20, 0.14 and then {{result}} healthy years per 1,000 dollars.
