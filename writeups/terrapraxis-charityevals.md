### Summary

TerraPraxis works to re-power the world's coal plants with advanced nuclear reactors, so the sites, grid connections and jobs outlive coal. It does this through designs, software and convening rather than by building anything itself. Our model counts what its work of 2022-2024 adds to the deployment of advanced nuclear power, against what it spent in those years. It comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="terrapraxis-charityevals">0.015</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. About three tenths of that is the tonnes of CO₂ the reactors avoid. The rest is money: mostly what reactors on coal sites save in construction, for power customers in China and other middle-income countries, where a dollar matters far more to the people paying. The result is mostly uncertainty. No coal plant has been re-powered yet, every step from TerraPraxis's work to a working reactor is a judgment, and the sign turns on what nuclear power will cost against what it replaces.

### What the charity does

TerraPraxis Inc, a US non-profit founded in 2021, spent $2.01 million in 2024, $1.62 million of it on its one reported programme (Form 990). It had eight staff and paid $0.82 million to consultants. Its UK sister company, TerraPraxis CIC, had one or two staff and £19,781 in the bank at the end of 2025. It is not a registered charity and files no income statement, so we add our estimate of its costs, about $60,000 a year. Over 2022-2024 TerraPraxis spent $6.3 million in all. By the end of 2024 its net assets were down to $58,560.

The programme has these parts:

- **REPOWER (coal repowering).** Standard designs for replacing a coal plant's boilers with mass-made reactors ("heat boxes"), developed with the architects Bryden Wood. Site-assessment software built with Microsoft from 2022. The REPOWER consortium of utilities, vendors, financiers and governments, with yearly retreats.
- **The Ten-Terawatt Initiative.** Launched in 2024; its white paper was presented at Davos in January 2025. It sets out what would let clean firm power grow fast enough to replace fossil fuels, and it includes AI tools for nuclear licensing built with Microsoft.
- **Smaller work.** Papers on synthetic fuels, industrial heat and nuclear-powered shipping, and pages on advanced geothermal and energy modelling.

### How the impact happens

TerraPraxis doesn't campaign for laws. It tries to make reactors cheaper and faster to deploy. It produces the designs, tools and working groups that utilities and governments would otherwise have to build for themselves, and it argues that reactors should replace coal on the sites coal leaves. US national laboratories found that four in five US coal sites could host advanced reactors, and that reusing a site saves 15% to 35% of construction costs. If that work brings reactors forward, the tonnes come when they replace coal or gas power, mostly in the late 2030s and 2040s.

### How the model works

The model starts from a yardstick rather than a forecast: the COP28 pledge to triple 2020's 392 GW of nuclear capacity by 2050, of which we judge 35% gets built, read at about 2040, the middle of the years counted. It splits that build into two lines.

- **Coal repowering.** A tenth of the new reactors stand on coal sites, about 27 GW. We count 40% of them as extra reactors rather than reactors moved from other sites. Each extra reactor replaces power with half of coal's CO₂.
- **Advanced nuclear elsewhere.** The rest of the build. The grids these reactors join have 35% of coal's CO₂ per MWh.

Each reactor runs at the world fleet's 83% capacity factor. The tonnes include the methane that leaks from the mines and gas systems supplying the power replaced. Both lines then take these steps:

- All groups' non-commercial enabling work in the period gets credit for 1.5% of the deployment.
- TerraPraxis's share of that work is 8% on coal sites and 0.6% elsewhere.
- The tonnes count for the shared number of years a result counts, discounted after the shared 15-year wait for a new technology.
- The shared evidence and missing-data steps apply.

Then come the money effects, valued as money gained or lost by those who pay for power:

- Reactors moved to coal sites save 25% of their construction cost (DOE). The saving uses each region's reactor cost from the IEA and NEA.
- The extra reactors' power costs more or less than the power it replaces, without any carbon price. The shared figures come from the IEA and NEA's levelised costs, at the site's 4% discount rate.
  - **Against fossil power.** New nuclear costs about $2 more per MWh in China, $6 less in India, $10 more in the US and $14 less in Korea and France.
  - **Against wind and solar.** These are cheaper to build. After a shared $15 per MWh for their system costs, nuclear costs about $10 more in India and $4 more in the US, and $5 less in China. In Korea and France it costs much less.
  - **Each line's mix.** Each line splits the power it replaces into fossil and clean shares to match its own tonnes.
  - **Coal sites.** The coal-site reactors' power is cheaper by the site's construction saving.

Where the reactors are built decides who pays: 45% in China, 10% in India, 15% in the US, 10% in other middle-income countries (valued like China) and 20% elsewhere (our judgment; half of what is under construction today is in China). A dollar counts in proportion to 1 ÷ the payers' consumption on the site's survey scale: about $4,200 a year in China, $630 in India and $32,700 in the US. A small share is added for the remaining work (5%), and the side effects come in as small shares: jobs kept in coal towns, nuclear risk. The total is divided by a typical year's spending in 2022-2024. The next dollar is valued at 57% of that average, because money is short.

### What drives the result

The two lines give about equal parts of the tonnes. The money comes to about two and a half times the tonnes' value. Most of it is the construction saved on coal-site reactors, and part of that is offset by the advanced nuclear line's power costing more than India's wind and solar. The biggest inputs are these:

- **The shared uncertainty on nuclear's projected costs.** At $15 a MWh more than projected, the result turns negative.
- **The discount rate.**
- **The system cost of wind and solar.**

Next come the shared years a result counts and the credit shares.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether coal repowering happens at all.** Utilities have announced studies. One reactor is going up beside a Wyoming coal plant, but no conversion is running.
- **How much design and convening work moves deployment.** Reactors turn mostly on cost, finance, demand and policy. Nobody has measured how much enabling work changes that.
- **TerraPraxis's share.** It is one of the few organisations focused on coal repowering, but national laboratories, the IAEA and industry bodies do much of the same work with far larger budgets.
- **What nuclear power will cost in the 2040s against what it replaces.** The IEA/NEA figures are projections for plants commissioned around 2025. They put nuclear power slightly below coal in India and slightly above it in China at a 4% discount rate.
- **Its finances.** Revenue fell two years running and reserves are almost gone. That is part of the next-dollar figure.
