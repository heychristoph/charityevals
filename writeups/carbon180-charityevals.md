### Summary

Carbon180 is a US non-profit that writes and campaigns for federal policy to scale up carbon removal: pulling CO₂ out of the air and storing it. Our model counts the federal programmes it worked on that were won or defended between 2020 and 2025, against what it spent over those years. It comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**, a net harm: the tonnes come with public money (tax credits of $85 and $180 a tonne, grants, farm and forest money) that costs US taxpayers more than the tonnes are worth on the site's scale. Direct air capture is still small and costly, each win is shared with many groups, companies and what Congress would have done anyway, and the next dollar mostly defends wins already made.

### What the charity does

Carbon180 spent $10.06 million in 2025, $8.15 million of it on programmes (Form 990, Part III):

- **Policy, analyses and regranting: $6.20 million (76%).** Federal policy on direct air capture, soil and forest carbon and, since 2025, ocean carbon removal; appropriations requests; and $0.9 million of grants to community groups so they can take part in decisions on carbon-removal projects near them.
- **Education and communication: $1.02 million (13%).** Newsletters, briefings for reporters, an online library on carbon removal.
- **Science and innovation: $0.74 million (9%).** Research that grounds the policy work, including standards for what counts as good carbon removal.
- **Lobbying: $0.19 million (2%).**

All four serve one campaign, so the model counts them together.

### How the impact happens

Carbon180 helps draft bills, briefs members of Congress and agencies, and builds coalitions behind them. A won programme pays for carbon removal or storage that would otherwise not happen. The model counts five branches:

- **The first megaton direct air capture plants.** The 2021 infrastructure law put $3.5 billion into Regional Direct Air Capture Hubs, and the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act raised the 45Q tax credit for direct air capture to $180 a tonne. Three plants rest on them: STRATOS in Texas (500,000 tonnes a year at design capacity) and the two selected hubs, Project Cypress in Louisiana and the South Texas hub (about 1 million tonnes a year each, DOE). Carbon180's annual reports say it helped structure the hubs programme and led its defence in 2025.
- **A faster technology.** These programmes, the Department of Energy's carbon-removal research (about $116.5 million a year, held in the 2026 budget) and a $35 million purchase prize can bring down costs and speed the technology worldwide. The yardstick is the IEA's net-zero pathway: about 70 million tonnes a year of direct air capture in 2030 and 600 million in 2050, read in the years the speed-up counts (around 2040).
- **The larger 45Q credit at other plants.** The same credit pays for capture at power plants and factories: the site's shared figure is 60 million tonnes a year once built. Carbon180 doesn't campaign for that use, so its share here is small.
- **Farm conservation money.** The 2022 law put $19.5 billion into USDA's conservation programmes; in 2024 they paid for climate-focused practices on more than 11 million acres. Carbon180 says its advocacy shaped this. We count the soil carbon with the site's shared figure for cover crops, scaled for the mix of practices.
- **Reforestation money.** The 2021 law lifted the $30 million cap on the Forest Service's reforestation fund, about five times more money a year; 360,000 acres were replanted in its first two years. Carbon180 lists this among the provisions it helped structure.

### How the model works

For each branch: tonnes a year × the share of a won policy that is advocacy's doing (a shared figure, 30%) × the years a won reform counts (the shared 5 years, discounted, after the wait until the tonnes come) × the share that wouldn't have happened anyway × Carbon180's share of all the groups' work behind it × the share actually delivered. Direct air capture uses some energy, so we count 85% of the CO₂ it captures as removed. For the technology we also count how much of the IEA pathway gets built (15%) and how much of that the US programmes cause (8%).

Carbon180 is credited with 10% of the advocacy behind the plants, 8% behind the technology, 2% behind the farm and forest money and 0.3% behind the credit at other plants, from the budgets of the other groups behind each win. The hub awards nearly ended on DOE's 2025 termination list and survive with less money, so we count 45% of the plants' design tonnes and half the programmes as delivered. The 2025 budget law took the climate focus off the farm money, so half of it counts.

Each branch has its own evidence step: the first plants count 70% because such plants have run below design, soil carbon 70% because farms keep practices less than trials do, replanted forests 50% for seedlings that die and stands that burn. The branches are added up and spread over the six years. Then come the shared missing-data step, other work not modelled (+5%: ocean research, standards, the community grants) and two side effects: emission cuts put off because removal is promised (−5%), and extra oil from captured CO₂ used in old wells (−8% of the 45Q branch). The total is divided by a typical year's spending in 2020-2025. The next dollar is valued at 27% of that average. Carbon180 spent three times its income in 2025 while holding about 20 months of spending in reserve, and its next dollar goes mostly to defending these wins and to newer fields.

Removed or stored CO₂ counts for the heat deaths it prevents. It cuts no smoke from burning fuel, so it saves no air-pollution deaths. The programmes are public money, not rules, so no compliance cost is counted. The public money is netted against what it costs taxpayers (method, section 2.23): the credits per tonne, the hub grants, the farm and forest money and the public purchases of the removals the faster build brings.

### What drives the result

The public money's cost to taxpayers outweighs the tonnes, so the sign rests on the credits per tonne and the value of a tonne. 
The 45Q credit at other plants gives about half the modelled tonnes, despite Carbon180's small share, because that credit is much bigger than the direct air capture programmes. The technology gives about a fifth, the first plants a sixth, the reforestation money a tenth and the farm money a few per cent. The shared value judgments move the result most: the discount rate, when heat deaths come, and the years a won reform counts.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How far direct air capture will scale.** Plants have run below their design, costs are several hundred dollars a tonne and buyers are few. We put the share of the IEA pathway that gets built at 3% to 50%.
- **How much of a win is advocacy's doing.** The shared 30% runs from 10% to 60%, and Carbon180's share of each win is our judgment.
- **Whether the hubs get built.** DOE has obligated only $50 million to each hub so far.
- **Carbon180's share of the 45Q credit.** 0.1% to 1.5%, and that branch carries about half the result.

### What isn't modelled

Wins before 2020 aren't counted, such as the first $60 million of carbon-removal appropriations in 2019, and neither is their cost. The ocean work and the 2025 forestry appropriations are covered only by the 5% for other work: they were mostly funding held steady, and the soil bills haven't passed.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits are in audits/carbon180-charityevals/. Round 1 found the slow build-out of the 45Q tonnes counted three times (the shared figure already allows for it), so each branch now has its own evidence step. It also asked for a branch for the farm conservation money in place of a share, and for the technology's pathway to be read in the years counted rather than at 2050. Round 2 asked for the reforestation money as a branch, the attribution shares anchored on the other groups' budgets, and one shared soil-carbon figure for cover crops, which The Nature Conservancy's model now reads too.
