### Summary

St. Jude Children's Research Hospital in Memphis treats children with cancer and other catastrophic diseases free to their families, runs one of the world's largest childhood cancer research programmes, and supports children's cancer care in other countries through St. Jude Global. It also trains clinicians and scientists and runs community health programmes in Memphis. Its fundraising arm, ALSAC, raises almost all of the money.

Research comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="st-jude-charityevals-research">0.005</span> years of healthy life per $1,000** you give, about a thousandth of the site's global-health median. Our model of patient care with St. Jude Global, training and community programmes (about 0.004) is held back until its last fix has been reviewed; it is described below. The reason isn't that St. Jude does its work badly. Most of the patient-care result is St. Jude Global, whose partner hospitals and their governments carry most of the cost of treating the children it helps, and whose gains rest on before-and-after results at a few flagship sites. Its US patients would otherwise be treated at other US children's hospitals with nearly as good results, at US prices. Its research pays off only after many years, and only as one funder among many. St. Jude publishes no controlled measure of any programme's results, so every part of both models counts at the site's shared discount for missing data.

### What the charity does

All figures are for the fiscal year to June 2025, from the audited combined accounts of St. Jude and ALSAC. St. Jude spent $2.83 billion in all:

- **Patient care ($727 million):** about 1,000 new patients a year, and more than 9,300 patients seen. Three in five new patients have cancer, most often brain tumours, leukaemia or lymphoma. The rest have blood disorders, sickle cell disease, HIV and other conditions. Insurers paid $141 million of the cost. Families pay nothing, and St. Jude covers their travel, housing and food.
- **Research ($883 million):** basic, translational and clinical research. Grants paid $162 million of it. About seven-tenths is cancer research; the rest is mostly sickle cell disease and other blood disorders, infectious diseases such as influenza, and basic science.
- **Education, training and community services ($392 million):** two different things share this line.
  - The hospital's part ($146 million) is mostly St. Jude Global. The rest, about $41 million, is clinical training (six haematology-oncology fellows a year and other fellowships), the graduate school (about a dozen PhD students a year) and community programmes in Memphis (HPV vaccination, HIV education, school health lessons).
  - ALSAC's part ($246 million) is the awareness content of its direct mail and television, which the accounts partly treat as appeal costs. We count it as fundraising.
- **Fundraising and administration ($832 million).** St. Jude also holds about $10.6 billion in cash and investments, about 3.7 years of spending.

### How the impact happens

- **Patient care.** A child treated at St. Jude has a somewhat better chance of surviving than at another US centre. In the one like-for-like published comparison, for acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, St. Jude's trial reached 93.5% five-year survival. The Children's Oncology Group's trials reached 90.4%. Families are also spared the out-of-pocket costs of treatment.
- **St. Jude Global.** Partner hospitals in middle-income countries get training, treatment protocols, consultations and, since 2025, medicines through the Global Platform for Access to Childhood Cancer Medicines, run with WHO. Survival there often starts at about half, so the same improvement saves more children. At Recife, Brazil, event-free survival from leukaemia nearly doubled within a decade of twinning with St. Jude.
- **Training.** Doctors trained at St. Jude go on to treat patients for decades. Studies of where surgeons trained find their patients have somewhat fewer complications if the programme was a strong one, but almost no difference in deaths.
- **Graduate school.** Better training makes scientists somewhat more productive: NIH postdoctoral fellowships raised publications by about a fifth.
- **Community programmes.** Training clinicians to recommend the HPV vaccine raised vaccination by about five points in US trials. Vaccination in adolescence prevents about four in five cervical cancers.
- **Research.** New protocols and drugs raise survival for one childhood cancer after another, and for sickle cell disease and infections. The gains come years after the research is done, wherever the results are taken up.

### How the model works

St. Jude has two models, weighted by what each part spends from St. Jude's own funds. The patient-care model is held back for now (its last audit's fix is unreviewed), so the site shows the research model alone.

**Patient care, St. Jude Global, training and community programmes** (direct health) has five branches:

- **US patients:** new patients a year × deaths averted per patient. Under standard care about 15% of children with cancer die within five years, and St. Jude cuts that by about a quarter.
  - Deaths are valued at the site's one value of a death, by age.
  - Late effects spared are counted in DALYs, at the GBD disability weights for hearing loss, heart failure and neurocognitive effects.
  - The costs families are spared are valued like cash.
- **Partner hospitals:** children treated (at supported units and through the Global Platform's medicines) × baseline deaths × the cut the partnership brings. The model then takes off the improvement that would have come anyway, and credits St. Jude only with its share of all the resources behind the care.
- **Clinical training:** trainees a year × patients each treats a year × their deaths × the small cut better training brings, over a career.
- **Graduate school:** graduates a year × the research each directs over a career × the extra output from better training × the health return of a research dollar, after the long wait for research to reach practice.
- **Community programmes:** adolescents whose clinicians the HPV programme reaches × the rise in vaccination × the lifetime risk of dying of an HPV cancer × the vaccine's effect, decades later. The other community activities count at a share of the HPV work's value per dollar.

Insurers', governments', hospitals' and other partners' money lowers St. Jude's share of the credit, compared at direct cost on both sides. Partner hospitals' costs are put in 2024 prices. Overhead is spread over the spending St. Jude pays for itself. Every wait is discounted at the shared rate, including the 3.7 years a donation spends in the reserves, where it earns a real return.

**Research** (medical research) has two branches. The cancer branch starts from the world's childhood cancer burden: 11.7 million DALYs a year (GBD 2023), nearly all of it from 144,000 deaths. The non-cancer branch starts from sickle cell disease (376,000 deaths a year, counting the infections and other conditions it makes deadly, and about 0.6 million years lived with illness) and influenza in children under five (2.5 million DALYs, 27,600 deaths). Each multiplies four things:

1. the share of that burden a year's advance averts;
2. the chance a year of the field's research yields one;
3. the years it brings the advance forward;
4. the wait until the advance is in practice, discounted.

St. Jude gets its share of each field's funding: about a fifth of childhood cancer research, about 7% of sickle cell and influenza research. The rest of the non-cancer research, mostly basic science, counts at half the targeted research's value per dollar. The deaths inside each burden are taken out of the DALYs and valued by age at the site's one value of a death, as in every other model; only the illness stays in DALYs. The cost per outcome comes from the field's past record, so the result is scaled by the shared "next dollar" slider.

### What drives the result

- **St. Jude Global's effect at partner hospitals:** the cut in deaths, the children reached, the evidence discount and the partners' own spending. St. Jude Global is 14% of the patient-care model's cost but brings nearly nine-tenths of its result. Partner hospitals and their governments carry most of the cost of treating these children, so St. Jude's share of the credit is about a fifth.
- **The discount for missing data:** every branch of both models takes it, so the shared slider moves both results in proportion.
- **Your values:** the value of a healthy year against a doubled income, and the value of a life. US patient care is almost all deaths averted.
- **Research:** the chance a year's research yields an advance and how much of the burden it averts. Both are judgments, anchored on published returns to medical research. Two check rows bound them: the share of the burden a year's advances remove (0.5%) against the observed fall in childhood cancer deaths, and the field's result per dollar against the UK's roughly 10% a year health return on cancer research. They are sanity bounds, not validations: the UK study counts different gains in a different way. The shared "next dollar" slider moves research the most.

### Biggest uncertainties

- How much better St. Jude's survival is than other US centres' once you allow for which patients it takes. The comparison is between trials; no children were randomised between centres.
- How many children St. Jude Global's support reaches, and how much it improves their survival. There is no controlled evaluation, and St. Jude publishes no count.
- The training, graduate-school and community branches rest on research about other programmes. They are small: together about 6% of the patient-care model's cost and 5% of its result.
- The research chain is almost entirely judgment. Its plausible range spans about two-hundredfold, and the patient-care model's about a hundredfold.

### What isn't modelled

- ALSAC's awareness messaging, counted as fundraising rather than as a programme.
- Lost parental earnings during treatment, which St. Jude doesn't replace.
- The knowledge St. Jude's patient care produces through its trials, which is in the research model rather than counted twice.
- Influenza research's benefit to adults, and herd protection from HPV vaccination.

### How this model was checked

Mechanical checks run on both models (scripts/audit-model.ts, scripts/normalization-audit.ts), then independent audits, each by a fresh reviewer with the audit-cea skill.

**First build (2026-11-03).**

- **Round 1** found 3 high, 4 medium and 8 low issues. All were fixed:
  - the write-up is filed under the charity's main model;
  - partner hospitals' costs follow the review's middle-income figures ($10,000 a child, was $5,000);
  - other partners' contributions moved from the counterfactual into St. Jude's share of resources, so they are no longer counted twice;
  - the twinning evidence cites the JAMA paper, with St. Jude's own account of its twinnings as a reference class;
  - overhead goes on own-funded spending only;
  - ages come from the patients' age table;
  - the paediatric out-of-pocket figure is used;
  - late effects and the reserves' return are built from their parts.
- **Round 2** found no high issues, 2 medium and 10 low, all fixed.

**Rebuild (2026-11-04)**, to the site's new rule that no programme is valued at another model's result and every part whose outcome isn't measured is discounted:

- the training and community programmes, valued before at St. Jude's research result, are now three branches built from the research on training and on HPV vaccination promotion;
- the non-cancer research, valued before at another charity's research model, is now its own branch built from the burden of sickle cell disease and children's influenza;
- every branch takes the shared discount for missing data;
- the late-effect disability weights, typed before, are now shared GBD facts.

- **Round 1** of the rebuild found 2 high, 8 medium and 13 low issues. Fixed:
  - St. Jude's share of the resources at partner hospitals now compares direct costs on both sides, as the research model does (it had set St. Jude's cost with overhead against partners' direct costs);
  - the families' costs covered take the US branch's missing-data discount;
  - partner hospitals' costs are converted from 2018 to 2024 prices;
  - the children the Global Platform's medicines reach are counted, and the gap between St. Jude's share and its share of a unit's own budget is explained;
  - each evidence adjustment now rests on the validity of its own evidence, without repeating the missing-data reason;
  - the patient-care summary says why St. Jude Global, most of the result, is low;
  - the Glover et al. citation and the derivation of the return per research dollar are corrected;
  - years brought forward is stated as calibrated jointly, without a published anchor;
  - the lows: a reading row, labels, the countries count, source details, the overlap between St. Jude Global and the research model, the graduate school's lag, the programme weight.
- **Round 2** found no high issues, 2 medium and 7 low. Fixed:
  - years brought forward: the non-cancer branch takes the template's default (3), the cancer branch keeps 5 with its reason (a small field of long trial series);
  - a second check row against the observed fall in childhood cancer deaths, and the limits of the UK comparison stated;
  - the lows: explainers, a share, the 2018 launch release as a second source, the opening summary.
  - Kept: St. Jude Global's result is spread by world population, the site's rule for work across many low- and middle-income countries with no main one.
- After round 2, the research burden was split, as the site does everywhere: the deaths inside GBD's DALYs are valued by age at the site's value of a death (new shared facts for the ages of childhood cancer, sickle cell and children's influenza deaths), the illness kept as DALYs. The research result fell by about 13%.
- **Round 3** found 1 high, 2 medium and 4 low issues. Fixed:
  - the patient-care model's graduate-school branch, whose return per research dollar is mostly deaths, now values those deaths by age like the rest of the site (high; it moved the result by 0.5%);
  - sickle cell disease is built from its deaths and its illness rather than from a DALY total that its deaths alone would exceed;
  - the check against the observed fall in deaths uses high-income countries' share of the burden in GBD 2023 (6%, not 18%);
  - the lows: labels, the ranges of two new shared facts, the UK-only caveat on the research return, a stale figure.
  - The research result is now 11% below the first rebuild's, the patient-care result 24% below.
