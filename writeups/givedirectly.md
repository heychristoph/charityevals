### Summary

GiveDirectly sends about $1,000 by mobile money to each poor household it enrols in East Africa, with no conditions. GiveWell's January 2025 model of its Cash for Poverty Relief program, the one the rankings use, comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="givedirectly-cash-poverty-relief-2024">5.5</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. GiveWell's earlier model of the Kenya program, which we rebuilt row by row and describe below, comes to <span data-figure="headline" data-model="givedirectly">4.4</span>.

Our adjustments barely move either: most of GiveDirectly's cost is the transfers themselves, so the staff-pay adjustment has little to act on. The plausible range is wide, almost entirely because of GiveWell's own uncertainty about spillovers and long-run returns.

### What the charity does

GiveDirectly identifies villages with high poverty rates, enrols eligible households, and sends one lump-sum transfer, now about $865 in Kenya. About 84 cents of each donated dollar reaches recipients; the rest pays for targeting, enrolment, delivery and overhead. Recipients decide how to spend it. In trials, households consume a little over half in the first year and invest the rest, mostly in livestock, roofs and small businesses.

### How the impact happens

1. Cash raises the household's consumption in the first year, by about 43% over a baseline of $1.79 a day.
2. The invested share pays returns over the next nine years.
3. The money spent locally raises the incomes of neighbours who didn't receive a transfer (spillovers).
4. Preliminary evidence suggests the transfers reduce deaths of young children.

GiveWell values consumption on a log scale, so a dollar counts for more the poorer the person, and converts averted deaths with its moral weights.

### How the model works

The [Kenya model](/givedirectly/givedirectly) follows GiveWell's November 2024 spreadsheet row by row. It works through program reach, then recipient consumption, spillovers and mortality. It finishes with supplementary adjustments (morbidity, development, fraud and fungibility, together +8%). Country data such as purchasing-power conversion and child mortality come from our shared Kenya facts, so other Kenya models will use the same numbers.

### What drives the result

The three biggest levers are:

- **How much GiveWell values consumption against deaths** (a moral weight). It moves the result by about ±22%.
- **How far to trust the spillover estimate.** It rests on one study (Egger et al. 2022), significant only at the 10% level, so GiveWell discounts it heavily. Its internal and external validity adjustments each move the result by about ±20%.
- **How long the investment returns last.** This sits within GiveWell's ±40% range.

No single input changes the result by more than about a quarter. The joint range is wider because it sets them all at once.

### Where we differ from GiveWell

Only in the adjustments we add on top. These are judgments of ours and are labelled as such.

- **Salary sacrifice:** we treat the gap between staff pay and what staff could earn elsewhere as an in-kind donation. For GiveDirectly it is small: staff pay is about 8% of cost and the gap about 15%, so the result falls by about 1.4%.
- **Shared credit:** GiveDirectly delivers transfers itself, so full credit and shared credit give the same answer.


### Biggest uncertainties

- The mortality effect comes from preliminary, unpublished results. GiveWell halves it, and a different read of the final paper could move the result by more than our range shows.
- Spillovers are estimated in Western Kenya in 2014–2017; today's programs run in different markets.
- Our staff cost and pay-gap figures are guesses until we extract GiveDirectly's audited financials.

### What isn't modelled

Room for more funding, organisational strength and track record matter for a giving decision; they aren't scored here yet. Effects on recipients' wellbeing beyond consumption and mortality enter only through GiveWell's supplementary adjustments.
