### Summary

Rainforest Foundation US (RFUS) backs Indigenous peoples in Peru, Brazil, Guyana and Panama to secure and defend their forest. Our model of its 2025 spending comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="rainforest-foundation-us-charityevals">0.013</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**.

Most of that is not the forest's carbon:

- Nearly three fifths is a small cut in the chance of a new pandemic from tropical forest kept standing. That is the least grounded step in the model.
- About three tenths is the pay RFUS's community patrollers bring home, in some of the poorest households of the Amazon.
- The forest's carbon is the rest. Land carbon is worth little in healthy years, as everywhere on the site.

The forest effect rests on a randomised trial of RFUS's own monitoring programme in Peru. Peru's monitoring today is that programme, delivered the same way, so it counts as measured; Peru's territories are about half the result. The other branches take the site's missing-data discount.

### What the charity does

RFUS, founded in 1988, works through Indigenous federations and councils. It does three things, which its tax return reports as three programmes:

- **Land rights.** Legal and mapping work for communal titles. In 2025 it helped secure titles for 67,762 acres in Peru, and strengthened tenure on about 2 million acres in all, through boundary work and title claims.
- **Rainforest protection.** It trains and pays community patrollers who act on monthly satellite deforestation alerts. In 2025 it had more than 400 patrollers, covering 19.6 million acres in Guyana, Brazil, Peru and Panama.
- **Strengthening Indigenous organisations.** Governance, administration and fundraising support, and grants, for 22 Indigenous organisations. In Guyana, for example, it supports district councils that pursue illegal gold miners. About 14% of programme spending goes to the Global Alliance of Territorial Communities and its networks in Africa and Asia.

In 2025 it spent $11.25 million: $9.29 million on programmes, $0.84 million on management and $1.12 million on fundraising. The audited accounts don't split the programmes. In the 2024 Form 990, land rights took 16% of programme spending, rainforest protection 22% and strengthening organisations 61%.

### How the impact happens

The model has five branches, set against the same cost.

1. **Peru's territories.** Paid patrols acting on alerts deter loggers and settlers, so a little less forest is cleared that year.
2. **The other territories** (Guyana, Brazil, Panama). Thinner patrols and stronger organisations do the same.
3. **Global work.** Support for the Global Alliance of Territorial Communities, which presses for land rights and direct finance for forest communities in 24 countries.
4. **Titles.** A title makes invasion and clearing harder. RFUS's work brings titles forward by some years.
5. **The patrollers' pay.** Patrollers are paid for the days they patrol, which raises what their households consume.

The forest kept holds carbon. Less clearing of tropical forest may also cut the chance of a new pandemic a little.

### How the model works

The [model](/rainforest-foundation-us/rainforest-foundation-us-charityevals) follows the site's conservation template.

**Cost.** All $9.29 million of programme spending, plus management and fundraising spread over it: $11.25 million in all.

**The territories.** We start from the 7.9 million hectares under monitoring: 1.05 million in Peru and 6.8 million elsewhere. We multiply by:

- **The share lost each year without the support.** We judge it by country. Peru's is 0.25%, the trial's control communities' rate. Elsewhere it is about 0.08%: Guyana loses about 0.06% a year by its national monitoring, and Guyana is 70% of that area.
- **The share that stays lost** rather than being delayed: half.
- **Carbon per hectare lost:** the tropics' average, about 650 tonnes of CO₂.

In the randomised trial (Slough et al. 2021, 76 communities in Loreto), alerts, training and paid patrols cut forest loss by 52% in the first year and about 21% in the second. Most of Peru's communities are now past their first year. The trial had about 2,200 hectares per monitor. Then the adjustments:

| | Peru | Elsewhere |
|---|---|---|
| Hectares per patroller | about 3,600 | about 62,000 |
| Share of the loss the support prevents | 23% | 8% |
| RFUS's share of the resources | 70% | 38% |
| Evidence | 0.85 | 0.75 |
| Missing-data discount | none: measured | the site's discount |

Elsewhere, most of the work is the organisations RFUS strengthens, such as Guyana's district councils pursuing illegal miners. Over the last decade their budgets came to about twice what RFUS raised for them, so RFUS's share is lower. Delivery is 90% in both.

**Global work.** The alliance's communities hold 958 million hectares. We take a tenth of a percent of that land's yearly forest loss as prevented by a year of the alliance's work, and give RFUS 10% of the credit. This branch is thin and small: about 3% of the result.

**Titles.** The 27,400 hectares titled in 2025, plus a tenth of the 780,000 hectares whose titling advanced. We multiply by:

- the share of untitled community forest lost each year (0.25%);
- the share a title prevents (35%). Blackman et al. 2017 found titling in Peru cut clearing by more than three quarters for two years. In Brazil, full property rights cut deforestation and demarcation alone didn't;
- the site's shared years a won reform counts (five).

RFUS's credit here is 40%: the regional government grants the titles, and the federations file the claims.

**Pandemic risk.** The forest kept in both branches cuts the risk by the same shared figures as the site's other forest models.

**The pay.** We take 400 patrollers × 60 patrol days a year (our judgment) × the $8 a day the trial paid, about $10 in 2025 prices. Half of that is a net gain, since patrol days take time from fishing and farming. That is about $120,000 for about 1,500 people. We put their consumption at 35% of Peru's average, as for the site's other model of Amazonian Indigenous communities. Two side effects are added, as for other cash: deaths averted (+12%) and gains to local traders (+10%).

**A unit cost.** More money pays for more patrollers, titles and partner support at about the same cost, so the model has no next-dollar factor.

### What drives the result

The biggest swings are:

- how much of the pandemic risk from land use scales with forest loss, and how likely a COVID-scale pandemic is;
- how much of the forest kept stays standing;
- the discount rate;
- the missing-data discount;
- the share of Peru's loss the patrols prevent, and Peru's loss rate.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **What the patrols do.** The trial's estimate is imprecise: its first-year interval runs from a 19-hectare cut to a 3-hectare rise per community. Outside Peru the cover is much thinner, and nothing measures it.
- **Pandemic risk from forest loss.** This is a shared judgment, and here it is over half of the result.
- **The patrollers' pay.** RFUS doesn't publish how many days it pays for or at what rate today.
- **Titles' lasting effect.** The Peruvian evidence covers two years after a title. Brazil's evidence is mixed.

### What isn't modelled

These are shares in each branch, not separate rows:

- **Less mercury** from illegal mining, fire brigades, biodiversity and water.
- **The communities' own gains from secure land,** a positive share in the titles branch.
- **Patrollers' skills and standing,** and the dangers they face, in the pay branch.
- **Leakage,** clearing pushed elsewhere, a negative share.
- **Money RFUS helps partners raise:** more than $10.5 million in 2025. It is not counted as extra, since most of it would fund forest work elsewhere.

### How this model was checked

Independent auditors checked the model; the receipts are in audits/rainforest-foundation-us-charityevals/. The first round found that Peru's monitoring is the programme RFUS's own trial measured. So the territories became two branches, Peru counted as measured. It also asked for RFUS's share outside Peru to be built from its partners' budgets, the trial's pay in 2025 prices, and fewer cautious cuts in the evidence adjustments. The second round passed. It asked for the global work as a branch of its own and for Peru's effect to be anchored on the trial's later years, since most communities are past their first; both were done and verified.
