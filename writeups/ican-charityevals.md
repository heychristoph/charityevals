### Summary

ICAN, the International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons, is a Geneva-based coalition of over 700 groups that campaigns for states to join the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons and against nuclear deterrence. Our model of all its work in 2024 comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. That figure rests on forecasts of how likely a nuclear war is, on an estimate of what a typical dollar of nuclear work does to the risk, and on our judgment of how much a treaty that no nuclear-armed state has joined can do. All three are deeply uncertain, so the plausible range runs from about 0.22 to about 340.

### What the charity does

ICAN spent CHF 2.2 million ($2.5 million) in 2024, the latest year with audited accounts. Its accounts book costs by type, not by programme: staff and consultants CHF 1.7 million, campaigning CHF 0.21 million, treaty universalisation CHF 0.07 million, communication CHF 0.06 million, and office and audit costs CHF 0.15 million. Its 2024 annual report describes the work:

- **Getting states to join the treaty.** Four states became parties in 2024: Indonesia, São Tomé and Príncipe, Sierra Leone and the Solomon Islands. That made 73 states parties and 25 further signatories. ICAN lobbied governments and parliaments and held a conference on joining the treaty for African Union states in Addis Ababa.
- **Making the treaty work.** Briefings for diplomats, the treaty's coordination committee and a workshop with the nuclear-weapon-free zones.
- **Campaigning against nuclear weapons.** A yearly report on nuclear weapons spending and a Week of Action with over 50 demonstrations. With PAX it tracks investors in the weapons' producers, which fell from 306 to 287. It runs the cities appeal (123 cities joined in 2024) and the parliamentary pledge (2,128 signers), and publishes research such as The Impact of Nuclear Weapons on Children.
- **Justice for people harmed by past use and testing.**

### How the impact happens

Nuclear war carries a yearly chance of catastrophe, taken from the domain experts' forecasts in the 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament and spread evenly to 2100. They give an 8% chance by 2100 of a war that kills over 10% of people, and 0.55% of one that ends humanity's future. The superforecasters' lower figures are the low end of each range.

A typical project cuts that risk by a share per dollar, taken from Rethink Priorities' cross-cause model, including its chance that a project backfires. The cut counts for the years such progress would otherwise have taken to come (5 by default), with each year discounted. A war the world survives is taken to kill 45% of people, mostly through famine, valued across the world's age groups like every death on the site. A war that ends humanity's future also takes the lives of everyone not yet born. Those future lives count only through the discount rate.

### How the model works

The model has two branches.

**The campaign ({{row:nuclear_cost_share}} of the cost).** Each dollar counts as a typical dollar of nuclear work, then three adjustments:

| Adjustment | Value |
|---|---|
| How far a typical project's effect applies | 0.27 |
| Work other groups would not have done now | 0.7 |
| ICAN's share of the resources | 0.55 |

The first blends two parts. About 15% of the campaign is the treaty's implementation, valued at the treaty's own figure (below). The rest, the work on deterrence, spending, divestment and the media, counts at 0.3. ICAN's work is aimed mostly at states without nuclear weapons and at the public. No arsenal, doctrine or budget is shown to have moved because of it, and nuclear weapons spending rose 11% in 2024, by ICAN's own count. The share of resources is below 1 because partners do much of the work under ICAN's banner.

**The year's new states parties (30% of the cost).** This includes the cities appeal and the parliamentary pledge, whose signers work for their country to join. We value the treaty against what typical nuclear work would achieve with the same resources. About $100 million has been spent on the treaty itself since 2010, by ICAN (about $14 million), its partners, the Red Cross and governments. We judge the treaty to do a tenth of what that much typical work would do, from all but nothing to as much. The nine nuclear-armed states and their allies stay outside it, and the treaty binds only states without weapons. Its effect comes through a legal norm against possession and deterrence. Even public support hasn't moved an allied government: three in four Japanese adults want their country to join, yet Japan stays out. Each new state party adds one 73rd of the treaty's cut, weighted at 0.6: the treaty needed only 50 states to exist, and three of 2024's four are small states outside any alliance, though Indonesia is the largest party. ICAN is credited with 60% of that, the share that wouldn't have happened in 2024 without the campaign, and then with 40% of what remains, its share of the campaigners' resources behind the four ratifications.

The other 5% is the justice work, which cuts no risk of a future war and counts as a side effect. Side effects add 10% in all: a norm against any use also makes a single detonation or a limited war less likely, which has no shared forecast. The treaty is contested among the states it wants to persuade, so harm is allowed down to −10%. Every branch takes the missing-data discount (0.5).

ICAN's income fell from CHF 3.6 million in 2023 to 2.0 million in 2024, and it ran deficits in 2024 and 2025, drawn from funds set aside in 2023. The states left to join are harder to win than those already in. The next dollar counts at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of the past average.

### What drives the result

Deaths in a war the world survives bring almost all of the result. At the default 4% discount rate the future counts for little; at 0.1% the result rises to about 9. The campaign brings about two-thirds of the result and the new states parties a third. The treaty that is ICAN's main achievement doesn't constrain the states whose weapons make up the risk.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much a dollar of nuclear work cuts the risk.** Rethink's figures are placeholders, and the range runs ten times either way. This is the largest swing, from about 0.51 to 23.
- **How much the treaty does.** From all but nothing to as much as typical work, the result moves from about 1.8 to 11.
- **How far the campaign beats or trails a typical project.** From 0.05 to 1, the result moves from about 1.2 to 6.6.
- **How many years a cut counts before it would have come anyway.** The shared slider runs from 1 to 20 years.
- **How likely and how deadly a nuclear war is.** The superforecasters put the chance at half the experts' figure. The share who die in a war the world survives runs from 15% to 85%.

### What isn't modelled

- Losses beyond deaths, such as survivors' lost income and health, are a shared figure set to 0.
- The forecasts are spread evenly over the century, though forecasters put much of the risk later.
- The 2025 accounts are preliminary (CHF 2.3 million spent) and aren't used. The audited 2025 statement isn't published.

### How this model was checked

The model was built from the catastrophic-risk template. Round 1 of the independent audit passed with three medium and four low findings, all fixed. The treaty's paths to the risk and the campaign's now each have one home: the treaty's implementation is valued at the treaty's own figure, and the cities and parliamentary work moved into the new-parties branch. The treaty's resources count only what was spent on the treaty itself, with ICAN's part summed from its annual reports. A new state party now weighs less than the average one. The next-dollar figure is weighted by both branches, and template placeholders were removed. Round 1 lowered the result by about a quarter. Round 2, a check of those fixes, passed with four small findings, also fixed: a smaller implementation share, ICAN's share of the campaign's resources, the next-dollar note's weights and sources for the treaty precedents. A final check covered those changes. The rounds are in audits/ican-charityevals/.
