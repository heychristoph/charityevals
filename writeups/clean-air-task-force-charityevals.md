### Summary

Clean Air Task Force (CATF) does research and advocacy for climate policy, from methane rules to advanced nuclear power. Our model counts the six biggest policies it worked on that were won between 2022 and 2025, against what it spent over those years. It comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. Nearly all of that comes from two methane rules. The result is low for two reasons: CATF shares each win with many other groups and with what governments would have done anyway, and the US rules have since been weakened or repealed.

### What the charity does

CATF spent $43.0 million in 2025, $33.5 million of it on programmes (audited accounts). The accounts don't split programme spending by programme. The 2024 tax return does:

- **Methane: $5.1 million (18%).** Rules on methane from oil, gas and coal in the US, Europe and, since 2024, up to 20 low- and middle-income countries. This includes work on agricultural and waste methane.
- **Advanced nuclear: $5.0 million (18%).** Licensing reform, finance, and global pledges to triple nuclear capacity.
- **Fusion: $2.0 million (7%).** Regulatory frameworks and supply chains for fusion energy.
- **Other: $16.0 million (57%).** US power-plant and carbon-capture policy, hydrogen and clean fuels, superhot-rock geothermal, US state laws, energy access in Africa, and legal defence of existing rules.

### How the impact happens

CATF designs rules, publishes the research behind them, lobbies for them and defends them in court. A won rule or tax credit cuts emissions once it takes effect. The model counts these wins:

- **The EU Methane Regulation (2024).** Its import standard would cut about 110 million tonnes of CO₂e a year from 2030 (Oeko-Institut).
- **EPA's oil and gas methane rule (2023).** About 100 million tonnes a year (EPA).
- **The Inflation Reduction Act's bigger carbon-capture credit (2022).** About 60 million tonnes a year once projects are built. This is our judgment: Princeton’s 2022 projection credited the law with about 185 million.
- **EPA's power-plant carbon standards (2024).** About 69 million tonnes a year (EPA). EPA repealed most of them in 2026.
- **The ADVANCE Act (2024)** and the tripling pledges for nuclear power, which help advanced nuclear reach DOE's pathway of 200 GW by 2050.
- **Fusion's regulatory framework** (2023, written into law in 2024).

### How the model works

For each win: tonnes a year × the share of a won policy that is advocacy's doing (a shared figure, 30%) × the years a won reform counts (the shared 5 years, discounted, after the wait until the tonnes come) × the share that wouldn't have happened anyway × CATF's share of all the groups' work behind it × the share actually delivered. For the technologies we also count how much of DOE's pathway gets built (30%) and how much of that the period's policies cause (5%). Fusion also needs fusion to work commercially, which we put at 15%.

The wins are added up and spread over the four years. The rules' compliance costs and the extra cost of the nuclear power the wins bring forward (the shared US figure, from the IEA/NEA's levelised costs) are taken off as money lost by those who pay. Then come the shared evidence and missing-data steps, other work not modelled (+30%) and small side effects: extra oil from captured CO₂, nuclear risk. The US methane rule's local smog (EPA's 56 to 74 ozone deaths a year, the shared figure) is counted with the tonnes, as in the Earthjustice and Sierra Club models. The total is divided by a typical year's spending in 2022-2025. The next dollar is valued at 39% of that average: CATF holds about 13 months of spending in reserve, and its next dollar goes to harder ground, like defending rules, Europe's implementation and new fields.

Each tonne counts for its heat deaths. Methane's come sooner than CO₂'s, within decades, so they are discounted less. Methane also forms ground-level ozone worldwide, which kills people with lung and heart disease; at the defaults those deaths are worth about as much as its heat deaths. Both use the site's shared methane figures. New reactors and the power-plant rules cut US power-plant smoke, but at the shared US figures that is very little.

### What drives the result

The EU regulation gives about half the modelled tonnes, the US methane rule about three-tenths and the carbon-capture credit an eighth. Nuclear and fusion give 2% or less: their tonnes come late and depend on several uncertain steps. So the methane line, under a fifth of spending, carries most of the result. The shared value judgments move the result most: the discount rate, the years a won reform counts and methane's ozone deaths per tonne.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much of a win is advocacy's doing.** The shared 30% runs from 10% to 60%, and CATF's own share of each win is our judgment.
- **Whether the rules survive.** EPA has delayed and loosened the methane rule and repealed most of the power-plant standards. The EU has proposed more flexibility in its import standard.
- **Reshuffled trade.** Exporters could send their cleanest gas to Europe and the rest elsewhere. We count half the import cut as real.
- **Methane's ozone deaths per tonne.** The shared figure runs from a third to three times its best guess.

### What isn't modelled

Wins before 2022 aren't counted: the US methane and coal-plant rules of the 2010s and the 2018 carbon-capture credit. Their cost isn't in the period either. Smaller wins of the period are covered only by the 30% for other work: Europe's own methane rules, methane programmes in other countries, hydrogen rules, state laws.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits are in audits/clean-air-task-force-charityevals/. Round 1 found methane's ozone deaths missing; they now come from a shared figure, which raised the result about 3.5 times. It also re-anchored the carbon-capture tonnes on Princeton's REPEAT projection, used EPA's own figures for the coal plants' smoke and the methane rule's local smog, and moved the EU import limit's date to 2031. Round 4 checked the site-wide methane change: methane's heat deaths on their own timing and its ozone discounted over methane's lifetime, both in the shared conversion (+3.5%).
