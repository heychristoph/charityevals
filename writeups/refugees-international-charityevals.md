### Summary

Refugees International is a Washington advocacy group that researches displacement crises and presses governments and the UN for aid, access and protection for displaced people. It takes no government or UN money. It spent $4.5 million on advocacy and strategic outreach in 2024, its latest full audited year. We estimate **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. It publishes no measured outcome, so each result below is built from public data and ranged judgments and counted at half by the site's missing-data slider.

### How the model works

Both programme lines (advocacy, and the media and public education of strategic outreach) serve the same campaigns, so the wins below are set against all of the spending.

**Haitian parole** (about two fifths of the result). About 97,000 Haitians were paroled into the US in 2024 under the processes for Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans and Venezuelans, from US Customs and Border Protection's counts. Refugees International pressed for these pathways. Each parolee gains the step from consumption in Haiti to that of refugees newly in a rich country (the site's shared figure, about the lowest-income fifth of US households), on the site's log-income scale, at 2024 prices on both sides. Most of the gap in adult death rates between Haiti and the US goes too, each death valued by age. This counts for the share who wouldn't otherwise have come, over the years they stay (4, from 1.5 to 15: the processes ended in 2025). Money sent home to family in Haiti adds to it, and the loss of skilled workers to Haiti takes a little away (both side effects). The pathway was the administration's own border policy, so advocacy is credited with 10% of it and Refugees International with 5% of the advocacy.

**Sudan** (about a quarter). Sudan's 2024 humanitarian response plan received $1.9 billion. A dollar of the response is valued at the site's shared figure for a humanitarian dollar (0.2 of cash) times 1.5 for a famine response. Half the extra money is taken from other crises (a side effect). Advocacy as a whole is credited with 3% of the funding. Refugees International is credited with 4.5% of that advocacy: the US paid 45% of the plan, and it was about a tenth of US advocacy on Sudan. Access is its own small branch: the reopened Adre crossing let through about 2% of the year's aid, a fifth of it due to advocacy.

**Costa Rica** (under a twentieth). A June 2024 decree put a work permit on asylum seekers' identity cards, reversing part of a 2022 decree that Refugees International and IRCA Casabierta had contested. About 20,000 permits a year are issued. We take 70% of them as the reform's doing, at 40% of the consumption gain from Colombia's regularisation of Venezuelans (48%, Ibáñez et al.), over the years a won reform counts.

**Other campaigns** add 42% of these: Ukraine's localisation of aid, the Gaza scorecard on aid obstruction, the Sudan envoy and genocide determination, bills it helped draft that haven't passed, climate displacement, family-reunification parole, its Refugee Fellows and the Refugee Advocacy Lab.

Each branch has its own evidence, counterfactual and credit adjustments and the missing-data discount. Past wins count at Refugees International's own share for the next dollar ({{row:marginal_vs_past_average}}): it holds about nine months of spending in reserve, and the US ground has turned harder since 2024.

### What drives the result

The missing-data slider and the next-dollar share come first. Then the value of a humanitarian dollar, the share of Sudan's funding advocacy added, the share of the parole pathway due to advocacy, how long parolees stay, and Refugees International's share of each campaign. The plausible range runs from {{low}} to {{high}}.

### Biggest uncertainties

How much any advocacy changed the funding or the policy, and Refugees International's part among many groups, are judgments with no study behind them. How long parolees stay after the processes ended is unknown.

### How this model was checked

Built with charityevals' policy-research-meta template on 14 November 2026. Round 1 of the independent audit found two high findings, both fixed: the template's career placeholders became shared defaults read by every model of the family, and Haiti's consumption is now at 2024 prices (it had used 2017's price ratio). Also fixed: a humanitarian dollar uses a shared figure, with Sudan's famine premium its own row; access counts on the funding's gross value; the other campaigns' share is anchored by a count; remittances are built from the money sent; the whole adult death-rate gap is counted, not only gang violence; and the share of Costa Rica's permits due to the reform rose to 70%. The result rose from 0.11 to 0.16, and round 2 passed. Its four medium findings were then fixed: the humanitarian dollar now reads the site's shared rule for aid won (0.2 of cash), the Sudan evidence adjustment no longer repeats other rows, access has its own branch and counterfactual, and the credit shares are anchored. Its lows were fixed too: deaths valued by age, US consumption from the shared refugee figure, the loss to Haiti counted on the parolees' own gain only, family reunification in the other campaigns. The result fell to 0.13. A verification round checked those changes.
