### Summary

Rethink Priorities is a research organisation that helps funders and charities decide where money does most good, in animal welfare, global health and catastrophic risks. We model a gift for its own research, in three parts: animal welfare, global health and development, and its cross-cause and survey work. Its animal welfare research comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**, its global health research to {{result:rethink-priorities-charityevals-global-health}}, and its cross-cause research and surveys to {{result:rethink-priorities-charityevals-cross-cause}}. Research changes how other people spend money they would have spent anyway, so each part is worth the money it steered times how much better that money now does. Nobody has measured what its clients did differently, so most of the numbers behind this are our judgments, with wide ranges.

### What the charity does

Rethink Priorities spent $22.4 million in 2025, by its audited accounts. Most of that, $15.6 million, was spent by projects it fiscally sponsors or incubated: Apollo Research, Epoch, the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, Truthful AI and others. That money was given to those projects and is theirs, so it is left out here. Epoch AI has its own model on this site.

Its own four research departments spent $3.4 million:

- **Animal welfare** ($1.40 million): research and coordination for animal advocates and funders. In 2025 it helped funders deploy almost $10 million of grants for EU farmed animal welfare advocacy, ran a strategy forum for 26 advocacy groups, built a fish welfare roadmap with 18, and did three commissioned projects for Open Philanthropy.
- **Global health and development** ($0.98 million): 12 commissioned projects for foundations such as the Gates Foundation, Packard, Open Philanthropy and D-Prize, paid for by their fees, and a donor guide to the US foreign aid pause.
- **Worldview investigations** ($0.72 million): cross-cause prioritisation, moral weights, digital minds and models of movement building.
- **Surveys and data analysis** ($0.32 million): polls and message tests, mostly private commissions for effective-giving organisations and funders.

Management and fundraising cost another $3.3 million. We judge that about 70% of it serves the four departments; the rest serves the sponsored projects, which pay fees.

### How the impact happens

Rethink Priorities runs no programmes itself. Funders and organisations read its research and change some of what they fund or do. If the research is right, money that would have gone to something ordinary goes to something better.

### How the model works

Each part counts the money its research informed in 2025 and the share of it that the research changed. For animal welfare, that is the funders' $10 million of EU grants (we judge 15% changed), the budgets of the 26 groups at its forum (we judge 2% changed) and three commissioned projects for Open Philanthropy. For global health, it is the funding decision behind each of its 11 global health projects (about $3 million each, by our judgment), of which we judge a tenth changed; a twelfth project was a climate strategy. For worldview investigations and surveys no count is published, so the money informed, the share changed and the split between causes are our judgments.

All of this money would have been spent anyway, so it counts only for what it gains. By the site's rule for shifted money, it moves from an ordinary charity of its cause to the cause's portfolio (the charities a published fund paid for that cause), read live. For animal welfare, that is the animal portfolio. For global health, it is GiveWell's Top Charities, weighted by its funds, against GiveDirectly. For the cross-cause work it is global health, animal welfare and AI safety, weighted by our judgment of the teams' 2025 work (25%, 35% and 30%), each against an ordinary charity of the same cause; its digital minds and movement work have no cause on the site and drop out.

Each part then takes an evidence step (0.7; 0.6 for cross-cause work, whose judgments are more contested) for research that points the wrong way. It also takes the site's discount for outcomes nobody has measured. Side effects are a row in each part. A further dollar does less than the average dollar so far: {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} for animal welfare, which holds two years of restricted money; 17% for global health, whose commissioned work paid for itself, so a gift buys its independent research; 31% for the rest.

### What drives the result

- **The shares changed.** These are our judgments, and each moves its part's result in proportion.
- **The size of the decisions informed.** For global health this is a judgment from $0.5 million to $20 million per project.
- **The value of a dollar in each cause.** The animal part is tiny on the common scale because the site's animal weights make animal charities tiny. The global health part is large because the programme money it informs (family planning, education, credit) is valued at the global-health portfolio, GiveWell's Top Charities. The cross-cause part follows its global health and AI shares, since its animal share is worth almost nothing on the common scale.
- **The next dollar.** Rethink Priorities holds about 11 months of its costs without donor restrictions, against an aim of three, and more restricted to its departments. It says extra money would go to exploratory research rather than the work counted here.

### Biggest uncertainties

- How much funders and organisations actually changed because of the research. Rethink Priorities reports outputs and says it is building an evaluation of their effects.
- Whether the changes made the money do more good, or less.
- How much of the management cost the sponsored projects' fees pay for.

### What isn't modelled

- Research from earlier years that funders still use, such as its welfare ranges, which this site uses for its animal weights. Its 2025 results are counted, not its whole record.
- The sponsored projects' own work: theirs, paid for by their own donors.
- Where the fellows and early-career researchers it trained went: no count is published, so they are only part of each model's side-effects row.

### How this model was checked

Audit rounds are recorded in audits/rethink-priorities-charityevals/ (and the other two models' folders). Round 1 found four high and four medium problems. The global health part now values what a gift buys, its independent research, through its own next-dollar figure, which cut its result by about three fifths. The next-dollar figures read the reserves correctly: about 20 months, not near a 12-month aim. The animal part adds three commissioned projects and a mean, not a median, budget for the groups it convenes. The cross-cause part weights the causes by named judgments instead of equal thirds. Side effects no longer count research pointing the wrong way twice, nor earlier years' welfare-range work. Round 2 found three high problems, all in the cross-cause part: its AI share now reads the AI charities, not all catastrophic-risk charities, and its shifted money is compared with an ordinary charity of each cause, not GiveDirectly alone, which also made its simulation stable again.
