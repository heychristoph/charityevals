### Summary

Besides medical care, Médecins Sans Frontières spends about 65 million euros a year on awareness-raising (public communications and witnessing, *témoignage*), MSF Access (advocacy for affordable medicines and tests) and a grant to the Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative (DNDi), which it co-founded. We estimate **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="doctors-without-borders-charityevals-advocacy">0.094</span> years of healthy life per $1,000** for this work, 3.3% of MSF's social-mission spending. None of the three publishes outcome data, so each is built from the research on its kind of work, with wide ranges, and counted at half by the site's shared missing-data slider. MSF's main model, its medical care, is [here](/doctors-without-borders/doctors-without-borders-charityevals).

### How the model works

**MSF Access: the price cuts it campaigned for** (77% of the result). Two cuts of 2023 are documented: Cepheid's Xpert TB test from $9.98 to $7.97 for high-burden countries, after the Time for $5 campaign MSF, Partners in Health and Treatment Action Group coordinated from 2019 (about 20 million tests procured in 2022, the Global Fund's figure), and bedaquiline for drug-resistant TB from $289 to $130 a course through the Global Drug Facility's tender ($8 million a year saved). Other cuts of the period (the BPaLM regimen, vaccines) add half as much again. A dollar the buyers save is valued at the site's shared value of a dollar of Global Fund spending (65%) and of government spending (35%). Each cut counts the site's shared years a won reform brings forward (5), against MSF Access's spending over the seven campaigning years (from its accounts, in 2025 prices, 1.16 times 2025's). Half of each cut is taken as not coming yet without the campaigners (bedaquiline's came with its patent's expiry and a tender, Xpert's after four years of pressure), and MSF has a quarter of the advocates' resources (a coalition of more than 150 civil society organisations; the bedaquiline price was negotiated by GDF with USAID and the Global Fund).

**Awareness-raising** (17%). The research on media and aid finds that news coverage moves humanitarian funding: US disaster relief depends on whether other news crowds a disaster out (Eisensee and Strömberg 2007), a minute of news raised tsunami donations by about 2.5% (Brown and Minty 2008), and coverage is the most consistent predictor of how fully an appeal is funded (Rost and Clarke 2025: ten times the news articles, 6 points more of the requirements met), though donor officials say only emergency top-ups respond, not annual allocations (Scott et al. 2021). Our chain: the 2025 appeals' funding (35% of $47 billion) to the crises MSF speaks about (70%) × a funding response of 0.1 per unit of coverage (Rost and Clarke's slope at the coverage of MSF's crises, at 2025's 35%) × the 1.5% more coverage MSF's communications bring × the 30% of the extra money that is new rather than taken from other crises, at the value of cash to the very poor; plus half as much again for its other aims (pressure over attacks on hospitals, access, migration policy).

**DNDi** (6%). The one published return of a drug-development partnership, Medicines for Malaria Venture's (87 million DALYs averted by its antimalarials for $2.3 billion of R&D and $785 million of delivery, Christen et al. 2026), scaled to DNDi's portfolio of smaller diseases (15% of MMV's return per dollar), of which half is gain other developers wouldn't have brought (Christen et al.'s DALYs are already net of the older treatments), with DNDi's share of its partners' in-kind work, after an eight-year wait from funding to use. This branch is counted in DALYs and enters through the site's bridge.

Because the price cuts and DNDi's treatments are past wins set against past spending, the next dollar counts at the site's shared share of the average (50%).

### What drives the result

The missing-data slider and the next dollar's share of the average (each from about 0.02-0.03 to 0.19), the years a win counts (0.04 to 0.24), MSF's share of the price campaigns (0.05 to 0.17), and how much of the cuts wouldn't have come yet (0.05 to 0.14). The result is mostly uncertainty: the combined range runs from 0.01 to 0.85.

### Why it is so low

Under a tenth of the site's median health charity: advocacy's effects are spread over many actors and MSF's share of them is small, the evidence links spending to outcomes only through long chains of judgment, all three branches are discounted for missing data, and past wins count at half for the next dollar. A higher figure would need MSF to have caused more of the price cuts than a quarter of a half, or its communications to move much more aid than the research suggests.

### How this model was checked

New on 4 November 2026: these programmes were valued until then in MSF's medical model at other site models' results (awareness-raising at Development Media International's, MSF Access and DNDi at the median of three policy charities'), a proxy the site retired the same day. Built with charityevals' policy-research-meta template; the mechanical audit and the site's invariants pass. Independent audit, round 1 (no high, 6 medium, 5 low): the Xpert volume now rests on the Global Fund's 2022 figure; awareness's counterfactual is no longer asked twice (the coverage share is already MSF's addition); the media effect is derived from Rost and Clarke's appeal-funding estimate (0.1, from 0.2; round 2 read their slope at the coverage of MSF's crises rather than the least-covered); MSF Access's spending over 2019-2025 comes from its accounts in 2025 prices; the counterfactual asks what wouldn't have come yet; the evidence adjustment no longer doubts the shared value of a dollar; DNDi's counterfactual no longer discounts the older treatments twice, and its wait is discounted. Round 2 (no high, no medium, 4 low): the media effect's source and slope, the coalition's size and the price quotes corrected.
