### Summary

Habitat for Humanity International (HFHI) helps low-income families in more than 60 countries build, extend and repair their homes through Habitat national organisations, and works with lenders and building-material firms so that poorer households can buy housing loans and products. In the year to June 2025 it spent $75.5 million on this international work. Our estimate of a dollar given to HFHI for it is **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. HFHI's US affiliates work, its largest programme, is modelled separately ([US model](/habitat-for-humanity/habitat-for-humanity-charityevals), {{result:habitat-for-humanity-charityevals}}), as are its public awareness, education and advocacy.

HFHI's dollar is about a fifth of the resources behind the network's housing, close to 60% of the families are in middle-income Latin America, Europe and the Middle East, and a home improvement is an in-kind transfer. Habitat doesn't measure what families got, so both branches take the site's shared discount for missing data.

### What the charity does

Outside the US and Canada, most of Habitat's direct work is incremental: a floor, a room, a roof, a latrine at a time (265,000 people in FY2025), plus repairs (178,000 people), a few new or rehabilitated homes (30,000) and disaster shelter. HFHI's money reaches it as cash transfers ($31 million: grants to the national organisations it funds, and the eleven consolidated in its accounts spending directly on materials and contractors) and through its area and country offices' and consolidated organisations' staff ($28 million). About $1.5 million of the line is Home Equals advocacy, counted with the awareness model. Its Terwilliger Center for Innovation in Shelter, MicroBuild and the national offices' market programmes work with microfinance institutions and firms; Habitat counts 2.4 million people in households that bought from these partners.

### How the impact happens

1. A family gets better housing than it would have built itself that year, paid for by Habitat net of what the family repays: a transfer, enjoyed over the years the improvement lasts.
2. Some improvements (cement floors, latrines, stoves) also improve health.
3. Market partners sell housing loans and products on better terms than the families' alternatives; the surplus over the next-best option is the benefit.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's cash-transfer template with two branches:

- **Direct housing.** Habitat's count of 472,900 people in direct housing outside the US and Canada, at a net subsidy of about $1,500 a family, spread over the improvement's life and valued per person on the site's log-consumption scale at each region's consumption (the World Bank's household-survey means for 13 Habitat countries, carried to 2024 by real growth and the site's price index, times how far below the survey mean Habitat's families are). HFHI is credited with its share of the resources behind that housing: its own cost of the direct work (about four-fifths of its international cost: its transfers for housing and a judged share of its staff), against the national organisations' own spending (what the families got less what HFHI's transfers delivered, over the share of a national organisation's spending that reaches families: 60%, below the 79% Habitat for Humanity Poland's audited accounts show for all its programme work, staff included), volunteers' time and HFHI's cost. That share is about a fifth. Spillovers to local builders and a small share for child deaths averted are added.
- **Market development.** Habitat's count of 2.4 million people reached through partners, a surplus of about $150 for a household that borrowed (the microcredit trials find small gains: Banerjee, Karlan and Zinman 2015; Meager 2019) and $20 for one that bought materials or services, two in five borrowers, counted once, region by region at somewhat higher consumption, with only the share of partners' sales due to Habitat (40%), a discount for the reliability of the count (80%), HFHI's share of Habitat's market work (70%), and Habitat's next-dollar figure, since these sales come from lines set up in earlier years.
- **Missing data.** Both branches take the site's shared discount for programmes whose results aren't measured.

### What drives the result

- **How much we count a programme whose results aren't measured** and **the value of a healthy year against a doubled income** (site-wide sliders).
- **The share of a national organisation's spending that reaches families**, which sets HFHI's share, and **the share of the housing that wouldn't have happened anyway**.
- The families' consumption in each region, and the market branch (about an eighth of the result).

### Biggest uncertainties

- HFHI publishes no split of its international spending between direct housing and market work, and no average subsidy per family.
- The market branch rests on Habitat's own count of partners' customers and on judgments of what Habitat added.
- Health effects are measured for cement floors in Mexico, not for Habitat's mix of work.

### What isn't modelled

Tenure security from land and titling help, illness averted by floors and latrines, and disaster resilience of the homes are a positive share on top, not modelled one by one. Habitat's Home Equals advocacy (about $1.5 million of this line, taken out of the cost here) is modelled with the public awareness programme.

### How this model was checked

Independent reviews, each by a fresh reviewer working from the model alone and re-checking the sources: three rounds of an earlier build (3 November 2026, not published), three of the rebuild (4 November: survey-scale consumption through shared per-country facts, the consolidated national organisations no longer discounted twice), and an audit on 15 November 2026. That audit put the direct branch on the network's count with HFHI credited by its share of the resources, as in the US model (the result roughly doubled), made the market branch a past average, took donated products out of the cost and the overhead ratio, and re-anchored the market counterfactual. Left open: no published split of HFHI's international spending, and the national organisations' delivery share rests on one country's accounts.
