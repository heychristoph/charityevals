### Summary

Habitat for Humanity International (HFHI) helps low-income families in more than 60 countries build, extend and repair their homes through Habitat national organisations, and works with lenders and building-material firms so that poorer households can buy housing loans and products. In the year to June 2025 it spent $75.5 million on this international work. Our estimate of a dollar given to HFHI for it is **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals-international">0.07</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. HFHI's US affiliates work, its largest programme, is modelled separately ([US model](/habitat-for-humanity/habitat-for-humanity-charityevals), <span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals">0.003</span>), as are its public awareness, education and advocacy (modelled separately; that model is held back until a review finds no high finding).

It is well below cash transfers because only about 17 cents of each dollar reaches families as housing, close to 60% of the families are in middle-income Latin America, Europe and the Middle East and better off than cash recipients in East Africa, and a home improvement is an in-kind transfer. HFHI counts neither the families its own money reached nor what they got, so both branches take the site's shared discount for missing data.

### What the charity does

Outside the US and Canada, most of Habitat's direct work is incremental: a floor, a room, a roof, a latrine at a time (265,000 people in FY2025), plus repairs (178,000 people), a few new or rehabilitated homes (30,000) and disaster shelter. HFHI's money reaches it as transfers ($31 million: grants to the national organisations it funds, and the eleven consolidated in its accounts spending directly on materials and contractors) and through its area and country offices' and consolidated organisations' staff ($28 million). About $1.5 million of the line is Home Equals advocacy, counted with the awareness model. Its Terwilliger Center for Innovation in Shelter, MicroBuild and the national offices' market programmes work with microfinance institutions and firms; Habitat counts 2.4 million people in households that bought from these partners.

### How the impact happens

1. A family gets better housing than it would have built itself that year, paid for by Habitat net of what the family repays: a transfer, enjoyed over the years the improvement lasts.
2. Some improvements (cement floors, latrines, stoves) also improve health.
3. Market partners sell housing loans and products on better terms than the families' alternatives; the surplus over the next-best option is the benefit.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's cash-transfer template with two branches:

- **Direct housing.** HFHI's audited transfers to international partners ($31.6 million), times the share of them for direct housing (80%, a judgment: HFHI doesn't split its spending) and the share that reaches families as materials, labour and subsidy: 90% for the eleven national organisations consolidated in HFHI's accounts, whose staff are already in its salaries line (about 30% of the transfers), and 60% for the independent ones HFHI funds (below the 79% that Habitat for Humanity Poland's audited accounts show for all its programme work, staff included). Divided by a net subsidy of about $1,500 a family, this gives the families HFHI's own transfers reach. The subsidy is spread over the improvement's life and valued per person on the site's log-consumption scale at each region's consumption (the World Bank's household-survey means for 13 Habitat countries, carried to 2024 by real growth and the site's price index, times how far below the survey mean Habitat's families are), weighted by where the families are. HFHI is credited with 80% of the resources behind that housing (national organisations' own staff and donors and volunteers share the rest; families' own labour is netted out of the subsidy). Spillovers to local builders and a small share for child deaths averted are added.
- **Market development.** Habitat's count of 2.4 million people reached through partners (as low as the Form 990's 300,000 Terwilliger households in the range), a surplus of about $150 for a household that borrowed (the microcredit trials find small gains: Banerjee, Karlan and Zinman 2015; Meager 2019) and $20 for one that bought materials or services, two in five borrowers, counted once, region by region at somewhat higher consumption, with only the share of partners' sales due to Habitat (30%), a discount for the reliability of the count (80%) and HFHI's share of Habitat's market work (70%).
- **Missing data.** Both branches take the site's shared discount for programmes whose results aren't measured.

### What drives the result

- **How much we count a programme whose results aren't measured** and **the value of a healthy year against a doubled income** (site-wide sliders).
- **The market surplus per borrower** and **how many partners' sales Habitat caused**: the market branch is about a third of the result.
- **The share of the transfers national organisations pass on to families**, and the families' consumption in each region.

### Biggest uncertainties

- HFHI publishes no split of its international spending between direct housing and market work, and no average subsidy per family.
- The market branch rests on Habitat's own count of partners' customers and on judgments of what Habitat added.
- Health effects are measured for cement floors in Mexico, not for Habitat's mix of work.

### What isn't modelled

Tenure security from land and titling help, illness averted by floors and latrines, and disaster resilience of the homes are a positive share on top, not modelled one by one. Habitat's Home Equals advocacy (about $1.5 million of this line, taken out of the cost here) is modelled with the public awareness programme.

### How this model was checked

Earlier build (3 November 2026), not published: three rounds of independent review each found a high finding. Round 1 (1 high, 6 medium, 5 low): the share of spending reaching families was applied to the wrong cost base (rebuilt on the audited transfers line). Round 2 (1 high, 2 medium, 6 low): families' consumption was on the national-accounts scale (moved to the survey scale). Round 3 (1 high, 3 medium, 7 low): the survey ratio came from stand-in countries but multiplied richer regional averages (each region's level now comes from the stand-in countries' own survey means).

Rebuild (4 November 2026), three rounds of independent review: round 1 (1 high, 2 medium, 8 low): the survey means were moved to 2024 prices by a typed factor (now per-country shared facts, facts/survey_consumption.json, through the site's price index, carried forward by the World Bank's line-up rule); the market gain is taken region by region; the eleven national organisations consolidated in HFHI's accounts are no longer discounted twice for their staff. Round 2 (0 high, 0 medium, 5 low): Home Equals' cost taken out of this line, the consolidated share's anchor stated. Round 3 (0 high, 0 medium, 4 low): household sizes from the consumption stand-ins' own surveys (DHS, Eurostat), growth passed through at 0.7 for consumption surveys, newer Kenyan and Bangladeshi surveys, wording. Left open: the consolidated national organisations' share of the transfers (0.3, range 0.1 to 0.5) has no published split; the Form 990's Schedule F could anchor it.
