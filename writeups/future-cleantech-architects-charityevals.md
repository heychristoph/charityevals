### Summary

Future Cleantech Architects is a small German think tank that gives EU and German lawmakers technical analysis on clean technologies for the hardest sectors: industrial heat, steel, cement, hydrogen, and aviation and shipping fuels. Our model counts the two policies it helped win between 2021 and 2025, against what it spent over those years. It comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="future-cleantech-architects-charityevals">0.0007</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. Nearly all of that comes from one win: the EU's target that 5% of new renewable capacity be innovative technology. The result is mostly uncertainty.

### What the charity does

Future Cleantech Architects gGmbH, based in Remscheid with a team in Brussels, spent €1.37 million in 2024, $1.48 million (accounts filed in the German Bundestag's lobby register). Staff costs were 79% of that. The accounts don't split spending by programme, so all of it is counted. It hired its first employee in 2021; spending grew from €0.63 million in 2022 to €1.03 million in 2023. Donations reached about €4.1 million in 2025, and it hired eight people that year.

Its work is technical reports and policy briefs, a monthly briefing series for EU and Bundestag staff ("Coffee & Cleantech"), consultation responses and coalition letters, events at the UN climate conferences, and research consortia with German public grants. One of these, on making cement with solar heat, is climate work. Two others are on how manufacturing firms organise work and learning (AgilOLab, DICoLab): about 2% of its spending over the period, by our estimate.

### How the impact happens

A policy it helped win cuts emissions once it takes effect. Future Cleantech Architects' annual reports name two wins in the period:

- **The 5% target for innovative renewables (Renewable Energy Directive, 2023).** Each member state should set an indicative target that at least 5% of the renewable capacity it adds by 2030 be "innovative renewable energy technology". The target began as a European Parliament amendment. Future Cleantech Architects says it advised on the revision from December 2021, made joint recommendations with the research centres' association EUREC, and now tracks how member states implement the target.
- **The low-carbon fuels delegated act (2025).** The European Parliament's industry committee asked Future Cleantech Architects for an in-depth analysis of the Commission's method for counting the emissions of low-carbon hydrogen. It presented that analysis before the vote. In October 2025 the Parliament rejected the motions to block the act, by 373 votes to 180. The motions had sought looser accounting of methane leaked upstream.

### How the model works

**The 5% target.** If every member state met it, the EU would add about 24 GW of innovative renewables: 5% of the roughly 470 GW of wind and solar expected in 2025-2030 (industry outlooks, a shared figure). We count the electricity this capacity makes, at a 30% capacity factor, and the 30% of it that displaces fossil power rather than other renewables, at 0.45 tonnes of CO₂ per MWh. EU power plants are under the emissions trading cap, so most allowances the displaced plants free are used elsewhere. Only the part the market stability reserve cancels is a net cut: 30%, a shared figure. That is about 2.5 million tonnes a year. Member states will report most of the target as met (we assume 70%), because almost anything counts, but only about an eighth of what they report is there because of the target: about 2 GW.

The target's purpose is learning: early capacity makes these technologies cheaper later. Each doubling of what is built cuts their cost by about 20% (Way et al. 2022). We assume yearly installations grow about 1% for each 1% fall in cost; measured demand for home solar panels ranges from about that to several times it (Gillingham & Tsvetanov 2019; Hughes & Podolefsky 2015). Floating wind is expected to grow about tenfold from 2030 to 2040. So each gigawatt the target causes brings about three-quarters of a gigawatt more later, two-fifths of it in the EU (under the cap) and the rest elsewhere, where coal is a larger part of the power it displaces. Those tonnes come after the 15-year wait the site uses for new technologies.

**The delegated act.** We count 0.6 million tonnes a year of hydrogen made from gas with carbon capture, Hydrogen Europe's figure for the EU's 2030 potential. We add 0.3 tonnes of extra emissions per tonne that a looser methane rule would have let through.

**The research consortia.** These take about 2% of spending. We assume 25 cents a year of later income per dollar, for ten years after a three-year wait. Discounted, that is about $2 a dollar, below what Jones & Summers (2020) estimate for research on average. The gains go to German workers and owners, and enter the tonnes through the site's fixed bridge between units.

**For each win**, the steps are:

- the share of a won policy that is advocacy's doing (a shared figure, 30%);
- the years a won reform counts (the shared 5 years, discounted, after the wait);
- the share that wouldn't have happened anyway. For the target it is the eighth above. For the act it is 10%, because the motions failed by a wide margin;
- Future Cleantech Architects' share of all the advocates' work: 10% for the target and its learning, 5% for the act.

The wins are added up and spread over the five years. Then come our evidence adjustment, the shared missing-data step and 30% more for climate work with no decided law yet: industrial heat, thermal storage, cement and steel, aviation fuels and hydrogen priorities. The total is divided by a typical year's spending in 2021-2025; 2021 and 2025 are our estimates, as their accounts aren't published.

The next dollar is valued at 24% of that average. Future Cleantech Architects held about two years of spending in reserve at the end of 2024 and raised about twice its spending in 2025, so money isn't what limits it. Its next dollar goes to files that have no decided law yet.

Each tonne counts for its heat deaths and for the smoke of the power it displaces. In Europe that power is mostly gas, with a fifth coal (Markandya and Wilkinson's deaths per unit of electricity). The later tonnes displace more coal (two-fifths), so their smoke is higher. The act's tonnes are methane, which warms sooner and forms ozone.

### What drives the result

The 5% target gives most of the result: more than half from the later learning and under a third from the capacity it causes. The research consortia give about 11% and the delegated act about 2%. The shared value judgments move the result most: the years a won reform counts, the deaths per tonne and the discount rate. Of the model's own judgments, these matter most: how much of the target's capacity is there because of it, Future Cleantech Architects' share of the advocacy, and how much fossil power that capacity displaces.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether the target adds capacity at all.** It is indicative and loosely defined, and its capacity counts toward the same national renewables targets. The share of reported capacity credited to it runs from 4% to 30%.
- **Learning.** No study measures how much cheaper this target makes these technologies. The learning rate, the deployment response and the later growth are general figures.
- **The emissions trading cap.** How many of the freed allowances are cancelled depends on the reserve's rules in the 2030s and on whether lawmakers tighten the cap. The shared figure runs from 5% to 60%.
- **Credit.** Future Cleantech Architects' own reports say the target was adopted "with support from" it "and other partners"; its share is our judgment.

### What isn't modelled

The extra cost of the newest technologies isn't counted. Floating offshore wind, ocean energy and new geothermal cost more per MWh than the solar and wind they replace, and consumers or taxpayers pay the difference. The site counts a won rule's costs only from a regulator's impact analysis (method, section 2.21). The target began as a Parliament amendment that no analysis costed, so there is no figure to use. A cost would lower the result, and could outweigh the tonnes if the later savings from learning are small.

Climate work without a decided law in the period is counted only through the 30% for other work. The delegated act's compliance costs aren't counted either: no impact assessment gives a figure. Climate damage other than deaths is left at the shared zero. The 2025 spending is a judgment until those accounts are filed.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits are in audits/future-cleantech-architects-charityevals/. Round 1 found the research consortia missing; they are now a branch. It also started the period in 2021, when the work on the directive began. It split the target's adjustment into one question for what is reported and one for what the target caused, and derived the learning from the learning-curve literature. Round 2 added the EU emissions trading cap, a new shared figure that takes back most of the target's direct cut. It also derived the smoke of the displaced power from one coal share, and discounted the consortia's gains at the shared rate. Round 3 re-anchored the technologies' growth on floating-wind outlooks and gave the later tonnes their own coal share. Round 4 checked those changes and passed; a few explainers were then tightened and checked once more (round 5).
