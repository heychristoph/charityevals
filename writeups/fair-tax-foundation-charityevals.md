### Summary

The Fair Tax Foundation runs the Fair Tax Mark, a certification for businesses that pay their tax responsibly. Our model counts its work since 2014 against three outcomes: tax that certified businesses pay and would otherwise avoid, the same for the multinationals on its benchmarking index, and a small chance that the UK makes multinationals publish their tax country by country. It comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="fair-tax-foundation-charityevals">0.07</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, a little above the site's global-health median. The figure is very uncertain: nobody has measured how much tax the mark changes.

### What the charity does

Fair Tax Foundation Limited is a not-for-profit community benefit society in Manchester, registered with the Financial Conduct Authority (no. 32308R), with an asset lock. It is not a registered charity, so gifts get no Gift Aid. It does take donations, but these came to only GBP 148 in 2025. Fees from the businesses it certifies made up 90% of its income, and grants the rest. It spent GBP 451,698 in 2025 with 7.6 staff, and paid GBP 36,014 in interest to the members who hold its shares. Its accounts are exempt from audit; an independent reporting accountant checks them.

The Fair Tax Mark has certified 275 businesses since 2014. They pay GBP 4.1 billion a year in corporate income tax. They range from co-operatives and family firms to SSE, Mitie, Iberdrola and Repsol. To be certified, a business publishes a tax policy that rules out avoidance and the artificial use of tax havens, reports openly on its tax (country by country if it is a multinational), and is reassessed every year. Since 2024 the society has also run the Tax Responsibility and Transparency Index with CSR Europe. The index has benchmarked 21 multinationals that pay over EUR 30 billion a year. The society also runs Fair Tax Week and the Councils for Fair Tax Declaration (63 councils), writes reports on avoidance, and presses the UK to require public country-by-country reporting.

### How the impact happens

A business that wants the mark may drop tax arrangements it would otherwise keep, and pays more tax where it really works. That money is public revenue. In rich countries, where most of it is paid, a dollar of revenue spread across everyone does little per dollar. The part paid in middle-income countries, where the big certified multinationals also work, does about seven times more. The people who pay the tax are mostly richer shareholders, so it costs them less than it is worth to others.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** an average year of spending since 2014, in 2025 prices, with the interest on members' shares. It is built from the accounts for 2022 to 2025 and the grant disclosures for 2017 to 2021 (income stands in for spending there). The years 2014 to 2016 are judged at GBP 90,000 a year. That comes to three fifths of 2025's cost.
- **Certification (about 72% of the result):**
  - certified businesses pay GBP 4.1 billion a year. That figure is raised by a tenth to cover the few firms that have dropped out;
  - we judge such firms would avoid 3% of their tax without the mark, below HMRC's 4.3% gap for large UK businesses and below the roughly 10% that profit shifting costs worldwide, because firms that seek the mark are already careful taxpayers. Under strong public pressure, FTSE 100 firms named by a campaign gave up about all of their avoidance against their peers (Dyreng, Hoopes and Wilde). These firms chose the mark and many changed little, so we judge it has 17% of that effect: it removes about 15% of the avoidance;
  - 13% of the tax open to avoidance is paid in upper-middle income countries. Repsol's own report puts 45% of its income tax there, but most of it is Libya's and Algeria's oil tax, paid at the full rate, which the mark can't change; the rest is in Peru, Indonesia, Colombia and Brazil. We judge a quarter of Iberdrola's is in Brazil and Mexico. The rest is in rich countries. Each dollar is valued as public money spread over everyone's consumption there, less what it costs the payers. We judge the payers consume four times the UK average, and we add the shared extra cost of raising public money;
  - each win counts for the years a won reform counts on the site, less 3% of firms dropping out each year;
  - the society gets half the credit; the firms' own staff and the wider campaign do the rest. Trust in the chain is 65%;
  - side effects: firms that copy the standard without applying add a fifth. Revenue lost by low-tax countries takes away 5%, and avoidance the mark shields from scrutiny another 5%.
- **Benchmarking (about 26%):** the same chain for the indexed multinationals' EUR 30 billion. A quarter of it is already counted under certification. The index has 15% of certification's effect, since it has no pass mark: 2% of the avoidance. 15% of the tax is paid in upper-middle income countries. The society gets 40% of the credit, as it shares the index with CSR Europe, and trust is 50%.
- **UK public reporting (1%):** the law would raise 1% of large UK businesses' GBP 49.7 billion of corporation tax (HMRC). The chance it passes is half the site's shared 40% for a contested policy issue, as no UK government has used the power it has had since 2016. Effect comes after about four years. The society has 4% of the campaign's resources and civil society 30% of the credit. 40% of its part of the work is done. Side effects: its other research and media work adds 30%, and the shared 5% for backlash is taken off.
- **All three branches** are spread over the 12 years of work and discounted by the shared missing-data slider.
- **The next dollar** is valued at 40% of the average. Fees pay for certification, so a gift funds the research and advocacy, which do less per dollar here.

### What drives the result

The missing-data slider, the share of tax firms would otherwise avoid, the years a won reform counts, the next dollar's value and the share of avoidance the mark removes move the result most. The index's effect and the society's share of the credit come next.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **What the mark changes:** there is no study of certified firms' tax before and after certification. Many firms say they changed little. Our 15% could be a fifth or nearly three times that.
- **Where the tax is paid:** the society publishes no country split, and Iberdrola's is our judgment. Revenue in Brazil, Peru or Mexico counts about seven times as much per dollar as revenue in the UK, so this split matters: it is half the result.
- **The index:** it is two years old, and whether benchmarking changes any firm's tax is unknown.

### How this model was checked

Audit rounds are listed in audits/fair-tax-foundation-charityevals/. Round 1 found one high and three medium issues. The share of tax paid in upper-middle income countries now comes from Repsol's own country-by-country report (10% to 22%). Lapsed certifications are read from the counts of businesses, not the fall in the tax figure. The mark shielding avoidance is its own side effect. The mark's effect is anchored on a study of public pressure on FTSE 100 firms, and the index's is a share of it. Interest on members' shares is in the cost, and the UK base is HMRC's published liability. Together these took the result from 0.06 to 0.08. Round 2 passed with two medium findings, both fixed: Libya's and Algeria's oil tax, which the mark can't change, is left out of the upper-middle income share (22% to 13%), and the mark's effect is now an explicit share of what the pressure study found. That took the result to 0.07, checked in a verification round.
