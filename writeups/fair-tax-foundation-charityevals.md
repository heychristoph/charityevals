### Summary

The Fair Tax Foundation runs the Fair Tax Mark, a certification for businesses that pay their tax responsibly. Our model counts its work since 2014 against three outcomes: tax that certified businesses pay and would otherwise avoid, the same for the multinationals on its benchmarking index, and a small chance that the UK makes multinationals publish their tax country by country. It comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="fair-tax-foundation-charityevals">0.06</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, about the site's global-health median. The figure is very uncertain: nobody has measured how much tax the mark changes.

### What the charity does

Fair Tax Foundation Limited is a not-for-profit community benefit society in Manchester, registered with the Financial Conduct Authority (no. 32308R), with an asset lock. It is not a registered charity, so gifts get no Gift Aid. It does take donations, but these came to only GBP 148 in 2025. Fees from the businesses it certifies made up 90% of its income, and grants the rest. It spent GBP 451,698 in 2025 with 7.6 staff. Its accounts are exempt from audit; an independent reporting accountant checks them.

The Fair Tax Mark has certified 275 businesses since 2014. They pay GBP 4.1 billion a year in corporate income tax. They range from co-operatives and family firms to SSE, Mitie, Iberdrola and Repsol. To be certified, a business publishes a tax policy that rules out avoidance and the artificial use of tax havens, reports openly on its tax (country by country if it is a multinational), and is reassessed every year. Since 2024 the society has also run the Tax Responsibility and Transparency Index with CSR Europe. The index has benchmarked 21 multinationals that pay over EUR 30 billion a year. The society also runs Fair Tax Week and the Councils for Fair Tax Declaration (63 councils), writes reports on avoidance, and presses the UK to require public country-by-country reporting.

### How the impact happens

A business that wants the mark may drop tax arrangements it would otherwise keep, and pays more tax where it really works. That money is public revenue. In rich countries, where most of it is paid, a dollar of revenue spread across everyone does little per dollar. The part paid in middle-income countries does more. The people who pay the tax are mostly richer shareholders, so it costs them less than it is worth to others.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** an average year of spending since 2014, in 2025 prices. It is built from the accounts for 2022 to 2025 and the grant disclosures for 2017 to 2021 (income stands in for spending there). The years 2014 to 2016 are judged at GBP 90,000 a year. That comes to two thirds of 2025's spending.
- **Certification (about three quarters of the result):**
  - certified businesses pay GBP 4.1 billion a year. That figure is raised by a third to cover firms that have dropped out: the 2024 figure was GBP 5.3 billion;
  - we judge such firms would avoid 3% of their tax without the mark, below HMRC's 4.3% gap for large UK businesses and below the roughly 10% that profit shifting costs worldwide, because firms that seek the mark are already careful taxpayers. We judge the mark removes 15% of that avoidance;
  - 10% of the tax is judged to be paid in upper-middle income countries such as Brazil and Mexico, the rest in rich countries. Each dollar is valued as public money spread over everyone's consumption there, less what it costs the payers. We judge the payers consume four times the UK average, and we add the shared extra cost of raising public money;
  - each win counts for the years a won reform counts on the site, less 6% of firms dropping out each year;
  - the society gets half the credit; the firms' own staff and the wider campaign do the rest. Trust in the chain is 60%;
  - side effects: firms that copy the standard without applying add a fifth, and revenue lost by low-tax countries takes away 5%.
- **Benchmarking (about a quarter):** the same chain for the indexed multinationals' EUR 30 billion. A quarter of it is already counted under certification. The index removes 2% of the avoidance, since it has no pass mark. 15% of the tax is paid in upper-middle income countries. The society gets 40% of the credit, as it shares the index with CSR Europe, and trust is 50%.
- **UK public reporting (2%):** the law would raise 1% of large UK businesses' GBP 53 billion of corporation tax (from HMRC's tax gap figures). The chance it passes is half the site's shared 40% for a contested policy issue, as no UK government has used the power it has had since 2016. Effect comes after about four years. The society has 4% of the campaign's resources and civil society 30% of the credit. 40% of its part of the work is done. Side effects: its other research and media work adds 30%, and the shared 5% for backlash is taken off.
- **All three branches** are spread over the 12 years of work and discounted by the shared missing-data slider.
- **The next dollar** is valued at 40% of the average. Fees pay for certification, so a gift funds the research and advocacy, which do less per dollar here.

### What drives the result

The missing-data slider, the share of tax firms would otherwise avoid, the years a won reform counts, the next dollar's value and the share of avoidance the mark removes move the result most. The index's effect and the society's share of the credit come next.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **What the mark changes:** there is no study of certified firms' tax before and after certification. Many firms say they changed little. Our 15% could be a fifth or nearly three times that.
- **Where the tax is paid:** no country split is published. Revenue in Brazil or Mexico counts about seven times as much per dollar as revenue in the UK, so this split matters.
- **The index:** it is two years old, and whether benchmarking changes any firm's tax is unknown.
