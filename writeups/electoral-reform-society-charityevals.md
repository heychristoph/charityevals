### Summary

The Electoral Reform Society campaigns for proportional representation for the House of Commons and for other changes to how the UK votes and is governed. Our model of its work comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="electoral-reform-society-charityevals">0.05</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. It is a very uncertain figure: whether a voting method changes how well people are governed, and the chance that the UK adopts proportional representation, are both our judgments.

### What the charity does

The Society was founded in 1884. It has offices in London, Edinburgh (ERS Scotland) and Cardiff (ERS Cymru), 18 staff and about 7,000 members. In the year to March 2025 it spent £2.4 million ($3.0 million), £1.6 million of it on campaign staff and campaigns. It publishes research on elections, briefs MPs and peers, runs petitions and convenes coalitions such as Democracy Group Cymru.

It is a company limited by guarantee owned by its members, not a registered charity. Gifts to it get no Gift Aid or tax relief. It is the only entity that takes donations, so it is what this models. Most of its spending is drawn from a £43 million capital fund, built from the 2018 sale of its former ballot-services business.

### How the impact happens

The Society's main aim is proportional representation for Westminster, which hasn't been won. In the last decade it helped win smaller reforms:

- the supplementary vote restored for mayors (2026);
- the 92 hereditary peers removed from the Lords (2026);
- votes at 16 for all UK elections and pilots of automated voter registration (the bill is in the Lords);
- votes at 16 and for foreign nationals in Wales, and for foreign nationals in Scotland (2020);
- a larger Senedd (2024), though it opposed the closed lists that came with it.

In every case the government or the Senedd made the change, with the Society as one campaigner among several.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** the Annual Review's figures for 2024-25 (the audited accounts are filed without the income and expenditure account). Governance, operations and investment management are spread over the programme. A year's cost is the decade's average, 86% of 2024-25's in real terms, from the five years with published spending and a judgment for the other five.
- **The past record:** a dollar does what the average year of the Society's work from April 2016 to March 2026 did. Each reform won counts the Society's share of it, spread over those ten years.
- **Proportional representation (about half of the result), a campaign still pending:**
  - an 8% chance (2% to 24%) that the Commons is elected proportionally within ten years, for all 69 million people in the UK: a fifth of the 40% rate at which contested issues change policy;
  - the Commons counted at 45% of a full switch of all a person's governments;
  - the gain: a judged 5% of the income becoming a democracy brings, at 0.6 for doubt;
  - the Society's half of the movement's money, spread over the campaign's years so far and until the win, about 15.
- **The supplementary vote (about a fifth):** the 38 million people in England who will elect a mayor from 2028 (police and crime commissioners are being abolished). A mayor counts as 15% of a full switch. The gain is 0.6 of ranked-choice voting's judged 2%, with the Society's share 20%.
- **Votes at 16, registration and finance rules, the hereditary peers, the 2020 franchise acts and a larger Senedd (about a third together):** each is a judged 0.05% to 0.3% of democracy's gain, with the Society's share 10% to 15%. The closed lists that came with the larger Senedd aren't credited to the Society, which opposed them.
- **Years:** the gain builds over about 25 years. A win counts as brought forward by the site's five years, with a 3% yearly chance of repeal.
- **Every branch** is discounted by the missing-data slider.
- **Side effects:** the good of fairer elections beyond income (+20%) and the counter-measures reforms provoke (−10%), shared with the US voting-reform models. In 2022, for example, a new law switched mayors to first past the post.
- **Next dollar:** 21% of the average. The capital fund holds about 18 years of spending, so a gift mostly lets the fund grow.

### What drives the result

1. The years a won reform counts (1 to 20).
2. The missing-data slider.
3. The next dollar's share of the average (8.5% to 47%).
4. Democracy's long-run income gain (7% to 35%).
5. Proportional representation's share of democracy's income gain (1% to 15%).
6. Proportional representation's record against other contested issues (0.05 to 0.6 of their rate).

### Biggest uncertainties

- No study measures how much a voting method changes how well a country is governed. The shares of democracy's gain are judgments.
- The chance of proportional representation is a judgment. The two largest parties gain from first past the post, and the 2011 referendum on the alternative vote was lost two to one.
- The Society's shares of the wins are judgments. Governments made each change for their own reasons.

### What isn't modelled

- Reforms the Society opposed that passed, such as voter ID in 2022, except through the backlash side effect.
- Scotland's campaigns for local democracy and citizens' assemblies: no reform has been won yet.
- Any value per petition signed or report published: these count only through the reforms won.

### How this model was checked

Built from the template and audited by independent reviewers (rounds in audits/electoral-reform-society-charityevals/). Round 1 built the decade's spending from the published years and the shared price index, limited the supplementary vote to England's mayors (commissioners are being abolished), lowered the next dollar to 21% for the size of the capital fund, added the registration and finance reforms, took the chance of proportional representation from the shared rate for contested issues, and credited the Society only with the part of the Senedd reform it backed. The result fell from 0.065 to 0.049. Round 2 passed; its notes on wording and sources were fixed and checked in a last round.
