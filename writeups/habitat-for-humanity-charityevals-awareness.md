### Summary

Habitat for Humanity International (HFHI) spent $24.3 million in the year to June 2025 on public awareness and education: Habitat's media, website, social media and learning events. Its housing-policy advocacy, partly booked in its other two programmes (about $3.5 million by our estimate, taken out of those programmes' models), is counted here with it. In the US the Habitat network counts 142 policy wins in the year and $6.65 billion of state and local money for home affordability attached to them; worldwide, the Home Equals campaign counts $3.7 billion of government money for informal settlements and 23.7 million people since it began in May 2023. Our estimate of a dollar given to HFHI for this work is **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals-awareness">0.006</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**.

Three things set the result. Public money is not free: it comes from taxpayers, so only its move from those who pay to poorer households counts, on the site's log-income scale with both on the household-survey scale (docs/normalization.md 2.14); after programme costs, the in-kind form of housing and the cost of raising taxes, that move loses a little in the US and gains a little in the Home Equals countries. Research on advocacy finds most campaigns change nothing. And HFHI is one voice among many in the coalitions behind each win. Habitat measures none of this itself, so both branches take the site's shared discount for missing data.

### What the charity does

The Cost of Home campaign (FY2020–24) organised Habitat affiliates, homeowners and volunteers to push state and local governments for down-payment help, housing trust funds, repair programmes and land-use reforms; HFHI's campaign raised $6.8 million, Habitat organisations put in $23.7 million, and HFHI gave more than 240 grants to affiliates. It continues as Habitat Voices in Action. Internationally, Home Equals asks governments to upgrade informal settlements and secure residents' tenure. The rest of the programme is Habitat's communications: media, habitat.org, 2.38 million social-media followers and learning events.

### How the impact happens

1. Habitat organisations and their partners push for a housing policy; some of the time the push decides whether it passes, and when.
2. The money the policy carries reaches lower-income households as down-payment help, repairs, cheaper homes or settlement upgrades, enjoyed over the years the housing lasts.
3. The money comes from taxpayers or other public spending, so the gain is the move from the payers to poorer households, less the cost of raising it.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's template for policy work, with two branches:

- **US housing advocacy.** Habitat's count of the money its network's wins carried, the average of the two years of Habitat Voices in Action ($3.5 billion; $6.65 billion in FY2025, $369 million in FY2026), and of the people it reaches (about $4,400 a person). From that is taken what the same dollars cost those who pay, at the household survey's $32,723 a person: state and local taxes are roughly proportional to consumption (ITEP's Who Pays), and raising public money costs about 1.2 times the money (Dahlby 2008). The two nearly cancel. A fifth of the money is a lasting stream, counted for the site's years a won reform counts; the rest once.
- **Home Equals.** The same chain, country by country over 13 Habitat countries (each region a quarter, since Home Equals publishes no split), for $1.2 billion a year at about $156 a person (Habitat's totals to its Year 3 report), with 70% reaching households (about a quarter of village road spending went missing in Indonesia: Olken, 2007), plus the shared shares for spillovers and child deaths averted that the international model uses. Informal-settlement households consume half their country's survey mean (the household survey's scale, the site's rule for consumption baselines); those who pay are on the same survey scale, taxed by consumption taxes that are mildly progressive because the poor buy informally (Bachas, Gadenne and Jensen, 2024), with a cost of raising public money of 1.2 (Auriol and Warlters, 2012, for African countries).
- **What the advocacy caused.** From the research: of 98 random US lobbying campaigns, about 60% changed nothing (Baumgartner and colleagues, 2009), and in field experiments organised constituent contact raised a legislator's support for a bill by about 12 percentage points (Bergan and Cole, 2015), while lobbyists' information alone moved none (Camp and colleagues, 2023). So 15% of the US money (10% for Home Equals) would not have come then without the coalitions' advocacy.
- **HFHI's share.** Habitat organisations are about 15% of the coalitions' resources in the US (20% in Home Equals), and HFHI about a fifth of the Habitat network's (two fifths for Home Equals); and Habitat's own counts are taken at 60% (50%) for double counting.

The general awareness work has no outcome of its own beyond the advocacy: its effect on giving and volunteering is fundraising, so its cost is counted and nothing is added for it.

### What drives the result

- **How much of the Home Equals money reaches households**, **how much poorer they are than those who pay**, and **what the housing is worth to them against cash**: across these ranges the result runs from a net harm to several times the estimate.
- **How much we count a programme whose results aren't measured**, and **how much the next dollar does against the average one** (site-wide sliders).
- **How much of the money the advocacy caused**, and **Habitat's share of the coalitions**.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Habitat's money and people counts are its network's self-reports, with no published method, and swing eighteenfold from one year to the next.
- How much any one group's advocacy caused is barely measured anywhere; the research gives a range, not a figure for Habitat.
- Who pays for the money: new taxes on the better-off would make the US branch worth something; money taken from other programmes for poor households would make it a loss.

### What isn't modelled

As a share of what households gain: wins that carry no money (zoning and land-use reforms, which in US cities add about 0.8% to the housing stock within a decade, mostly at the upper end of rents: Stacy and colleagues, 2023; land-tenure rules abroad), any change in public attitudes from the awareness work, and illness averted by the water, sanitation and drainage works abroad add a few percent of what households gain, which is most of the result because the gain and the cost nearly cancel.

### How this model was checked

Independent reviews, each by a fresh reviewer working from the model alone: three rounds at the first build, two verification rounds after the 4 November 2026 move to survey-scale consumption, and an audit on 15 November 2026. That audit added the spillovers and child deaths averted that the international model counts for the same housing, moved Home Equals to its Year 3 totals (from the 2023 launch), anchored the share of the money reaching households, and took donated advertising out of the cost; the result went from a small net harm to a small net good.
