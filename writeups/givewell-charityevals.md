### Summary

GiveWell researches global health and poverty programmes and directs donors' money to the most cost-effective ones it finds. This model is of a gift for GiveWell's own work, its research, grantmaking and outreach, not of the money that passes through it to the charities it funds. It comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. GiveWell's operations are not short of money, so a further dollar for them mostly frees another donor's dollar for grants and is worth about a dollar granted. The small rest adds to its work, where each dollar spent led to about eleven dollars of giving it caused.

### What the charity does

GiveWell (legally The Clear Fund) spent $183.9 million in 2024, by its audited accounts. Of that, $163.1 million was grants: donors' money passed on to the charities it funds. Those grants are the charities' cost and are counted in their own models, so they are taken out here. What is left, $20.7 million, is GiveWell's own spending: staff and contractors, research, outreach and administration.

In its 2024 metrics year (February 2024 to January 2025) GiveWell raised $415 million and directed $397 million. Three quarters went to its four Top Charities: the Against Malaria Foundation, Malaria Consortium's seasonal malaria chemoprevention, Helen Keller Intl's vitamin A supplementation and New Incentives. The rest went to about 30 other organisations, from PATH's malaria vaccines to tuberculosis contact tracing and malnutrition treatment. Its research influenced another $45 million that other funders gave on their own.

### How the impact happens

A gift to GiveWell's Unrestricted Fund pays for its operations. But GiveWell keeps two years of operating needs in unrestricted funds and designates anything above that for granting. In 2024 unrestricted gifts were $111 million, five times its operating costs. GiveWell takes the excess from its largest gifts, so most donors' gifts are spent on operations; but a further gift still raises the excess by as much. So a further dollar for operations mostly lets GiveWell grant out one more dollar of a large donor's gift. We count 95% of a further dollar (80% to 100%) as a dollar granted, worth what GiveWell's grants achieve. The other 5% adds to its research and outreach.

That work decides where hundreds of millions of dollars go. Without it, some of that money would still have reached good charities and some would not. The model counts only the giving GiveWell caused:

- **Open Philanthropy** (now Coefficient Giving) gave $108 million. It would have given this money to global health anyway, through its own grantmakers. GiveWell is credited with a quarter of it (10% to 50%), our judgment, for steering it better than Open Philanthropy would alone. All of it counts as shifted money, none as new.
- **Other donors** gave $307 million, less what their unrestricted gifts paid for GiveWell's operations. In GiveWell's last published donor survey (2015), giving worth 84% of the surveyed total changed because of GiveWell; 16% came from donors just starting to give. The donors who answered gave {{row:survey_response_share}} of what all surveyed donors gave. Counting the rest at half the respondents' influence, the site's shared rule for surveys, that is 59% (47% to 84%).
- **The $45 million its research influenced** is giving its funders already said GiveWell's research caused. It takes only the step for funders who didn't say so: 71%.

A dollar moved is worth what the charities that get it achieve. Each recipient is read live at GiveWell's figure for it on this site, under your values, weighted by what GiveWell directed to it in 2024. The 16 small grants to organisations with no model here ($4.9 million) are valued at the average of the rest. The influenced money is valued at the charities it went to, with equal weights. A dollar GiveWell grants is valued at the funds it directed alone.

Half of other donors' giving that GiveWell caused is counted as new money. The other half would have gone to other charities, so it counts only for the gain over where it would have gone. These are the site's shared figures for every organisation that moves money.

### How the model works

Money moved = (funds raised − what paid for operations) × the share GiveWell caused, by source, plus the influenced funding. That is about $231 million against $20.7 million of spending. Each dollar moved is valued at its recipients' live results, net of where shifted money would have gone. A 10% side effect is added, our judgment: research used by funders and governments who don't report it, less donor attention drawn to what can be measured. That average is multiplied by {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} for the next dollar of work, because its research is moving into less proven areas. A further dollar for operations is then 95% a dollar granted and 5% that work. Money moved is counted, not estimated, so no branch is discounted for missing data.

### What drives the result

- **How much of a further dollar ends up granted** (95%, from 80% to 100%). At 100% a dollar for GiveWell is worth exactly a dollar granted; at 80% the result nearly doubles.
- **The value of a life** and **the discount rate**, through the charities GiveWell funds.
- **The share of other donors' giving it caused** (59%, from 47% to 84%), from a 2015 survey of engaged donors.
- **Where shifted money would otherwise have gone** (one times GiveDirectly, from 0 to 3 times).

### Where we differ from GiveWell

GiveWell publishes no estimate of the cost-effectiveness of its own operations. It reports funds raised and directed, about 19 dollars directed per dollar spent in 2024.

- **Where a further dollar goes.** Funds directed per dollar spent is an average over operations GiveWell has already funded. We count most of a further dollar as granted, as GiveWell's excess-assets policy implies.
- **The share it caused.** Funds directed counts every dollar. We count only the giving GiveWell caused, and half of other donors' part as new money.
- **Shared credit.** With credit by share of resources (off by default), staff share it for pay they give up, and other donors for their new money.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether a dollar for its operations adds research, or only frees another donor's unrestricted dollar for grants.
- How much of its donors' giving would have reached equally good charities without it. The only survey is old and comes from engaged donors.
- The value of the newer programmes it funds, many of them pilots, research or scoping grants read at GiveWell's figure for the organisation.

### What isn't modelled

- Money GiveWell raised in earlier years and granted in 2024, and the interest it earns while waiting ($10.6 million in 2024). The interest roughly offsets the wait before money is granted, so neither is counted.
- GiveWell's influence on how other evaluators and governments judge programmes, beyond the 10% side effect.

### How this model was checked

Built from GiveWell's audited 2024 accounts and its 2024 metrics report, with its grantees read live. An independent audit then checked every source against the documents. Its first round found that the model counted a further dollar for operations at a fifth of the operations' average, although GiveWell grants out what its operations can't use; the model now counts most of that dollar as a dollar granted, which lowered the result by about two fifths. It also took the share of donors' giving GiveWell caused from GiveWell's own survey rather than a judgment (the second round corrected how the survey's non-respondents count, and valued a freed dollar at GiveWell's own grants only), counted Open Philanthropy's money as shifted only, and took cost and the operations deducted from funds raised from one period. The rounds are kept in audits/givewell-charityevals/.
