### Summary

The Council on Strategic Risks is a Washington DC security-policy institute founded in 2017. It works against biological weapons and pandemics, against nuclear risks, and on the security risks of climate change. Our model of all its work in 2025 comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. About 69% of that comes from the fellowships, 8% of the cost. About 23% comes from the nuclear work, about a quarter of the cost, and 8% from the biological work, about a third of the cost. The plausible range runs from {{low}} to {{high}}.

### What the charity does

CSR spent $5.2 million in 2025 and had 23 employees. Its programmes cost $4.5 million:

- **Janne E. Nolan Center on Strategic Weapons: $2.8 million.** Work to end biological weapons threats and on US biodefense, including the Biodefense Budget Breakdown of federal spending. The Global Biosecurity Accelerator brings allied governments together, as at the Berlin Biosecurity Dialogue in June 2025, and publishes a Global Biosecurity Tracker. There is also work on nuclear risks and arms control, such as a report on the consequences of using tactical nuclear weapons and a record of every nuclear weapon the five recognised nuclear powers have deployed. The Center runs four fellowships: two for early-career fellows (on ending bioweapons and on nuclear risk), a biodefense bootcamp, and an international fellowship whose first 11 fellows met in 2025.
- **Center for Climate and Security: $1.5 million.** Analysis and dialogues for militaries and security agencies on climate risks, among them the International Military Council on Climate and Security.
- **Converging Risks Lab: $0.2 million.** Research and foresight on risks that cut across fields, including a project on climate, nuclear power and nuclear security.

### How the impact happens

Nuclear war and pandemics carry a yearly chance of catastrophe, taken from the shared forecasts. A typical project cuts that risk by a share per dollar, taken from Rethink Priorities' cross-cause model. The cut counts for the years such progress would otherwise have taken to come (5 by default), with each year discounted. Deaths count across the world's age groups, like every death on the site; a COVID-scale pandemic's deaths count at COVID's age mix. A catastrophe that ends humanity's future also takes the lives of everyone not yet born, counted only through the discount rate.

Fellows count through the work they add to the field: the share who go on to work on these risks because of the fellowship, valued at a typical dollar of that work. The fellowships are credited with that work in full, unless another programme trained the same people. The employers who pay the fellows are not part of the share: like any party that acts because of the charity, they come under the shared mobiliser setting.

The climate-security work aims to get militaries and governments to plan and spend for climate risks as their scale warrants. A dollar of it is taken to bear on about $500 of public money a year. The anchor is the US Defense Department's $5.1 billion request for climate-risk work in 2024, at most half of which is left after the 2025 cuts. Rich countries' public investment loses about {{row:gov_efficiency_gap}} of its value to inefficiency. The work is taken to close 0.3% of that gap in what it touches. The public value gained is valued at what a dollar is worth to the average American.

### How the model works

The return doesn't split the Nolan Center's spending by area. The fellowships are taken to cost about $350,000. Of the rest, 60% is put on biological threats. The anchor is Coefficient Giving's three-year grant for biosecurity fellowships, policy and operations, about $2.2 million a year from November 2024. Less the fellowships and a share for running the organisation, that leaves about 58% of the Center's other work. Half of the Converging Risks Lab counts as nuclear work and half as climate security. That puts about {{row:bio_cost_share}} of the cost on pandemics, {{row:nuclear_cost_share}} on nuclear risk, 8% on the fellowships and 36% on climate security. Each part has its own adjustments:

| Adjustment | Pandemics | Nuclear | Fellowships | Climate security |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How far the effect applies | 0.8 | 0.7 (the lab's project 0.4) | 1 | 0.7 |
| Work others would not have done now | 0.7 | 0.7 | 1 | 0.6 |
| CSR's share of the resources | 0.9 | 0.9 | 0.95 | 0.8 |

Every part is discounted 50% for missing data. The Converging Risks Lab's climate-nuclear project counts at 0.4 because foresight research is a step further from the risk. The climate-security figure allows for past gains in US defence planning being undone.

Side effects add 5%. Most of that is work against a single detonation or a limited nuclear war, which has no shared forecast. The rest is chemical weapons and the climate-security work's gains beyond better spending. No harm is added beyond a typical project's chance of backfiring.

CSR holds about two months of spending in unrestricted reserves, but most of its money is in multi-year grants for set purposes. The next dollar counts at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of the past average.

### What drives the result

After each part's adjustments, the fellowships bring 69% of the result, the nuclear work 23%, the biological work 8% and the climate-security work under 0.01%. The fellowships count for more than their 8% of the cost because the alumni's added work is valued at a typical dollar of the field's work, about ten dollars of it for each dollar the fellowships cost, and their adjustments are light. The result therefore rests on one share, the fellows who work on these risks because of the fellowship (10%), for which no alumni survey exists. A typical dollar of nuclear work cuts more deaths in Rethink's model than a typical dollar of pandemic work. The climate-security work counts for so little because it helps rich countries' public spending, not lives at risk in a catastrophe.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much a dollar of nuclear work cuts the risk.** Rethink's figures are placeholders, and the range runs ten times either way. From about 17 to 190.
- **How many years a cut counts before it would have come anyway.** The shared slider runs from 1 to 20 years.
- **How the Nolan Center's spending splits between biological and nuclear work.** From 45% to 80% biological, which moves the result from about 34 to 29.
- **How many fellows work on these risks because of the fellowships.** 10%, from 3% to 25%; no alumni survey is published. The result runs from about 17 to 65.
- **How much a dollar of pandemic work cuts the risk.** Also ten times either way.

### What isn't modelled

- Losses beyond deaths, such as survivors' lost income and health, are a shared figure set to 0.
- No treaty, law or standard won in 2025 is credited to CSR, so all the work is valued as typical field work.

### How this model was checked

The model was built from the catastrophic-risk template. Round 1 of the independent audit checked every source and found the figures right. Its one high finding was that the years a fellow stays in the work had been typed into many models. That figure is now one shared figure that every catastrophic-risk model reads, with the same value. Its smaller findings were fixed too. The Nolan Center's biological share was set from the grant's arithmetic (65% to 60%). The lab's climate-nuclear project got its own, lower multiplier. The climate-security budgets are now those of 2025. Round 2 passed. Its smaller findings were fixed: the branch shares are now given after each branch's adjustments, and the switched-off policy branch values pandemic deaths at the same age mix as the biological work. A verification round checked those fixes. A later round moved the fellowships' credit: the employers who pay the fellows are no longer counted as co-funders, only under the shared mobiliser setting, which raised the result and made the fellowships its largest part. The rounds are in audits/council-on-strategic-risks-charityevals/.
