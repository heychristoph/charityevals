### Summary

Evergreen Collaborative researches and drafts US federal and state clean-energy policy and makes the public case for it ($8.6M of expenses in 2024). Counting the wins of 2021-2024 that have published tonnes against the same years' spending, a dollar given today does about **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="evergreen-collaborative-charityevals">0.0026</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, through the greenhouse gas and air pollution those policies avert.

### What the charity does

Evergreen grew out of Washington governor Jay Inslee's 2020 presidential campaign and its climate plan. It is two organisations: Evergreen Collaborative, a 501(c)(3) formed in 2021 that does research, policy papers, communications and outreach (gifts to it are tax-deductible), and Evergreen Action, a 501(c)(4) that lobbies and campaigns (gifts to it are not). All staff are on the Collaborative's payroll; the Action pays its share of them at cost. This model is of the Collaborative, the organisation a tax-deductible gift reaches. Its Form 990 reports one programme ($7.4M in 2024); spending grew from $1.2M in 2021 to $8.6M in 2024. Its work now centres on state policy (power bills and data centres in Michigan, New York, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin) and on defending past laws.

### How the impact happens

Evergreen's own account of its wins in the period: the Inflation Reduction Act (2022), many of whose clean-energy credits, grants and Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund it had proposed in its 2020 plan and pushed for in 2021-2022; Michigan's 2023 clean-energy laws (100% clean power by 2040), for which it made the economic and health case; EPA's 2024 power-plant and vehicle standards, for which it helped mobilise public comments; and smaller wins (a federal grant to decarbonise Pennsylvania's heavy industry, a federal freight-charging strategy, utilities leaving a trade association over its lobbying). Each law or rule cuts greenhouse gas; burning less coal, gas and oil near people also cuts air pollution. The federal law also won public money for US households through home rebates and credits.

### How the model works

Each win with published tonnes is a branch: its tonnes a year once in effect (REPEAT's projection for the federal law, RMI and Energy Innovation's Michigan simulator for the state laws, EPA's own estimates for the rules; shared figures every model counting the win reads) × the share of a won policy due to advocacy at all × the years a won reform counts, discounted with the wait until the tonnes come × what wouldn't have happened anyway × Evergreen Collaborative's share of all the advocates' resources × the share delivered since. Its shares are judgments: 0.4% of the advocacy behind the federal law, 2% behind Michigan's laws, 0.5% and 0.1% behind the power-plant and vehicle rules, small because hundreds of groups, unions and industry worked on each and part of Evergreen's own campaigning was its sister's. The period's wins are set against the Collaborative's average yearly spending in 2021-2024 (in 2024 prices). The household money is valued as the recipients' gain less the taxpayers' cost, at half the Collaborative's share of the law; the rules' compliance costs, from EPA's own analyses, are counted against them (the vehicle rules save buyers money). The next dollar is valued at Evergreen Collaborative's own figure, 37% (17% to 76%) of the period's average: its net assets are about nine months of spending, and the federal ground that gave the period its wins has turned.

### What drives the result

The federal law gives about four fifths of the value, Michigan's laws under a tenth, the household money a little under a tenth, the two EPA rules about 2%. The law's credits were cut back by the 2025 budget law (35% of its projected tonnes counted as delivered), the power-plant and vehicle standards have since been repealed (5% delivered), and Michigan's House voted in 2026 to repeal the state laws, a repeal expected to die in the Senate (55% delivered).

### Biggest uncertainties

The discount rate, the missing-data discount and the shared years a won reform counts move the result most, then the deaths per tonne, the shared advocacy share and the Collaborative's share of the work on the federal law (0.15% to 1.2%). No tonne is measured for its work, so every branch takes the missing-data discount. Its spending before 2021, when Evergreen's plan was written, is not in its accounts, and the model gives that work no credit.

### What isn't modelled

The Pennsylvania grant, the freight strategy, EPA's other power-sector rules, the utility campaign and other states' clean-power laws are a share of the modelled value (15%, 5% to 40%). Michigan's laws have no regulator's cost estimate, so their cost to ratepayers is not subtracted; sponsors claim bill savings. Evergreen Action's own spending is not counted: its lobbying is one of the other groups' resources behind each win.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits are in audits/evergreen-collaborative-charityevals/. Round 1 passed with one medium finding, fixed: the share for other wins is now tagged as a side effect, so the side-effect weight moves it. Its lows were fixed too: Michigan's tonnes were cut from 22 to 15 million a year (8 to 30 million) after a check against the state's whole power sector (about 50 million tonnes, its coal exits already planned), which lowered the result about 4%; quotes, the grants to Michigan groups and the repeal's status in the Senate were corrected; and the Michigan branch now says why it has no compliance-cost row. A scoped verification round checked these changes.
