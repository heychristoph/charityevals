### Summary

One for the World asks students and young professionals to take the 1% Pledge: to give 1% of their income, now or after they graduate, to charities GiveWell recommends. It works through volunteer chapters at universities and graduate schools and through talks at companies, in the US, UK, Canada and Australia. Our model of its year to June 2024 comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="one-for-the-world-charityevals">6.4</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. Each dollar it spends leads to a little under half a dollar of discounted giving it caused, almost all of it to top global-health charities. The figure moves most with the discount rate, how much the next dollar does compared with the past average, its own count of the giving it caused, and how this year's new pledges compare with earlier years'.

### What the charity does

One for the World works through two non-profits run by the same board: One for the World, Inc. in New York (four staff) and One For The World, a charity registered in England and Wales. In the year to 30 June 2024 the US company spent $1,442,179 (audited). Of that, $684,439 was donors' money passed on to charities, and $40,148 was legal work given free. The UK charity spent £71,630, of which £39,928 was passed on (its accounts are independently examined). Its own costs, both entities together, come to about $758,000.

Its work is one programme of effective giving:

- **Chapters.** 36 active university chapters, now with a focus on graduate schools, above all MBA programmes, whose pledgers give more. They brought 166 pledges in 2023–2024.
- **Corporate talks.** Talks and corporate chapters at companies such as Accenture, with workplace giving through Benevity.
- **The donation platform and donor care.** Recurring gifts to its portfolio: Malaria Consortium, Against Malaria Foundation, Helen Keller Intl, New Incentives and GiveWell's All Grants Fund.

### How the impact happens

Its donors gave about $1.55 million in the year, $1.30 million of it to charities GiveWell recommends. The model counts only the giving One for the World caused. Its own count says 44%: $575,956 of the $1.30 million. It asks donors at checkout whether they knew GiveWell before, and it judged its largest and corporate donors one by one. It calls this a measure of what it is confident it caused, below its true effect. We take 44%, from the site's 30% for an organisation without a survey to the 61% its own target implied. A 7% allowance is added for giving it doesn't record. It says it misses "six figures" a year of off-platform giving.

Most of the year's giving comes from people who pledged in earlier years. Pledges are a flow on this site: a year's spending earns the lifetime giving of the pledges it brings, not the giving of earlier pledgers. So the recurring part (80%, our judgment) is converted:

- In a steady state, a year's recurring giving equals one year's pledges' lifetime giving, undiscounted.
- Their discounted value is about 56% of that, on the site's shared pledge curve (Giving What We Can's cohorts) over 38 years of giving.
- The first gift waits about a year, as students give only after they graduate.
- The 166 pledges of 2023–2024 were about half the yearly average before, though newer pledges give more. We take 65% of an average year's (35% to 100%).

One-off gifts (20%) count as they are. Together this comes to about $350,000 of giving it caused, discounted, against $758,000 of costs.

A dollar moved is worth what its recipients achieve, read live under your values at their figures on the rankings. 84% went to charities GiveWell recommends. They are split as the donations through its own books were: $505,604 to GiveWell (and through Every.org) for its recommended charities, valued at GiveWell's top charities, and $162,953 to Against Malaria Foundation. The other 16% went to charities outside GiveWell's list, mostly GiveDirectly, and is valued at GiveDirectly's figure. Half the giving it caused counts as new money. The other half would have gone to other charities anyway, so it counts only for the gain over where it would have gone. Both halves use the site's shared figures for every organisation that moves money. A 5% side effect is added (our judgment). Pledgers and chapter leaders may go on to give more, or to work in effective giving. On the other side, a 1% norm may anchor some who would have given more.

### How the model works

Value = giving caused (pledges' discounted lifetime giving and one-off gifts) × the net value of a dollar moved × (1 + side effects), over its own costs. Then × how much the next dollar does compared with the past average: 57%, from 29% to 95%. Money is its constraint. Its unrestricted reserves fell to about four months of its costs by June 2025, it projects a $160,000 deficit for 2025–2026, and Open Philanthropy no longer funds its operations. But the next dollar goes to a new growth strategy with mixed early results, at an organisation that missed most of its own targets in 2022–2024. Money moved is counted, not estimated, so no missing-data discount applies.

### What drives the result

- **The discount rate.** Pledgers give for decades.
- **The next dollar** (57%, from 29% to 95%).
- **The share it caused** (44%, from 30% to 61%).
- **This year's pledges against earlier years'** (65%, from 35% to 100%), and **the share of recurring giving** (80%, from 60% to 95%).
- **The value of a young child's life**, through its destinations.

### Where we differ from One for the World's own figures

It reports money moved and the part it caused in the year, with no ratio to costs and no valuation beyond deaths averted.

- **A year's giving against a year's cost.** It counts the year's giving, most of it from earlier pledges. We credit the year with its own pledges' discounted lifetime giving.
- **What a dollar moved is worth.** It counts deaths averted at its charities. We value each dollar at its recipients' figures on the rankings, net of where shifted money would have gone.
- **The share it caused.** We use its own 44%, with a range above it, because it calls the figure a floor.

### Biggest uncertainties

- The share of its donors' giving it caused. It rests on whether donors knew GiveWell, which it doubts is the right test.
- How the 2023–2024 pledges compare with earlier years', and how long its 1% pledgers keep giving. Its own retention looks like the shared curve in the early years; nobody has measured its pledgers over decades.
- Whether its total money moved includes the UK charity's £159,000. We take it as included.

### What isn't modelled

- The UK charity's money held for later grants. It is counted when given, without its wait.
- Effects of the chapters on the volunteers who run them, beyond the 5% side effect.
