### Summary

World Central Kitchen, founded by the chef José Andrés, cooks and delivers hot meals to people hit by disasters and wars. Our model of its 2025 relief work, 372.5 million dollars and 130 to 179 million meals, gives **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="world-central-kitchen-charityevals">0.12</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, a little above the median of the site's global-health charities. Over 70% of it comes from Gaza, where the meals fed about a million people through a year of blockade and famine.

### What the charity does

World Central Kitchen has one programme: emergency relief. Within days of a disaster it sets up field kitchens, pays local restaurants and food trucks to cook, and delivers meals; in long crises it runs community kitchens with local cooks. In 2025 its audited accounts show 411.2 million dollars of expenses: 372.5 million on relief, 263.7 million of it food, and 38.6 million on management and fundraising. Its Form 990 splits the spending abroad by region:

- **The Middle East** (200 million dollars, about half): Gaza, with some work in Lebanon and Israel. By February 2026 it had served 272 million meals in Gaza since the war began, reaching a million a day.
- **The Caribbean and Central America** (55 million): kitchens for families displaced by Haiti's gang violence, school meals there, and Hurricane Melissa in Jamaica.
- **Europe** (48 million, much of it a grant to its Spanish affiliate), **Ukraine** (24 million), **East Asia** (12 million: the Myanmar earthquake, the Philippines) and smaller responses in Mexico, Argentina and South Asia.
- **The US** (about 40 million): the Los Angeles fires, the Texas floods, the aftermath of Hurricane Helene and others.

### How the impact happens

A meal is food that people in a crisis would otherwise go without or pay for out of almost nothing. Where hunger is severe, as in Gaza in 2025, it also keeps some people alive: part of the war's deaths from causes other than violence came from hunger and the illness it brings.

### How the model works

The model has three branches, split by the Form 990's regions: Gaza and the Middle East, crises in poorer countries (Haiti, Jamaica, Ukraine, Myanmar and smaller places), and disasters in rich countries (the US and Europe). In each:

- **Meals and people.** The year's meals are divided between the branches by the charity's dated Gaza counts (about 70% in Gaza) and by each branch's spending and cost per meal. Gaza's meals went to about a million people, about a third of a year each; elsewhere people got fewer meals.
- **Food as consumption.** Each branch's food is its spending times the food bill's share of programme spending (71%), so the food valued adds up to what the charity spent on food: about 140 dollars per person in Gaza. It counts as consumption against what people had without it, on the site's log-income scale, over the months they were fed. In Gaza people had very little: we take about 1.60 international dollars a day.
- **Deaths averted.** In Gaza, deaths from causes other than violence above the pre-war rate (the first independent mortality survey of the war found about 0.006 a person-year in its first 15 months; we take 0.010 for 2025, the year of the blockade and famine, against 0.003 before the war), of which a daily hot meal averts about 15% for the people it reaches. In the poorer countries' crises the excess is smaller and the meals avert less of it (5%). In rich-country disasters no deaths are counted.
- **Adjustments.** What other agencies would have delivered anyway (in Gaza about half, because access through the crossings, not money, limited food; in US disasters most), meals that didn't reach the people counted (convoys looted, spoilage), the charity's share of the resources (its volunteers and UN logistics are others'), for the deaths averted only the unaudited meal count they rest on (the food, valued from the food bill, takes no evidence step), the half year its money waits, and the shared discount for outcomes nobody measured.
- **Side effects.** Wages for local cooks and restaurants (+5%), the time, fuel and dignity of a cooked meal (+5%), and harms at crowded distributions or to local food sellers (−5%).

### What drives the result

The shared discount for unmeasured outcomes, then how much food in Gaza others would have brought in through the same crossings, and how little people there consumed without the meals. A day's food is a large gain to someone with almost nothing, so Gaza carries the result; the same meal in a US disaster adds almost nothing on the log scale and the rich-country branch is under 1% of the total. Deaths averted are a small part even in Gaza: fewer than one person in a thousand fed.

### Biggest uncertainties

- How many of the meals in Gaza other agencies would have delivered: food waited at the borders for most of 2025.
- What people in Gaza had to eat without the charity: output per person there fell to 161 dollars in 2024, and almost all food came from aid.
- How many meals there were: the accounts say more than 179 million, the Form 990 and the charity's review more than 130 million, and nobody checks the count independently. It changes the result little, since the food is valued from the food bill; it sets only how long each person was fed.
- How much of the war's excess mortality in 2025 came from hunger and how much a meal a day prevents: no survey covers 2025 yet.

### What isn't modelled

Lives lost to violence, which meals can't prevent. The charity's advocacy for access to Gaza and its media profile, which may draw other donors in or crowd them out. The Spanish affiliate's own activities beyond what its grant from the US entity shows.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits (audits/world-central-kitchen-charityevals/). Round 1 found that the food's log gain was spread over a whole year although people were fed for a third of a year or less, and that valuing every meal at the average food cost gave Gaza more food than its spending could buy; both were fixed (the food now counted over the months fed, and each branch's food from its own spending), lowering the result by a third. It also asked for anchors on the Gaza counterfactual and the 2025 death rate, now in the model. Round 2 passed with only low findings, all fixed: the regional shares taken over the consolidated programme (the Spanish affiliate at its own spending), the bread spread into the days fed, and clearer reasons. A verification round checked those changes. A later site-wide pass took the evidence step off the food (valued from the food bill; it stays on the deaths, which rest on the meal count) and set the charity's share of the resources to 95%, as for other relief goods, moving the result from 0.10 to 0.12; round 5 verified it with no high or medium finding, and a sixth round checked its one fix.
