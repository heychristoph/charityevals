### Summary

The Campaign Legal Center goes to court and to lawmakers for fair US elections: the freedom to vote, fair maps, campaign finance law and ethics in government. In 2025 it also fought the administration's executive order on elections. Our model of its 2025 work comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. It is a very uncertain figure, built mostly on judgment: how much the whole field's work lowers the chance of a democratic slide, and how much a reform within a democracy changes how well people are governed.

### What the charity does

The Campaign Legal Center is a nonpartisan legal organisation in Washington, founded in 2002 by Trevor Potter, a former chair of the Federal Election Commission. It has a 501(c)(4) affiliate, Campaign Legal Center Action, which keeps its own accounts; gifts to it aren't tax-deductible, and this model leaves it out. In 2025 the Campaign Legal Center spent $21.5 million, $16.0 million of it on programmes:

- voting rights, $4.7 million: in 2025 the first lawsuit against the executive order on elections (LULAC v. Executive Office of the President), cases against the Justice Department's use of states' voter files and against a rule barring voter registration at naturalization ceremonies, and Restore Your Vote;
- communications, $2.6 million;
- campaign finance, $2.4 million: suits against the Federal Election Commission and against violators directly, and briefs defending contribution limits;
- policy, $1.8 million: federal and state advocacy (in 2025, against the SAVE Act and two provisions of the tax and spending bill);
- redistricting, $1.8 million: in 2025 the Utah ruling that restored the voters' 2018 ban on partisan gerrymandering, the Supreme Court stay that kept North Dakota's tribes' fair map, and a suit against Missouri's new congressional map;
- other programmes, $1.8 million, which the accounts don't describe;
- ethics, $1.0 million: complaints against officials and the defence of Congress's ethics office.

These figures are from its audited statement of functional expenses for 2025.

### How the impact happens

The work does good in two ways. First, it helps defend US democracy against a slide toward a hybrid regime: suing over the executive order on elections and other overreach, protecting the certification of elections, and holding officials to account. Second, it wins reforms within the democracy: fair maps, voting rights, campaign finance rules and ethics laws.

### How the model works

The model has two branches. Each counts what the whole field or movement achieves in a year, takes the Campaign Legal Center's share of the money behind it, and values better government as income.

- **A slide averted (nearly half of the result).** This branch reads the same shared figures as our Brennan Center, Common Cause and Protect Democracy models. Forecasters give the US an 11% chance of losing its democracy rating before 2030, and a year of the whole pro-democracy field's work cuts that chance by about a twentieth. A slide would cost a share of democracy's long-run income gain, built up over 25 years. We judge that about a third of the Campaign Legal Center's spending is direct work against a slide: half of voting rights, 30% of ethics, 15% of redistricting, 5% of campaign finance and 60% of the other programmes, which we take to hold most of its project protecting the certification of elections (gifts for it released $1.2 million in 2025). Its share of the field's money is {{row:dem_attribution_share}}.
- **Reforms (the rest, a little over half).** All its other work counts only here, never also against a slide. It is valued as in our Brennan Center model: what the movement for redistricting commissions achieves per dollar, scaled to a quarter for all of the Center's reform work, its fair-maps litigation included, because the shared movement figure leaves litigation and its wins out. Voting rights, money in politics and ethics count through any change in who is elected or what is decided, not per vote. The Utah ruling restored a commission reform already in that movement's record, so it counts through this branch rather than as a win of its own.

Both branches have side effects, each a shared figure. In the slide branch: democracy's value beyond income, less repression, US democracy's effect on democracy abroad, and backlash. In the reform branch: fairer elections valued beyond income, and the counter-measures that reforms provoke. With its next dollar at 36% of the past average, the result is {{result}}. The 36% reflects large reserves: about 19 months of spending available within a year, and revenue nearly twice its spending in 2025.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts move the result most, then the missing-data discount, the next-dollar figure and the size of democracy's income gain. After those, almost tied, come how much a reform dollar does against a redistricting dollar and the share of democracy's income gain that fair maps bring, then the field's effect on the risk of a slide.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether a year of the whole field's work changes the chance of a slide at all, and by how much. No study measures it.
- What a reform within a democracy is worth. The redistricting figures and the quarter for other reforms are judgments, anchored on studies of maps and of mail voting.
- How the spending splits between the branches. The accounts give programme columns, not the share of each that is direct work; ours is a judgment from the 2025 annual report.

### What isn't modelled

- Campaign Legal Center Action's lobbying, which has its own accounts and isn't tax-deductible.
- Diminishing returns within the reform branch: its value grows with the Center's spending, as in the other models of the field.

### How this model was checked

An independent auditor reviews the model in rounds; the findings and fixes of each round are in audits/campaign-legal-center-charityevals/.
