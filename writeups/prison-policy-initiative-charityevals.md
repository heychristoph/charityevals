### Summary

Prison Policy Initiative (PPI) researches mass incarceration in the United States and campaigns with others to change it. Counting its spending over fiscal years 2021 to 2025 against a share of the wins of those years, our model comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. About two thirds of that comes from deaths averted by the decarceration PPI's data fed, and about three tenths from the federal caps on prison and jail phone rates. Two things hold it down. Money saved by poor American families counts for little on a scale set by the world's poorest people. And PPI holds about five years of spending in reserve, so the next dollar counts at a quarter of the past average.

### What the charity does

PPI is a team of about a dozen people in Northampton, Massachusetts. In the year to June 2025 it spent $1.9 million, $1.7 million of it on its one programme of research, advocacy and organizing. It publishes the data reform campaigns use, such as Mass Incarceration: The Whole Pie and its jail and phone-rate studies. It led the national campaign against prison gerrymandering. It has campaigned against the price of phone calls from prisons and jails for two decades. It also gives technical support to advocates in about 24 states.

### How the impact happens

The model has four branches, one for each kind of win.

- **Federal phone caps.** The Martha Wright-Reed Act of 2022 let the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) cap the price of calls made within a state. The caps the FCC set in 2024 would have saved families about $450 million a year. In 2025 the FCC raised them, and the caps now in force still save families about $250 million a year, mostly on jail calls. The FCC's 2021 cut of interstate caps adds about $30 million a year, scaled from the 2024 savings by interstate calls' share and the size of the cut.
- **State free-call laws.** Connecticut, California, Minnesota, Massachusetts and Colorado made prison calls free between 2021 and 2023, together saving families about $75 million a year. Two other kinds of money win from the period are added to this branch as judgments: ending medical copays and capping fees (about $2 million a year), and bans on replacing in-person visits with paid video calls (about $2 million a year).
- **Decarceration.** PPI's data and support fed the reforms of the period, which we judge keep about 50,000 people out of prison or jail. Each year out is valued at the shared life-satisfaction cost of a year inside (facts/incarceration.json, 1.2 points out of 10) and at the deaths it averts: a year in prison costs about 2 years of life (Patterson 2013, also in facts/incarceration.json and read by the Brennan Center's model), as a share of a death at the remaining 40 years, discounted for the 3 years until they come. The two are different things, the quality of the years a person lives and the years they do not live, so neither repeats the other. Three side effects are rows of this branch: families with a parent home add 15% and earnings kept after release 10% (both shared figures in facts/incarceration.json, read by the site's other prison models), and crime by people not held takes away 10%.
- **Prison gerrymandering.** PPI led the campaign to count incarcerated people at their home address when districts are drawn, and several states changed for the 2021 maps. We judge about 2 million people gained a fairer say, worth a very small 0.0003 life-satisfaction points a year each (anchored on Swiss studies of political voice), with half the credit to PPI.

### How the model works

The cost is PPI's average real spending over fiscal years 2021 to 2025, with management and fundraising included. Each win counts for the site's shared number of years a won reform counts, discounted for the wait until it takes effect. Only PPI's share of each campaign's resources is credited: 7% for the federal caps (its spending in these five years against the coalition's over the whole campaign), 4% for the state laws (Worth Rises led those), 0.5% for decarceration and 50% for gerrymandering. A dollar a family saves is valued on the site's log scale at the families' low consumption per person. From that we subtract the same dollar's value to whoever pays instead: the county and state budgets that lost commissions (taxpayers, at national-accounts consumption) and the phone companies' owners (a group, at twice the US survey average per person, as for landlords in California YIMBY's model), for the federal caps, and state taxpayers (at the shared cost of public funds) for free calls. Every branch takes the shared missing-data discount. The phone wins have two side effects as rows of their own: more contact with family adds 10%, and possible cuts in phone service take away 3%.

### What drives the result

Deaths averted by decarceration give about two thirds of the result, the federal caps about three tenths and the state laws about a thirtieth. The decarceration wellbeing term and gerrymandering give about 2% each. The largest swings are the years a win counts, the missing-data discount, PPI's next-dollar figure, PPI's share of the federal campaign, the site's exchange rate between units of value and healthy years, and the value of fairer representation.

### Biggest uncertainties

- PPI's share of a phone-rate campaign many groups carried.
- The value of fairer representation, which no study measures.
- Whether the interim 2025 caps hold until permanent rules, and how much providers recover through charges the caps don't reach.
- The savings figures are advocates' and the FCC's projections, not measured bills.
- The number of people the reforms keep out, and PPI's part in them, are both our judgments.

### What isn't modelled

Health in prison short of death isn't counted separately, because it is part of the life-satisfaction gap; deaths are counted on their own.

### How this model was checked

Nine independent audit rounds were run (audits/prison-policy-initiative-charityevals/). Round 1 found two high-severity errors. The families' consumption had used a per-household ratio per person, and the payers had not been valued at the site's national-accounts consumption. The fixes also put federal credit on the whole campaign, took the 2025 savings lost from one source, and made gerrymandering, fees and visiting wins branches of their own. Round 2 passed and its remaining points were fixed. Payers were split between public budgets and phone-company owners, a shared state and local payer figure was added (also read by Habitat for Humanity's advocacy model), Connecticut was anchored on its own prison count, and the decarceration side effects now cite their studies. Round 3 verified those changes and asked for anchors on the smaller money wins and a corrected reading of the representation study. Round 4 verified those fixes, and round 5 a last wording fix. Round 6 verified the deaths channel added to decarceration, which doubled the result (0.029 to 0.060).
