### Summary

Prison Policy Initiative (PPI) researches mass incarceration in the United States and campaigns with others to change it. Counting its spending over fiscal years 2021 to 2025 against a share of the wins of those years, our model comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="prison-policy-initiative-charityevals">0.03</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. About four fifths of that comes from the federal caps on prison and jail phone rates. The figure is low for two reasons. Money saved by poor American families counts for little on a scale set by the world's poorest people. And PPI holds about five years of spending in reserve, so the next dollar counts at a quarter of the past average.

### What the charity does

PPI is a team of about a dozen people in Northampton, Massachusetts. In the year to June 2025 it spent $1.9 million, $1.7 million of it on its one programme of research, advocacy and organizing. It publishes the data reform campaigns use, such as Mass Incarceration: The Whole Pie and its jail and phone-rate studies. It led the national campaign against prison gerrymandering. It has campaigned against the price of phone calls from prisons and jails for two decades. It also gives technical support to advocates in about 24 states.

### How the impact happens

The model has four branches, one for each kind of win.

- **Federal phone caps.** The Martha Wright-Reed Act of 2022 let the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) cap the price of calls made within a state. The caps the FCC set in 2024 would have saved families about $450 million a year. In 2025 the FCC raised them, and the caps now in force still save families about $250 million a year, mostly on jail calls. The FCC's 2021 cut of interstate caps adds a judged $30 million a year.
- **State free-call laws.** Connecticut, California, Minnesota, Massachusetts and Colorado made prison calls free between 2021 and 2023, together saving families about $75 million a year. Two other kinds of money win from the period are added to this branch as judgments: ending medical copays and capping fees ($8 million a year), and bans on replacing in-person visits with paid video calls ($5 million a year).
- **Decarceration.** PPI's data and support fed the reforms of the period, which we judge keep about 50,000 people out of prison or jail. Each year out is valued at the shared life-satisfaction cost of a year inside (facts/incarceration.json, 1.2 points out of 10). Two side effects are rows of this branch: families with a parent home add 15%, and crime by people not held takes away 10%.
- **Prison gerrymandering.** PPI led the campaign to count incarcerated people at their home address when districts are drawn, and several states changed for the 2021 maps. We judge about 2 million people gained a fairer say, worth a very small 0.0003 life-satisfaction points a year each (anchored on Swiss studies of political voice), with half the credit to PPI.

### How the model works

The cost is PPI's average real spending over fiscal years 2021 to 2025, with management and fundraising included. Each win counts for the site's shared number of years a won reform counts, discounted for the wait until it takes effect. Only PPI's share of each campaign's resources is credited: 7% for the federal caps (its spending in these five years against the coalition's over the whole campaign), 4% for the state laws (Worth Rises led those), 0.5% for decarceration and 50% for gerrymandering. A dollar a family saves is valued on the site's log scale at the families' low consumption per person. From that we subtract the same dollar's value to whoever pays instead: phone companies' owners and the county and state budgets that lost commissions, for the federal caps, and state taxpayers (at the shared cost of public funds) for free calls. Every branch takes the shared missing-data discount. The phone wins have two side effects as rows of their own: more contact with family adds 10%, and possible cuts in phone service take away 3%.

### What drives the result

The federal caps give about four fifths of the result and the state laws about a seventh. Decarceration and gerrymandering give about 5% each. The largest swings are the years a win counts, the missing-data discount, PPI's next-dollar figure, PPI's share of the federal campaign, the site's exchange rate between units of value and healthy years, and the value of fairer representation.

### Biggest uncertainties

- PPI's share of a phone-rate campaign many groups carried.
- The value of fairer representation, which no study measures.
- Whether the interim 2025 caps hold until permanent rules, and how much providers recover through charges the caps don't reach.
- The savings figures are advocates' and the FCC's projections, not measured bills.
- The number of people the reforms keep out, and PPI's part in them, are both our judgments.

### What isn't modelled

Health in prison isn't counted separately, because it is part of the life-satisfaction gap. Nor are earnings after release, for the same reason.

### How this model was checked

Three independent audit rounds were run (audits/prison-policy-initiative-charityevals/). Round 1 found two high-severity errors. The families' consumption had used a per-household ratio per person, and the payers had not been valued at the site's national-accounts consumption. The fixes also put federal credit on the whole campaign, took the 2025 savings lost from one source, and made gerrymandering, fees and visiting wins branches of their own. Round 2 passed and its remaining points were fixed. Payers were split between public budgets and phone-company owners, a shared state and local payer figure was added (also read by Habitat for Humanity's advocacy model), Connecticut was anchored on its own prison count, and the decarceration side effects now cite their studies. Round 3 verified those changes.
