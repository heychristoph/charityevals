### Summary

The Federation of American Scientists is a Washington DC science-policy organisation founded in 1945 by Manhattan Project scientists. It counts the world's nuclear arsenals in the open, ran a Global Risk Program on AI, nuclear and biological risk, and has a small team on AI safety and security. Most of its money goes to making US science policy and government work better. Our model of all its work in the year to June 2025 comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. Almost all of that comes from the nuclear work, which is about 3% of the cost. The plausible range runs from about 0.07 to about 29.

### What the charity does

FAS spent $36.4 million in the year to June 2025 and had 116 employees. Its programmes cost $32.2 million:

- **Fiscal sponsorships: $13.2 million.** Nine projects run under FAS's tax status. The largest is the Alliance for Learning Innovation, which campaigns for federal education research.
- **Talent initiatives: $11.2 million.** Fellows placed in federal agencies, among them the Department of Agriculture, and ten fellows in civil society working on energy affordability, digital government and federal data.
- **Research, policy and technical assistance: $5.4 million.** The issue teams: climate and energy, wildfire and heat, government capacity, federal data, state and local government (it took over MetroLab Network), AI, the Global Risk Program and the Nuclear Information Project. That project publishes the Status of World Nuclear Forces, the Nuclear Notebook and the SIPRI Yearbook's nuclear chapter. The Global Risk Program ran an 18-month, $1.5 million project on AI and nuclear, biological and cyber risk, funded by the Future of Life Institute from February 2025.
- **Day One Project: $2.4 million.** A year-long sprint that produced more than 200 policy proposals for the new administration, and the start of the Center for Regulatory Ingenuity.

### How the impact happens

Nuclear war, pandemics and AI carry a yearly chance of catastrophe, taken from the shared forecasts. A typical project cuts that risk by a share per dollar, taken from Rethink Priorities' cross-cause model. The cut counts for the years such progress would otherwise have taken to come (5 by default), with each year discounted. Deaths count across the world's age groups, like every death on the site. A catastrophe that ends humanity's future also takes the lives of everyone not yet born, counted only through the discount rate.

The rest of the work aims to make US public spending and regulation work better. A dollar of it is taken to bear on about $100 of public money a year in the programmes it touches. Rich countries' public investment loses about {{row:gov_efficiency_gap}} of its value to inefficiency. The work is taken to close 2% of that gap in what it touches. The public value gained is valued at what a dollar is worth to the average American.

### How the model works

The return doesn't split the research line by issue, so the model splits it by headcount. About 5 of the roughly 35 issue-team staff work on nuclear weapons, 14% of the research line. The Global Risk Program, worked out from its grant and its director's pay, adds about 12%, split 40% nuclear, 20% pandemics and 40% AI. Other AI policy work adds 2%; today's AI team joined after the year. A few Day One proposals on catastrophic risks are added to the AI work. That puts about {{row:nuclear_cost_share}} of the cost on nuclear risk, {{row:ai_cost_share}} on AI, 0.4% on pandemics and the rest on science policy and government capacity. Each part has its own adjustments:

| Adjustment | Nuclear | AI | Pandemics | Science policy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How far the effect applies | 0.8 | 0.7 | 0.7 | 1 |
| Work others would not have done now | 0.7 | 0.6 | 0.7 | 0.6 |
| FAS's share of the resources | 0.9 | 0.9 | 0.8 | 0.7 |

Every part is discounted 50% for missing data. Ploughshares' grant to FAS ($90,000) is part of FAS's costs, so a donor here and Ploughshares each get credit only for their own dollars.

Side effects add 8%. Most of that is work against a single detonation or a limited war, which has no shared forecast. The rest is the public record of the arsenals and the wildfire and heat work's health gains, beyond what better spending counts. The climate teams' emissions cuts add almost nothing. No harm is added beyond a typical project's chance of backfiring.

FAS holds about two months of spending in unrestricted reserves and ran a small deficit, but most of its programmes run on grants restricted to them. The next dollar counts at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of the past average.

### What drives the result

The nuclear work brings 93% of the result, the AI work 4%, the pandemic work 2% and the science-policy work under 1%. The science-policy work counts for so little because it helps Americans, who are far richer than the people a dollar helps most. Only about 5% of FAS's spending is on catastrophic risks.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much a dollar of nuclear work cuts the risk.** Rethink's figures are placeholders, and the range runs ten times either way. This is the largest swing, from 0.35 to 12.
- **How many years a cut counts before it would have come anyway.** The shared slider runs from 1 to 20 years.
- **How much of the research spending is on catastrophic risks.** The Nuclear Information Project's share is by headcount, from 10% to 22%; the Global Risk Program's comes from its grant and pay, from 8% to 20%.
- **How much the science-policy work improves public spending.** That is a judgment with a range of a hundred times either way, but it moves the result very little.

### What isn't modelled

- Losses beyond deaths, such as survivors' lost income and health, are a shared figure set to 0.
- The nine sponsored projects other than the Alliance for Learning Innovation aren't named in the return. They are valued with the other science-policy work.

### How this model was checked

The model was built from the catastrophic-risk template, with a branch added for the science-policy work. Round 1 of the independent audit found that the split of the research line missed the Global Risk Program FAS ran in the year, which today's staff list no longer shows. It is now in, split between nuclear, pandemic and AI work. The climate side effect was put on the site's scale for emissions. Round 2 passed. Its smaller findings were fixed: the program's size from the grant's real dates and the director's pay, and today's AI team left out of a year before it joined. A verification round checked those fixes. The rounds are in audits/federation-of-american-scientists-charityevals/.
