### Summary

Opportunity Green uses law, policy and economic analysis to cut emissions from shipping and aviation, working from London and Brussels. Our model counts its part in the two policies its work from mid-2022 to March 2026 pressed hardest. Both are still waiting for a decision. It comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**, below zero. On the site's values, the shipping framework's emissions cuts are worth far less than the extra fuel costs ships would pay for them. Ships' payments into a new IMO fund cost those who pay them; the part the fund would send to climate-vulnerable countries makes up only part of that. Even the sign of the result is uncertain.

### What the charity does

Opportunity Green spent £2.54 million in the year to March 2026, £2.47 million of it on charitable activities (audited accounts). That includes £234,000 granted to its Brussels sister organisation, which does the same work. The accounts don't split spending by programme. The annual report describes four kinds of work:

- **Climate diplomacy.** Legal, technical and policy support for climate-vulnerable delegations at the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and the civil aviation body ICAO. Briefings went to 45 countries before the IMO talks.
- **Advocacy.** The SASHA Coalition, 18 companies making e-fuels and zero-emission planes and ships, presses for EU rules, including emissions trading on international flights.
- **Legal action.** A challenge to the EU Taxonomy's "green" label for fossil-fuelled planes and ships (heard in February 2026), advertising complaints about cruise lines' LNG claims, and a complaint against the steelmaker ArcelorMittal.
- **Analysis**, plus newer work on steel, trucking, data centres and farming.

### How the impact happens

Opportunity Green helps policies get made and then made stronger. A policy cuts emissions once it is in force. The model counts two:

- **The IMO Net-Zero Framework.** It sets a falling limit on how much greenhouse gas ships' fuel may emit, and ships that miss the limit pay into a new IMO Net-Zero Fund. It was approved in April 2025, but in October 2025 a vote put off adopting it for a year, under pressure from the United States and oil-producing states. The decision is due on 4 December 2026. In its first five years it would cut about 130 million tonnes of CO₂e a year (Transport & Environment's analysis of the IMO's impact assessment). The fund would raise about $10 billion a year. Opportunity Green presses for part of that money to go to climate-vulnerable countries.
- **EU emissions trading for more international flights.** The Commission proposed this in July 2026. From 2029 it would cover flights leaving Europe for places within 5,000 km, about 21 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. We count a tenth of that as actually cut.

Each policy has its own chance of being adopted: 35% for the IMO framework and 50% for the EU proposal. On top of that comes the shared share of any won climate policy that is due to advocates at all, 30%. Opportunity Green is credited with 3% of the advocates' work on the IMO framework and 4% on the EU proposal. Only the part of its work done in the period counts, about half: winning still needs the work of 2026 and after.

### How the model works

The model takes the expected tonnes from both policies over the years a won reform counts, discounted for the wait. It adjusts for what would have happened anyway, how much of each policy would be delivered, and, for shipping, the land cleared to grow crop biofuels. It divides by what the charity spent over the same four accounting periods. Its other work, which hasn't yet changed a rule, adds a tenth to the tonnes.

The IMO framework also moves money, and the model counts both sides in the main chain:

- **Compliance costs.** Ships' extra costs, about $300 per tonne cut by the IMO's own impact assessment, count as money lost by the people who pay them. Freight costs follow the volume of goods shipped, and about half of that goes to poorer countries, where a dollar counts for more.
- **Just-transition funding.** The share of the fund that reaches climate-vulnerable countries is valued as development aid of the kind the IMO lists: infrastructure, training, technology and capacity. That aid is taken to do half as much as the same money given as cash. We take 40% of the fund's revenue in the first five years, with a range of 10% to 70%. 15% of it is taken to replace aid donors would have given anyway.

Ships' payments into the fund are transfers, not compliance costs. What they cost those who pay them is netted against what the fund buys (method, section 2.23). The fund's rewards for clean fuels buy tonnes that are already in the framework's projected cut.

The next dollar is counted at 39% of the past average. Opportunity Green holds about eleven months of spending in unrestricted reserves, and new money goes partly to newer, unproven fields.

### What drives the result

On the site's values, a tonne of CO₂ kept out of the air is worth far less than the $300 ships would pay to cut it, so the IMO framework's climate effect alone is a net cost. The money the fund would send to climate-vulnerable countries pulls the other way and offsets part of the cost. The two are large and close, so the sign of the result turns on a few figures: how much of the fund reaches those countries, what that aid does per dollar, the cost per tonne, and how poor the people who end up paying it are. Because the result is a small difference between two large, uncertain amounts, its range runs from clear harm to clear good, and the site shows a warning on it. We keep each range as the evidence gives it rather than narrowing them to steady the result.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How the fund will be spent.** That is not yet decided. If the just-transition share is 70% rather than 40%, the result turns positive. If rewards for clean fuels take almost all of it, the result is more negative.
- **What the aid does.** At the value of cash, the result is clearly positive. At a fifth of cash, it is clearly negative.
- **The compliance cost.** Cheaper biofuels and LNG would lower it, and tight biofuel markets would raise it.
- **Adoption.** At the IMO's last meeting, about as many states pushed to reopen the framework as backed it, and an adopted text may be weaker.
- **Opportunity Green's share.** Many groups and governments work on both files. 3% and 4% are judgments.

### What isn't modelled

Any outcome of the pending EU Taxonomy case. The value of the advertising rulings and the ArcelorMittal complaint. The newer work on steel, trucking, data centres and farming, beyond the small share for other work. Who pays for EU allowances, and what governments do with that money.

### How this model was checked

A new build (November 2026), audited by independent reviewers. The first round found that the fund's money was valued too generously. It took a whole aid budget's value, which includes health aid, so the model now uses development aid's value. The money also missed the evidence step and any allowance for displacing other aid. The share of shipping costs borne in poorer countries was too low. With these fixes the result went from positive to slightly negative. The second round found that the displaced aid should be valued as donors' own aid, which made the result more negative. It also found that the result is unstable, being the difference of two large, uncertain amounts; we kept the ranges as they are. Before the third round, the compliance cost moved to the IMO's own impact assessment, about $300 a tonne, which made the result a little more negative. The rounds are in audits/opportunity-green-charityevals/.
