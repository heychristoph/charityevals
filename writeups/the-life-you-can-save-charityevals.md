### Summary

The Life You Can Save recommends about thirty charities fighting extreme poverty and raises money for them. Our model of its work in 2025 comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="the-life-you-can-save-charityevals">13</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. It reports moving $24 million that year, for $2.6 million of its own costs. We count the share of that giving it caused, about three dollars for each dollar it spends. Each dollar is valued at what its recipients achieve on this site. The figure moves most with how much the next dollar does compared with the 2025 average, and with the share of the giving it caused.

### What the charity does

The Life You Can Save works through two charities, in the US and in Australia, and publishes their accounts together. In 2025 the US entity spent $2.19 million on its own work (its Form 990: total expenses less the grants it passed on). The Australian entity spent A$653,207. Peter Singer, whose book gave it its name, chairs it.

Its work is one programme: getting people to give, and to give to charities that do a lot of good for the poorest people. It publishes a list of recommended charities, runs a website, podcast and outreach to donors, and takes gifts that it passes on to the charities it recommends. In 2025 it also ran an emergency fund with Founders Pledge, after the cuts to foreign aid.

The donations it passes on, $10.0 million in the US and A$13.4 million in Australia, are donors' money. They count as the recipients' cost, not as The Life You Can Save's.

### How the impact happens

Donors give, and give to better charities, because of The Life You Can Save. Its money-moved report counts six channels in 2025:

- gifts given through its US and Australian accounts ($18.7 million at the site's exchange rate);
- emergency-fund gifts that went straight to the fund ($0.8 million);
- gifts the charities, donors and giving platforms told it it had influenced ($4.5 million).

Only about one in two of the charities it recommends report such gifts, and fewer than 100 donors did. So we allow for gifts nobody reported: a quarter more than the reported ones (our judgment, from 0.9 to 2 times). Founders Pledge takes a quarter of the credit for the emergency-fund money it counts (our judgment, a half to none).

Not all of this giving happened because of it. Many donors give through it because it passes gifts on tax-deductibly, or would have found its charities anyway. It publishes no survey of what its donors would have done. So the model takes the site's shared figure for a charity that moves money without its own survey: it caused 30% of the giving (15% to 45%). That is about $7.3 million in 2025.

A dollar moved is worth what its recipients achieve. The charity publishes its 2025 money moved by charity. Each of the 13 recipients modelled here is read live, under your values, at its figure on the rankings. Two small climate recipients without a model take Clean Air Task Force's figure, the one climate charity modelled. The others, 18 charities and the emergency fund, have no model here. Together they took more than half the money, and they are valued at the average of the modelled ones.

Half the giving it caused counts as new money. The other half would have gone to other charities, so it counts only for the gain over where it would have gone. Both are the site's shared figures for every charity that moves money.

### How the model works

Money moved = recorded money moved (with unreported gifts allowed for, and the emergency fund's shared credit taken out) × the share it caused. Gifts through its accounts wait about two months before they reach the charities (our judgment, from a few weeks to the third of a year its year-end balance implies). That wait is discounted at the site's rate.

Each dollar moved is valued at its recipients' figures, net of where shifted money would have gone. A 5% side effect is added (our judgment). It covers gifts inspired by its book and recommendations that nobody reports, against donor attention drawn from other good uses.

Its US entity received $250,000 of donated services in 2025. They are others' resources behind the same money moved, so its credit is its share of its cost and theirs, 91%.

The result is multiplied by how much the next dollar does compared with the 2025 average: 28% (13% to 63%). From 2024 to 2025 its costs rose 43% and its money moved 18%: the extra cost came with about half the money moved per dollar of the year's average. Part of 2025's money moved was a one-off emergency fund. The US entity's operations took in $1.07 million against $2.19 million of cost in 2025, but the two entities hold about $4.8 million in reserve, about 22 months of their costs, most of it in Australia (A$5.2 million of net assets at the end of 2024).

Money moved is counted, not estimated, so the model takes no missing-data discount. Each recipient read live brings its own.

### What drives the result

- **The next dollar** (28%, from 13% to 63%).
- **The share of the giving it caused** (the shared 30%, from 15% to 45%).
- **Credit by share of resources.** When it's on, the donors' new money shares the credit with your dollar.
- **The value of a young child's life, the discount rate and the worth of a healthy year in income.** These act through its recipients' own results.
- **Where shifted money would otherwise have gone** (one times GiveDirectly, from 0 to 3 times).
- Gifts nobody reported, the side effect and the emergency fund's credit move it by up to a seventh.

### Where we differ from The Life You Can Save's own figures

The Life You Can Save reports its 3-year leverage ratio, money moved over costs: 9.19 for 2023–2025.

- **The share it caused.** It counts all money moved. We count the share it caused, by the site's shared figure.
- **New and shifted money.** We use the site's shared 50%, and credit shifted money only for the gain.
- **What a dollar moved is worth.** We value each recipient at its figure on the rankings.
- **Gifts nobody reported.** It counts only the gifts reported to it. We add an allowance for the rest.
- **The emergency fund and donated services.** We take out Founders Pledge's share of the fund it ran with it, and the donated services' share of the credit.
- **The wait and the next dollar.** It reports an undiscounted average. We discount the wait for its grants and take 28% of the average for the next dollar.
- **Staff time.** We credit staff for the pay they give up, as in every model on the site (credit by share of resources, off by default).

### Biggest uncertainties

- The share of the giving it caused. No survey of its own donors exists, so the figure comes from another organisation's donor surveys.
- The recipients without a model here, more than half the money: Fistula Foundation, Sanku, Educate Girls, Living Goods, Village Enterprise, Teaching at the Right Level Africa and the emergency fund are the largest.
- The next dollar: its 2025 money moved included a one-off emergency fund, and the Australian entity's balance sheet for the end of 2025 isn't published yet.
- Gifts nobody reported. Its own reports say they undercount, but not by how much, and the channels may overlap.

### What isn't modelled

- Its recommended charities' own fundraising that its listing makes easier, beyond the side effect.
- The by-charity table leaves out donors who asked to keep their designations confidential, and doesn't reconcile exactly with the channel total. Its split is used for shares only, so the unlisted gifts are assumed to go where the listed ones do.

### How this model was checked

Three audit rounds (audits/the-life-you-can-save-charityevals/). Round 1 checked every cost, reach and fact figure against the charity's documents. Its fixes:

- Its next-dollar figure now counts the Australian entity's operating surpluses.
- The charity's recorded spending is both entities' own costs, not the grants passed on.
- Two small climate recipients take Clean Air Task Force's figure.
- Counts of the recipients modelled were corrected.

Round 2 passed. It found the Australian entity's published net assets, which lowered the next-dollar figure from 33% to 28%. Round 3 checked that change and passed.
