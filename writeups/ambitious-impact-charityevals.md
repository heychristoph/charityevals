### Summary

Ambitious Impact, formerly Charity Entrepreneurship, finds promising ideas for new charities and trains people to found them. Our model of all its programmes in the year to March 2025 comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. Each charity it founds goes on to raise and spend money for years, and the model values that money at what the charities it has launched achieve on this site. The giving organisations and companies it launched are valued by the donations they are expected to move. The figure moves most with the missing-data discount, where funders' money would otherwise have gone, the discount rate and how much the next dollar does compared with the past average.

### What the charity does

Ambitious Impact is a UK charity (charity 1195850). In the year to 31 March 2025 it spent £3,953,275. Of that, £1,969,975 were seed and follow-on grants to the organisations it had incubated. That money is part of what the new charities raise and spend, so the model takes it out of the cost of Ambitious Impact's own work, about $2.5 million. The share of a further gift that goes to such grants, about a third, is valued at what its charities achieve.

Its programmes in the year:

- **Incubation Program.** Two cohorts founded nine charities: ACTRA, Rahi Impact, Lead Research for Action, Oxygen Access Project, Respira Health, Scale Welfare, the Radius Institute for Road Safety, LABRI and the Institute for Development Innovation. They received about $1.3 million of seed funding. Eight work on human well-being and one, Scale Welfare, on animal welfare.
- **Effective Giving Initiatives.** A one-off pilot launched five national giving organisations.
- **Founding to Give.** Eleven for-profit companies were launched by founders who commit to give much of what they earn.
- **Funding circles.** Groups of funders it runs granted $5.8 million.
- **Impactful Grantmaking.** It trained 14 grantmakers who control about $40 million a year.
- **AIM Research Program.** It trained 28 researchers.
- **Research.** Its research team recommended the 12 ideas behind its cohorts.

The accounts split spending by type of cost, not by programme, so all of it is one model with a branch for each programme.

### How the impact happens

A new charity does good with the money it raises over its life. The model counts that money as money Ambitious Impact moved, like the money an effective-giving organisation moves:

- **How much the charities raise.** Its 60 charities have raised $107 million since 2019, about $1.8 million each so far in 2024 prices, including the ones that closed. That part is measured, and most of it is spent in a charity's first three years. They are young and the survivors grow, so a charity's whole life is taken to raise 2.5 times that (1.5 to 5 times). The projected part beyond what has been raised so far is discounted for missing data and comes later, about six years after launch. Both waits are discounted at the site's rate.
- **How much of that Ambitious Impact caused.** 60% (30% to 85%): some of these charities would have existed anyway, later or in another form.
- **What a dollar to its charities does.** Each charity it has launched that the site ranks is read live at its figure on the rankings, under your values. That is GiveWell's figure for ten of them, including Suvita, Family Empowerment Media, Fortify Health, Ansh and Taimaka. ACTRA is the Happier Lives Institute's, NOVAH is charityevals' own, and twelve younger ones are Ambitious Impact's own idea-stage estimates. The animal charities are Shrimp Welfare Project (Animal Charity Evaluators') and Animal Policy International. Charities are weighted equally within each cause, and the causes eight to one, as in the year's launches.
- **New and shifted money.** About 60% of its charities' money comes from institutional funders such as GiveWell and Open Philanthropy, who would have granted it anyway. That money counts only for the gain over an ordinary charity of the same cause. Of the rest, from individual donors, half is counted as new money, the site's shared figure for every organisation that moves money.

Each effective giving organisation is expected to record $2 million of donations over its life if it succeeds ($0.5 million to $10 million). FarmKind, a giving platform from an earlier cohort, has raised almost $4 million. That money goes through the site's shared steps for a new meta organisation: its chance of success, its own effect on the donations (30%) and the missing-data discount. It is valued at GiveWell's Top Charities, half as new money, less the organisations' own running costs of about $0.5 million each over their lives. The giving organisation asks the donor and Ambitious Impact founded it, so both moved that money: they share the credit, {{row:egi_credit_share}} to Ambitious Impact (30% to 70%). Its funding circles count for 30% of their grants, the site's shared share for money a meta charity records without its own donor survey, all of it shifted. Founding to Give counts $150,000 of expected donations per company ($15,000 to $1.5 million), about ten years away, since most start-ups never pay out. None has given yet, so these donations are discounted for missing data. They are also valued at GiveWell's Top Charities. The grantmaker training and its published research change how others spend money they would spend anyway. For those, the model counts 1.5% of the trained grantmakers' grants for three years and 3% of the money its research informs, valued only for the gain and discounted for weak evidence and missing data. About a quarter of the research fellows moved into high-impact research. Those careers are valued by the site's shared career-change step.

### How the model works

Money raised = 9 charities × $1.8 million × 2.5 × the discounted waits × 60%, the projected part discounted for missing data. To that come the giving organisations' and companies' expected donations and the circles' share. Each dollar is valued at its charities' figures: institutional grants at their gain over where they would have gone, the rest net of where shifted money would have gone. The changed spending of the grantmakers and readers of its research and its fellows' careers are added. Then come two side effects: +8% for founders who go on to lead elsewhere and ideas others use, and −2% for partners' time spent on charities that fail. The total is then multiplied by how much the next dollar does compared with the past average: {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}}, our figure for Ambitious Impact. It has over a year of spending in reserve, and its constraint is founders and ideas as much as money. The third of a further gift that goes to seed grants is valued at its charities' figures instead.

### What drives the result

- **The missing-data discount.** It applies to the projected part of the charities' fundraising, the giving organisations' and companies' future donations, and the idea-stage figures of its youngest charities.
- **Where shifted money would otherwise have gone** (one times GiveDirectly, from 0 to 3 times). Most of its charities' money is institutional and counts only for this gain.
- **The discount rate.** The charities' money comes over years, so at 0.1% the result is about three times higher.
- **The next dollar** ({{row:marginal_vs_past_average}}, from 15% to 75%).
- **Founding to Give's expected donations** ($150,000 a company, from $15,000 to $1.5 million).
- **How much of their existence it caused** (60%, from 30% to 85%) and **their lifetime fundraising** (2.5 times what they have raised so far, from 1.5 to 5).

### Biggest uncertainties

- The charities' lifetime fundraising. The $107 million is Ambitious Impact's own count, and the multiple for the years to come is our judgment.
- How much of each charity's existence it caused, which no study has measured.
- The figures of its youngest charities, which are Ambitious Impact's own forecasts made before the charities ran.

### What isn't modelled

- Its charities that the site doesn't model, such as LEEP (whose only model here is unpublished), FarmKind, Rahi Impact and Scale Welfare. They are valued at the average of their cause.

### How this model was checked

Round 1 of the audit (audits/ambitious-impact-charityevals/) found two high-severity problems. The projected part of the charities' future fundraising took no missing-data step, and two future benefits were discounted by hand instead of at the shared rate. Both were fixed. The fixes for its other findings weight the causes by the charities actually founded, count institutional funders' grants as shifted money, and value the third of a further gift that goes to seed grants at the charities' figures. The share of each charity's existence it caused now answers one question, with the founders' alternative work moved into the side effects. Together these took the result from 20 to 14. Round 2 found the effective giving organisations valued as small charities rather than by the donations they move. They now have their own chain through the shared steps for a new meta organisation. The side effect was split into benefits and harms, without the founders' forgone pay, which the founded charities' own models already credit. The measured and projected money got their own waits. Together these took the result from 14 to 16. Round 3 found that Founding to Give's donations, none yet made, took no missing-data step. They now do, the giving organisations' running costs are subtracted, and the expected donations per company are built from a reference chain. That took the result to 15.
