### Summary

The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists is a Chicago non-profit that publishes expert journalism on nuclear weapons, climate change, biological threats and AI, and sets the Doomsday Clock each January. Our model of all its work in the year to June 2025 comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="bulletin-of-the-atomic-scientists-charityevals">20</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. That figure rests on forecasts of how likely a nuclear war is and on an estimate of what a typical dollar of such work does to the risk. Both are deeply uncertain, so the plausible range runs from about 1 to about 420.

### What the charity does

The Bulletin spent $3.5 million in the year to June 2025, with 21 staff. Its tax return reports one programme, costing $2.9 million:

- **The open website.** More than 350 articles a year, most written by outside experts with the Bulletin's editors. It had almost seven million views in 2024.
- **The magazine.** Six issues a year, found in 7,500 libraries.
- **The Doomsday Clock.** Scientists on its board set the Clock each January. The January 2025 setting was in 5,659 news stories on the day.
- **The Nuclear Notebook.** Counts of each country's nuclear weapons, written by the Federation of American Scientists and published by the Bulletin.
- **Events and fellowships.** Webinars, an annual gathering, and programmes for early-career writers.

The Bulletin has one editor each for nuclear affairs, climate change, disruptive technologies and biosecurity. The return doesn't split spending between them, so we judge the split: about 48% nuclear, 15% climate, 15% AI, 12% pandemics, and 10% on subjects outside these risks.

### How the impact happens

Each risk carries a yearly chance of catastrophe, taken from the experts' forecasts in the 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament and spread evenly to 2100. For nuclear war that is an 8% chance by 2100 of a war that kills over 10% of people, and 0.55% of one that ends humanity's future.

A typical project cuts each risk by a share per dollar, taken from Rethink Priorities' cross-cause model, including its chance that a project backfires. The cut counts for the years such progress would otherwise have taken to come (5 by default), with each year discounted. Deaths are valued across the world's age groups like every death on the site. A catastrophe that ends humanity's future also takes the lives of everyone not yet born, which count only through the discount rate.

Climate coverage is valued in tonnes of greenhouse gas kept out of the air, at our judgment of a typical climate dollar, then through the site's one conversion from tonnes to deaths.

### How the model works

Each area gets its own adjustments:

| Adjustment | Nuclear | Pandemics | AI | Climate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How far a typical project's effect applies to journalism | 0.7 | 0.6 | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| Work no one else would have done now | 0.7 | 0.6 | 0.5 | 0.4 |
| The Bulletin's share of the resources | 0.85 | 0.85 | 0.85 | 0.85 |
| Missing-data discount | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 |

The Bulletin reaches far more people than most groups in the field, but it informs rather than campaigns. So its nuclear work is valued below the Arms Control Association's briefs for Congress. Its AI and climate coverage sits in crowded fields, where much of it would be written elsewhere. Its share of the resources is below 1 because outside experts write most articles and the Clock's scientists give their time.

The Federation of American Scientists' model credits the Nuclear Notebook to its own spending, so no dollar is counted twice.

Side effects add 10%: coverage of a single detonation or a limited war, which has no shared forecast, the subjects outside the four risks, and the archive. No harm is added beyond a typical project's chance of backfiring.

The Bulletin had about six months of reserves at June 2025, after deficits of $0.6 million and $0.75 million in the last two years. The next dollar counts at 60% of the past average.

### What drives the result

The nuclear coverage brings 94% of the result, the pandemic coverage 4% and the AI coverage 2%. The climate coverage adds almost nothing: a typical climate dollar averts far fewer deaths than a typical nuclear dollar. At the default 4% discount rate, the future counts for little. At a 0.1% discount rate the result rises to about 79.

The result is about half the Arms Control Association's 41: only half the Bulletin's spending is on nuclear risk, and journalism is further from decisions. It is close to the Nuclear Threat Initiative's 23 and Ploughshares Fund's 26.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much a dollar of this work cuts the risk.** Rethink's figures are placeholders, and the range runs ten times either way. This is the largest swing, from 5 to 176.
- **How many years a cut counts.** The shared slider runs from 1 to 20 years, moving the result from 4 to 62.
- **How the spending splits between areas.** The accounts report one programme, so the nuclear share is our judgment.
- **How far journalism beats or trails a typical project.** Nothing about it is measured.

### What isn't modelled

- Losses beyond deaths, such as survivors' lost income and health, are a shared figure set to 0.
- The forecasts are spread evenly over the century, though forecasters put much of the risk later.
- Climate damage other than deaths is a shared figure set to 0.

### How this model was checked

The model was built from the catastrophic-risk template, with a climate branch added that counts tonnes of greenhouse gas. The rounds of independent audit are in audits/bulletin-of-the-atomic-scientists-charityevals/.
