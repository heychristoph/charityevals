### Summary

The Jesuit Refugee Service (JRS) spent $93.6 million in 2025 across 57 countries and served 1.09 million refugees and other displaced people. Its largest operations are in Chad, the DRC, Kenya, South Sudan, Lebanon and Europe. We model its $79.5 million of programmes: education in emergencies, livelihoods, emergency aid, protection and legal advice, mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), pastoral care, reconciliation and health care. Its $4.4 million of advocacy and communication has [its own model](/jesuit-refugee-service-charityevals-advocacy). We estimate **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. JRS counts the people each programme served but publishes no controlled evaluation or outcome data, so every programme is built from the research on its kind of work and counted at half by the missing-data slider.

The spending comes from the network-wide financial statement in JRS's 2025 annual report. It isn't stated to be audited: each regional office is financially autonomous, and the international office's audited accounts cover only the projects run from Rome.

### How the model works

**Education** ($34.1m, 477,034 participants; over 90% of the result). JRS runs schools in refugee camps and host communities. It is UNHCR's lead education partner in 19 camps in eastern Chad, and it also runs accelerated learning, secondary schools, vocational and language classes and teacher training. JRS publishes no count of pupils in its formal schools, and the US cuts of 2025 ended US funding for its camp secondary schools in Chad. We take 25% of participants (10-45%) to be in a school year they would otherwise lack, at half a standard deviation of learning, from a randomised trial of village schools in Afghanistan (Burde & Linden 2013). The rest get the median gain of randomised education evaluations in poor countries, 0.1 standard deviations (Evans & Yuan 2022). Of the gain, 40% lasts into adult life, where each standard deviation is worth 20% more earnings over 40 working years, starting about 8 years from now. Children with a mental disorder also gain a little from the psychosocial support built into the schools.

**Emergency aid** ($10.0m, 96,642 people; about 3%). Food, household items, cash and transit accommodation, about {{row:value_delivered_per_person}} a person delivered. Two thirds of it is in low-income crises, where mortality runs at 0.02 a year against a normal 0.008 and the aid removes 2% of the extra deaths over half a year. The rest is accommodation for asylum seekers in Europe, valued against their low consumption.

**Livelihoods** ($12.7m, 54,984 people; about 2%). Training, job placement and start-up support. It raises consumption 4% for three years. **Protection and legal advice** ($8.2m, 125,351 people; about 2%). Half the participants get legal help, and half of those cases resolve, each worth 5% more consumption for three years. The other half get protection work that improves the mental health of those with a disorder.

**MHPSS** ($8.1m, 134,921 people), **pastoral** ($3.0m), **reconciliation** ($2.8m) and **health care** ($0.6m) add under 1% together. A quarter of MHPSS participants get counselling, at 0.3 standard deviations from a trial of guided self-help with South Sudanese refugees in Uganda (Tol et al. 2020), including its spillover to the household, and averts a few suicides. The rest join community activities. Pastoral care and reconciliation are small judged gains in wellbeing. Health care is about $18 a person, at the cost per healthy year of primary care, half of it deaths averted.

Every branch has the same delivery adjustment (85%). Credit is shared with parents' associations, volunteers and community workers (70-85%). Others would have filled 10-40% of the gaps, in a year when appeals were 35% funded and JRS itself cut programmes after the US funding freeze.

### What drives the result

The value of a healthy year in units of value, the missing-data slider and the discount rate come first. Then come the education chain (earnings per standard deviation, persistence, the evidence adjustment and the share of participants in schooling they would otherwise lack) and GiveWell's household sharing of earnings.

### What isn't modelled

Violence averted by reconciliation, education's gains beyond earnings, livelihoods' spillovers to local markets, and the research on climate displacement (+). Tension with host communities, dependency and funging inside JRS (-). Together these are a judged +5%.

### How this model was checked

Built with charityevals' humanitarian-relief template on 14 November 2026, as branches over JRS's 2025 programme expenses (Convention 4), mirroring our NRC model wherever the two charities do the same work; the mechanical audit passes. Round 1 of the independent audit verified every spending line, reach count and key study and found no high findings, three medium and five low. Education's counterfactual now asks only whether another provider would have run the schools (the children's lack of schooling is in the access share), the access share fell from 30% to 25% with a wider range because JRS publishes no pupil count and its Chad secondary schools lost US funding, and MHPSS's suicides averted and health care's deaths averted became rows of their own. The result fell from 0.40 to 0.37. A scoped verification round checked the changes.
