### Summary

The California YIMBY Education Fund is the tax-deductible arm of California YIMBY, which campaigns for state laws that let more homes be built in California. Counting both organisations' spending over 2017 to 2024 against the Education Fund's share of the homes the laws of those years added, our model comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. About 85% of it is the ADU laws that followed the 2016 reforms. A little over half the value is the fall in rents an extra home brings, which renters gain and better-off landlords lose. The result is mostly uncertainty, and the organisations' share of the coalitions is a judgment.

### Which organisation

California YIMBY (founded 2017) is a 501(c)(4): it lobbies and sponsors bills, and gifts to it aren't tax-deductible. The California YIMBY Education Fund (tax-exempt since 2021) is its 501(c)(3), and the one modelled here. The two share staff and run one campaign. The model counts both organisations' wins and spending, and credits the Education Fund with its share of their spending (62% in 2024).

### What the charity does

In 2024 the Education Fund spent $3.05 million, $2.83 million of it on four programmes. Education ($1.67 million) covers events, trainings and reports such as its ADU retrospective and a study of local fees. A narrative project and the new Metropolitan Abundance Project cost $0.55 million. Advocacy ($0.31 million) means research, public education, coalition-building and help to cities in applying state housing laws. The Alliance for Housing and Climate Solutions, which it founded in 2021, cost $0.30 million. All four serve the same state housing-law campaign, so the model counts them as that campaign's cost.

### How the impact happens

- **ADU laws.** The 2016 state laws on accessory dwelling units (backyard homes, converted garages) came before California YIMBY and started the boom. The clean-up laws it supported or sponsored came after: AB 68 and AB 881 (2019), AB 2221 (2022), AB 976 (2023), SB 1211 and AB 3057 (2024), and others. In the cities' reports, ADU permits rose from 15,051 in 2019 under the 2016 rules to 39,077 in 2024 (HCD's own handbook gives a lower 30,354, the low end; the model uses the cities' series, the same one its completion counts come from). We judge 45% of 2024's permits are due to the later laws. About 65% of permits get built, from the state's completion counts. That makes about 11,000 extra homes a year, 90% of them used as homes.
- **Other laws.** Streamlined approval (SB 35, extended by SB 423), parking reform near transit (AB 2097), housing on commercial land (AB 2011), lot splits (SB 9) and other laws of the period have been used unevenly. We judge they add 2,500 homes a year that wouldn't otherwise be built.

### How the model works

An extra home does good in two ways. First, its rent is above what it costs to build, and whoever builds it keeps that gain. For an apartment the gap is about half the rent, land included, from Glaeser and Gyourko's comparison of prices with building costs in coastal California. For an ADU, which needs no land, it is about two fifths, from the median build cost in a statewide survey of ADU owners. Second, every extra home lowers rents across the state a little: 1% for each 1% more homes, between Pew's 0.5 across US metros and a national model's 2.5. Renters save that money and landlords lose it. Each dollar is valued on the site's log scale at its receiver's consumption at California prices, all on the shared survey spending per person. Renters are scaled by the survey's own renter figures, and ADU builders and landlords by multiples (1.25 and 2). Since renters are much poorer, a dollar moving from landlords to renters counts.

Each home counts over a 50-year life, discounted at the shared rate. Once the law would have come anyway, a further 5% a year comes off for homes the market would then have built. Each law counts for the site's shared number of years a won reform counts, discounted for a 2% yearly chance of repeal or being gutted and for the wait until the homes are lived in. The cost is the two organisations' average real spending over the eight years. The credit is their spending on each kind of law over the campaigners' resources behind it, about 25% for the ADU laws (15% of their spending against about $13 million from the other campaigners: the Casita Coalition's $1.2 million from its 990s and a judged $12 million from the large lobbies) and 12% for the others (half their spending against about $103 million: YIMBY Action's state work from its 990s and a judged $100 million from the rest of the coalition). Legislators and the housing department, who pass and carry out the laws, are credited through the mobiliser's share. That is then multiplied by the Education Fund's share of their spending. Both branches take the shared missing-data discount. Four side effects are rows of their own. Higher productivity of other workers as job centres grow adds 3%. Less homelessness from lower rents adds 2%. Costs to neighbours take away 3%. Lower emissions, 2 tonnes a home-year, go through the site's climate conversion and add about 0.1%. Then the next dollar counts at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of the past average, because the two organisations hold about two years of spending in reserve.

### What drives the result

The ADU branch gives about 85% of the value and the other laws 15%. Within each, the fall in rents gives a little over half and the builders' gain the rest. The largest swings are the years a win counts, the missing-data discount, the other campaigners' resources behind the ADU laws, how much rents fall per extra home, the next-dollar figure and the discount rate.

### Biggest uncertainties

- How much builders, realtors and other housing groups put into the same laws. Lobbying disclosures don't split spending by bill.
- How much statewide rents fall for each extra home when people can move in from other states.
- How much of the ADU boom the later laws caused, rather than the 2016 laws maturing.
- How many homes the streamlining and other laws really added.

### What isn't modelled

The 2025 wins (SB 79 on homes near transit, the CEQA reforms) and the 2026 bills are left out. They were bought partly with spending not yet filed, and their homes are years away. Crowding isn't counted. Neither is the surplus of the households who move into the new homes beyond the rent they pay, which is mostly paid back in rent.

### How this model was checked

Six independent audit rounds (audits/california-yimby-education-fund-charityevals/). Round 1 found two rule breaches: lower emissions had been a typed share instead of tonnes through the site's climate conversion, and renters' consumption a typed ratio instead of the shared survey figures. It also asked to cut the agglomeration side effect to spillovers, start the catch-up only once the law would have come, anchor the fall in rents on US data, build the coalition shares from spending, and put builders and landlords on the survey base. The result went from 0.16 to 0.12. Round 2 passed. Its points were then fixed: renters from the survey's own renter figures, the ADU completion share from the state's completion counts, the ADU build cost and Pew sourced, and the builders' multiple lowered (0.12 to 0.10). Round 3 verified those changes. Its open points were then fixed: everyone else's resources built from the Casita Coalition's and YIMBY Action's 990s plus judged lobby and staff totals (Cal-Access doesn't split lobbying by bill), permits from the same state series as the completion share, and the ADU survey's costs moved from 2019 prices (0.10 to 0.13). Round 4 verified those changes. Round 5 re-judged the two coalition shares for the credit rule: the legislators' and housing department's staff time left the denominators (they are credited through the mobiliser's share). Round 6 checked this text against the rows.
