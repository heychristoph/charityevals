### Summary

The Bail Project pays bail for people held in US jails because they can't afford it, and helps them get to court until their cases close. Its bail work comes to about **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="the-bail-project-charityevals">0.013</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, and its policy, public-education and research work, modelled separately, to about **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="the-bail-project-charityevals-advocacy">0.003</span>**. Both are low: a few weeks out of jail for a poor American is worth a lot to them, but costs about $2,900 a bailout, on a scale where the same money goes much further for the world's poorest.

### What the charity does

Set up in 2016 and bailing people out since January 2018, The Bail Project runs a national revolving bail fund: bail paid for a client comes back when the case closes, as about nine in ten bail dollars do, and is used again. Its staff (Bail Disruptors) meet people in jail and court, pay bail of about $3,000 on average, and send court reminders, give rides and refer clients to services. In fiscal 2025 (July 2024 to June 2025) it worked at 14 bail sites in 10 states, down from 31 two years earlier, ran community-release pilots for people freed without bail in Chicago, Washtenaw County and St. Louis, and spent $13.9 million on its Form 990, about 62% of programme spending on bail work and 38% on policy, public education and research (the split of fiscal 2024, the last year published). Revenue fell from $33.7 million in fiscal 2021 to $6.8 million in fiscal 2025, and it is spending down its reserves by design.

### How the impact happens

A person who can't pay bail stays in jail until the case closes or they find the money. Being held costs life satisfaction while inside, raises the risk of dying in the years after, makes people plead guilty to get out (so more are convicted and jailed), costs jobs and sometimes homes, and costs the county the jail days. Paying bail ends that early. The campaigns try to stop laws that would hold more people before trial or stop charitable bail funds from paying.

### How the model works

- **Cost**: the bail line with its share of overhead, plus the year's own lost bail (11% of the bail paid, forfeited or kept for fines and fees): about $9.7 million in fiscal 2025.
- **Reach**: the year's clients (5,394, the change in the charity's cumulative count), of whom about 3,300 were bailed out (the cumulative bailouts at June 2025 less those to June 2024) and the rest supported in the pilots.
- **Per bailout**: the charity's own days of pretrial detention prevented in the year over the year's bailouts, about 25 days, read as gross, plus the site's shared days of sentence avoided through better case outcomes; each year in jail valued with the shared loss of life satisfaction and the shared deaths caused per year incarcerated, at half the prison rate for weeks in jail; the shared earnings gain of release (Dobbie, Goldin and Yang's judge-assignment study); a small chance of keeping a home; the fines and fees paid from bail for the client; and the county's jail costs and the forfeited bail it keeps, valued as public money. Pilot clients avoid the jail days that missed court dates bring.
- **Adjustments**: 60% of the counted days as days clients would really have served (release by family, a bondsman or a judge counted only here), the validity of the release studies for these clients, the charity's share of the resources, days held again after missed court dates, a wait of about three years before a gift is spent (the charity holds years of spending), and the site's discount for missing data. Side effects: children and partners spared a parent's detention, and crime by people while released.
- **The policy model** counts, as a past average over fiscal 2023-2025, shares of the defeat of Texas's 2023 bail-denial amendment and personal-bond bill (two years until Proposition 3 and SB 9 passed), a twentieth of Proposition 3's effect for the protections in it, the defeat of two more Texas denial amendments in 2025, and a year's block of bills against charitable bail in Florida and Oklahoma. The Texas measures are sized on Harris County's yearly flow of eligible defendants, scaled to the state, and the share prosecutors have sought to hold in the first months of Proposition 3.

### What drives the result

Most of the value is the deaths that time in jail causes, then the earnings of release, then the county's savings and life satisfaction in jail. The result moves most with the missing-data discount, how many deaths a few weeks in jail cause compared with years in prison, and how much of the counted detention clients would really have served.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Deaths**: the site's figure comes from New York parolees' prison terms; no study measures the mortality of weeks in a county jail.
- **Days avoided**: the charity doesn't publish how it counts days of detention prevented.
- **Bailouts in the year**: the charity publishes no yearly count; ours is the change in its cumulative counts.
- **The policy work**: how much detention the defeated measures would have added, how much of their defeat is the campaign's, and whether the Florida and Oklahoma bills of 2025 (named only in the charity's report) were as close as it says.

### What isn't modelled

The forgone interest on the bail held in the courts (about $11 million at June 2025) is left out, since cost is the charity's own spending.

### How this model was checked

Two independent audits per model. Round 1 moved the bail model to fiscal 2025, the latest year with audited spending and reach, correcting a misread 2024 figure (1,299,438 cumulative days); it added the year's lost bail to the cost, read the charity's days gross so release by other means is discounted once, rewrote the anchor of the jail-mortality figure, priced missed court dates as days held again, split lost bail into clients' fees and counties' forfeitures, and moved every figure both models use into shared facts. In the policy model it limited a blocked bill to a year (the bills return each session), set the Texas delay to two years after a wait, added the 2025 Texas defeats and crime by people who stay out, and sized the Texas measures on Harris County's projection. Round 2 took the year's bailouts from the cumulative counts (about 3,300, so 25 days each), drew the year's days and clients as single reported figures, and in the policy model built the Texas measures from Harris County's eligible defendants and its prosecutors' few no-bail requests, gave SB 1318 its own term, sized SJR 87 on its sponsor's figure net of Proposition 3, and valued months of detention with a shared mortality figure for long stays. The rounds are in audits/the-bail-project-charityevals/ and audits/the-bail-project-charityevals-advocacy/.
