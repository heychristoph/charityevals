### Summary

The Bail Project pays bail for people held in US jails because they can't afford it, and helps them get to court until their cases close. Its bail work comes to about **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="the-bail-project-charityevals">0.017</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**, and its policy, public-education and research work, modelled separately, to about **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="the-bail-project-charityevals-advocacy">0.0015</span>**. Both are well below the median of the site's global-health models: a few weeks out of jail for a poor American is worth a lot to them, but costs about $2,700 a bailout, on a scale where the same money goes much further for the world's poorest.

### What the charity does

Set up in 2016 and bailing people out since January 2018, The Bail Project runs a national revolving bail fund: bail paid for a client comes back when the case closes, as it does for about nine in ten bail dollars, and is used again. Its staff (Bail Disruptors) meet people in jail and court, pay bail of about $3,000 on average, and send court reminders, give rides and refer clients to services. In fiscal 2024 (July 2023 to June 2024) it worked at 17 sites in 12 states, ran community-release pilots for people freed without bail in Chicago, Washtenaw County and St. Louis, and spent $14.5 million: $6.5 million on bail assistance and client support, $4.0 million on policy advocacy, public education and research, and the rest on management and fundraising. It has been shrinking: revenue fell from $33.7 million in fiscal 2021 to $6.8 million in fiscal 2025, and it is spending down its reserves by design.

### How the impact happens

A person who can't pay bail stays in jail until the case closes or they find the money. Being held costs life satisfaction while inside, raises the risk of dying in the years after, makes people plead guilty to get out (so more are convicted and jailed), costs jobs and sometimes homes, and costs the county the jail days. Paying bail ends that early. The campaigns try to stop laws that would hold more people before trial or stop charitable bail funds from paying.

### How the model works

- **Cost**: the bail line with its share of overhead, $9.0 million in fiscal 2024, the latest year with both a programme split and reach.
- **Reach**: the year's clients (3,972, the change in the charity's cumulative count), of whom about 3,300 were bailed out (the year's $10 million of bail over the average bail) and the rest supported in the pilots.
- **Per bailout**: the charity's own 40 days of pretrial detention avoided (its cumulative days over its cumulative bailouts), plus the site's shared days of sentence avoided through better case outcomes; each year in jail valued with the shared loss of life satisfaction and the shared deaths caused per year incarcerated, at half the prison rate for weeks in jail; the shared earnings gain of release (Dobbie, Goldin and Yang's judge-assignment study); a small chance of keeping a home; and the county's jail costs and the bail it keeps, valued as public money. Pilot clients avoid the jail days that missed court dates bring.
- **Adjustments**: 60% of the counted days as days clients would really have served, evidence, the charity's share of the resources, clients who miss court and are held again, a wait of about three years before a gift is spent (the charity holds years of spending), and the site's discount for missing data. Side effects: children and partners spared a parent's detention, and crime by people while released.

### What drives the result

Most of the value is the deaths that time in jail causes, then the earnings of release, then life satisfaction in jail and the county's savings. The result moves most with the missing-data discount, how many deaths a few weeks in jail cause compared with years in prison, and how much of the counted detention clients would really have served.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Deaths**: the site's figure comes from New York parolees' prison terms; no study measures the mortality of weeks in a county jail.
- **Days avoided**: the charity doesn't publish how it counts days of detention prevented, and its cumulative counts don't always move together from one report to the next.
- **Bailouts in the year**: the charity publishes no yearly count; ours is the year's bail over its average bail.
- **The policy work**: the advocacy model rests on judgments of how much detention the defeated Texas proposals and the blocked charitable-bail bills would have added, and how much of their defeat is the campaign's.

### What isn't modelled

The forgone interest on the bail held in the courts (about $14 million at June 2024) is left out, since cost is the charity's own spending. Fines and fees courts deduct from bail are counted as public money, not as a gift to clients, since clients would otherwise owe them later in most cases.
