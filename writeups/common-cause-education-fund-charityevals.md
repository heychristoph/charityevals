### Summary

The Common Cause Education Fund runs Election Protection and campaigns for fairer US elections: commission-drawn maps, easier voting, limits on big money and ethics rules. Our model of its year to June 2025 comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. It is a very uncertain figure, built mostly on judgment: how much the whole field's work lowers the chance of a democratic slide, and how much a reform within a democracy changes how well people are governed.

### What the charity does

Common Cause is a nonpartisan democracy-reform group founded in 1968. It has two arms. Common Cause itself is a 501(c)(4): it lobbies, brings most of the lawsuits, and gifts to it aren't tax-deductible. The Education Fund is its 501(c)(3), and this model covers only the Education Fund. In the year to June 2025 the Education Fund spent $25.1 million, $23.0 million of it on programmes:

- state programmes in 25 states, $10.0 million;
- voting and elections, $7.7 million, including the national Election Protection programme (thousands of volunteers and a hotline each election);
- other national programmes (policy, coalitions, member mobilisation, media), $2.6 million;
- media and democracy, $0.9 million;
- money in politics, $0.9 million;
- redistricting and representation, $0.9 million.

These figures are the Education Fund's own column in the audited accounts of Common Cause and Affiliate.

### How the impact happens

The work does good in two ways. First, it helps defend US democracy against a slide toward a hybrid regime. It does this by protecting voters and election officials and holding officials to account, and less directly through its organising and public education. Second, it wins reforms within the democracy. The largest is moving the drawing of electoral maps from legislators to citizens' commissions. California Common Cause sponsored California's 2008 commission measure, and Common Cause Ohio backed Ohio's failed 2024 measure. The others are easier registration and voting, rules on money in politics, ethics rules and media policy.

### How the model works

The model has three branches. Each counts what the whole field or movement achieved in a year, takes the Education Fund's share of the money behind it, and values better government as income.

- **A slide averted (about a third of the result).** This branch reads the site's shared figures. Forecasters give the US an 11% chance of losing its democracy rating before 2030, and a year of the whole pro-democracy field's work cuts that chance by about a twentieth. A slide would cost a share of democracy's long-run income gain, built up over 25 years. The Education Fund's share is the spending on its direct work (we judge 35% of its programme) over the field's money, counted in "direct-work dollars". That gives a share of 0.3%. Its reform spending counts only in the two reform branches.
- **Redistricting reform (about a quarter).** Since 2008, about 79 million Americans have come under commission-drawn maps. California, Michigan and Colorado have independent commissions. Virginia, Utah and Ohio have weaker ones, which we count at 30% of the effect. Missouri's 2018 reform was repealed before it was used, so it counts nothing. New York's 2014 commission isn't counted: Common Cause New York opposed it as not independent. We judge that a commission brings 1% of democracy's income gain. That is half the site's figure for ranked-choice voting, because maps change how votes become seats but leave who stands and how people vote alone. The movement for commissions spent about $22 million a year, and the Education Fund's share is 11%. A won reform counts the shared years, discounted, at the shared repeal rate.
- **Other reforms (the rest, a little over a third).** Voting access, money in politics, ethics and media policy count through any change in who is elected or what is decided, not per vote. Studies show such reforms change outcomes less than maps do: universal mail voting, for example, raised turnout by 2 points and changed neither party's share. So we judge a dollar of this work worth a quarter of a dollar of the redistricting movement.

All three branches have side effects, each a shared figure. In the slide branch: democracy's value beyond income, less repression, US democracy's effect on democracy abroad, and backlash. In the two reform branches: fairer elections valued beyond income, and the counter-measures that reforms provoke. With its next dollar at 45% of the past average, the result is {{result}}. The 45% reflects modest unrestricted reserves (about six months of spending) beside larger restricted gifts.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts move the result most, then the missing-data discount, the next-dollar figure and the size of democracy's income gain. After those come the field's effect on the risk of a slide and our 1% judgment for redistricting.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether a year of the whole field's work changes the chance of a slide at all, and by how much. No study measures it.
- What a reform within a democracy is worth. The 1% for redistricting and the quarter for the other reforms are our judgments, anchored on studies of maps and of mail voting, not on measured effects on income.
- The Education Fund's share of the work. We divide its money by the movement's money, but the redistricting movement's total is a judgment with a wide range.

### What isn't modelled

- Common Cause's own lawsuits and lobbying. These are the 501(c)(4)'s work, not the Education Fund's. The court victories (Moore v. Harper, the North Carolina map cases) are counted only through the field's effect, not as wins of the Education Fund.
- The split of Common Cause's shared staff between the two arms. We use their combined share of pay in cash spending.

### How this model was checked

An independent auditor reviews the model in rounds; the findings and fixes of each round are in audits/common-cause-education-fund-charityevals/.
