### Summary

Ubongo makes educational cartoons for television, radio and phones in Africa: *Akili and Me* for children of 3 to 6 and *Ubongo Kids* for 7 to 14. Our model of its 2024 work gives **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="ubongo-charityevals">26</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. Almost all of that comes from the shows' very low cost per child. The result is mostly uncertainty, chiefly how many of the children counted actually watch enough to learn, and the whole programme is discounted for missing data.

### What the charity does

Ubongo International, the US arm, spent 7.2 million dollars in 2024. Of that, 6.7 million went to its one programme: making the shows and getting them broadcast. That includes 3.5 million in grants to its sister organisations in Tanzania and elsewhere in Africa. Over 60 TV and radio stations in about 22 countries air the shows free, in 12 languages, and they are also on YouTube. Ubongo counts 42 million households reached a month, from media ratings and household surveys.

### How the impact happens

Children who watch daily learn letters, numbers, English and some science. Children who learn more earn more as adults.

### How the model works

The model starts from the households Ubongo counts. We multiply by people per household, the share aged 3 to 14 (World Bank) and a judged share of those children who watch. That gives about 28 million children, counted as one year's audience against one year's cost.

- **Learning.** A child who watches as the studies' children did gains 0.28 standard deviations on cognitive tests. That is the pooled effect of Sesame Street's local versions across 15 countries. Ubongo's own trials in Tanzania and Rwanda point the same way but publish no standard deviations to pool.
- **Repeat years.** A year of viewing counts at 60% of that, because children watch for several years and later years repeat some of what they learnt.
- **Earnings.** The gain becomes adult earnings at the site's one figure for a childhood test gain (10% per standard deviation, measured in adulthood). It is spread over working life and shared with the household, discounted for the 12 years until the children start work.
- **Who watches enough.** Only a quarter of the children counted watch near-daily, like the studies' children.
- **Evidence.** We take 0.3 of the effect: small studies tested right after viewing overstate it, and a few weeks of viewing likely fades more than a year of better teaching.
- **Credit and timing.** Ubongo International's share of the group's and partners' resources is about 72%. Part of a new gift replaces money large funders would have given anyway. A gift waits about 1.6 years, Ubongo's cash over its spending, before it is spent.

### What drives the result

The missing-data slider, the discount rate and the share of counted children who watch at the studied dose move the result most. The evidence adjustment and the repeat-year share come next. The low cost per child, a few cents, is what keeps the result high after those cuts.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Who really watches: Ubongo's monthly reach counts any household that tuned in, while the studies' children watched about 30 minutes a day for weeks.
- Whether short exposure to educational TV raises adult earnings. Only US evidence on Sesame Street speaks to this, and it is weak on wages.
- How Ubongo's audience counts are made: they are its own, and its documents describe the same 42 million as households in one place and children in another.

### What isn't modelled

Social-emotional and health learning, and the screen time that viewing takes from play and reading, are one judged share (+5%). Learning by caregivers and older children is another (+5%). No mortality effect is counted.
