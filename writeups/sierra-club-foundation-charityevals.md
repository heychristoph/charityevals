### Summary

The Sierra Club Foundation is the tax-deductible arm of the Sierra Club. It raises money and passes almost all of it to the Sierra Club for its charitable campaigns; the Sierra Club itself is a 501(c)(4) that also lobbies and backs candidates. We model the Foundation's grants in two parts, each against what it granted for that part in 2022 to 2024:

- **Climate and clean energy:** **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**, about $64 a tonne of CO₂e averted, mostly from the coal plants that closed.
- **Lands, clean air and outdoors:** **{{result:sierra-club-foundation-charityevals-lands-health}} years of healthy life per $1,000**, mostly from EPA's 2024 soot standard.

Both are low on the site's scale for the same reasons as the other US advocacy groups: each win has many authors, advocacy is only one cause of it, and much of what was won federally is being reversed.

### What the charity does

In 2024 the Foundation spent $102.1 million (Form 990), $87.5 million of it on programmes; $85.6 million went to the Sierra Club and $1.3 million to other groups. Its Form 990 names Beyond Coal ($17.1 million in 2024, down from $32.3 million in 2022) and, in 2022 and 2023, the Our Wild America lands campaign ($17.2 million and $15.0 million), which goes whole to the lands model. The rest goes to the Sierra Club's chapters, its national education work and other campaigns, without a split, so we judge half of it climate work (gas pipelines and export terminals, clean transport, federal climate rules) and half lands and waters, clean air and water and the outdoors programmes. The Foundation then moved its year end to June; its six-month return for January to June 2025 is left out as a short year.

### How the impact happens

The Sierra Club's organisers, lawyers and volunteers argue before utility commissions, comment on and defend federal rules, sue, and run campaigns in most states. The models count the wins of 2022-2024 that can be put on the site's scale:

- **US coal plants closed in 2022-2024** (26 gigawatts, EIA). Advocacy is credited with 15% of the closings; most were utilities' economic choices. Beyond Coal, the largest campaign against coal plants, gets 40% of that advocacy.
- **EPA's 2023 methane rule, its 2024 power-plant and vehicle standards, and California's Advanced Clean Cars II.** The site's shared tonnes, status and steps for each rule. The Sierra Club gets 5%, 10%, 8% and 8% of the advocacy behind them.
- **EPA's 2024 soot standard**, about 3,000 deaths a year avoided once in effect. The Sierra Club gets 4% for its own comments and members.
- **Inspiring Connections Outdoors**, 5,127 people a year on nature trips, counted as a small gain in life satisfaction.

The Foundation is credited with 70% of the Sierra Club's part: it pays for the charitable campaigns, and the Sierra Club's own money is counted among the other groups' resources.

### How the model works

Each win is a branch: its tonnes or deaths a year once in effect, the share due to advocacy, the years a won reform counts (shared) after its own wait, what would have happened anyway, the Sierra Club's and the Foundation's shares, and how much of it is delivered (the federal rules' shared delivered shares already allow for their repeals; 85% of coal closings stay closed despite emergency orders keeping some plants open). The coal figures are shared facts. Tonnes become healthy years through the site's one climate conversion, with coal and traffic smoke and the methane rule's local smog added. The sum is divided by an average year's spending in 2024 prices, then by the Foundation's next-dollar factor ({{row:marginal_vs_past_average}}).

### What drives the result

The coal closings are about four fifths of the climate model's tonnes; the federal rules count little because most have been repealed or weakened. The years a won reform counts, the missing-data discount and advocacy's share of the closings move it most. The lands and health model rests almost entirely on the soot standard: advocacy's share of it, how much is delivered and EPA's deaths estimate.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much the closings owe to advocacy.** Cheap gas and renewables retired most plants; Beyond Coal's own count (389 plants retired or committed since 2010) names closings, not its share.
- **The split of the Foundation's grants.** Its accounts don't split the grants outside Beyond Coal by campaign; the 50% climate share is our judgment.
- **The Foundation's share of the Sierra Club.** It granted half the Sierra Club's 2024 spending, more of the campaign work; we use 70% (50% to 90%).
- **The next dollar.** The Foundation ran a deficit in early 2025 and the Sierra Club has been through layoffs and a leadership crisis.

### What isn't modelled

Gas pipelines, export terminals and other climate work are a 50% share on top of the climate wins; the lands wins (national monuments, the Public Lands Rule and Western Arctic leasing limits, mostly rescinded in 2025, and species protections), clean-water cases and chapters' local work are a 30% share on the soot standard. The Sierra Club's opposition to nuclear power counts as a small harm.

### How this model was checked

Each model had an independent audit, and both passed with no high findings. What changed after them:

- **Climate:** the Our Wild America lands grants of 2022 and 2023 were taken out of the climate split (+10%), and the shared coal figures got their sources. The advocacy share of closings is now anchored on Coglianese, Gerarden & Stock (2020).
- **Lands and health:** Our Wild America was counted whole, which raised the cost. The Sierra Club's share of the soot standard was cut from 6% to 4%, since Earthjustice was its lawyer in court. Volunteers' time now counts as others' resources behind the outdoors trips, and the other-work share was re-anchored.

A scoped verification of these changes passed for both models.
