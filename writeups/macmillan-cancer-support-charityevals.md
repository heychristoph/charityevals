### Summary

Macmillan Cancer Support is the UK's largest cancer support charity. Our model of all its charitable work in 2025 comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="macmillan-cancer-support-charityevals">0.002</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**, under a hundredth of the global-health median on the site.

The result is low because of what the help is. Most people get light support: a call or two to the Support Line, or reading the Online Community and the website. They already have NHS care. The biggest gain in money is the welfare benefits Macmillan's advisers help people claim, identified at about £8,500 a year for each of 13,200 households, of which we count about £5,100 as awarded. That money is paid by other UK taxpayers, who are only somewhat richer than the claimants, so on the site's log-income scale the net gain is about a third of the claimants' own gain. Over two thirds of the result comes from welfare rights, net of that cost, and about a tenth from grants. Every branch but the grants (audited money paid) is counted at half (the site's shared slider for results nobody measured), since none of Macmillan's services has a controlled evaluation.

### What the charity does

In the year to 31 December 2025 Macmillan committed £112.1m to charitable work in the year (£104.2m in its statement, after £11.7m of old grants released unspent and with £3.8m of discounting added), paid £56.6m in grants on this and earlier years' commitments, and spent £70.9m on raising £268.4m, 99% of it from the public. Its spending fell from £150.5m in 2024 as it moved from funding NHS posts to a new strategy. The work splits into five areas:

- **Service Offer for All** (£71.6m): the Support Line, with cancer information nurses (35,000 people), information and support advisers (38,000) and welfare rights advisers (34,000 people helped, £112m of potential benefits found for 13,200); the Online Community (350,000 people with cancer); information centres, the website and print; 3,800 people supported by volunteer Buddies; and £1.4m of grants to individuals (hardship grants, and a pilot of one-off £350 grants to 500 people in the North East). In all, 476,000 people with cancer were supported.
- **System Transformation** (£17.4m): social investment in proactive community care for frail older people (three Neighbourhood Transformation Funds, £23.2m committed), end-of-life care services, Improving the Cancer Journey in Scotland, and advocacy on national cancer plans.
- **Professional Engagement** (£13.5m): learning, conferences and development grants for 11,100 Macmillan professionals, whose posts the NHS pays for.
- **Communities and Participation** (£5.3m): grants to community groups and Cancer Champions.
- **Data, Evidence and Research** (£4.3m): needs data, evaluation of its own services and research grants on personalised care.

### How the impact happens

1. **Support**: information and emotional support ease the anxiety and low mood that come with a diagnosis, for a few months.
2. **Money**: benefits and grants raise the household's income while the illness lasts.
3. **Better care**: professionals with better training and tools give better personalised care, and funded community services reach people earlier.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's template for social services in rich countries. Each branch has its own effect and adjustments, and is put into healthy years by the site's shared conversions:

- **Cost**: the year's direct and support costs in all five areas plus grants paid in cash (£138.1m, with the £1.4m of grants to individuals). Grants are counted as paid, not as committed, because services running in 2025 were partly paid for by grants booked in earlier years. Fundraising is spread over it (×1.52). Volunteers' 103,000 hours barely lower Macmillan's share of the credit.
- **Support Line, Buddies, online**: the effect is anchored on two sources. Telephone support programmes for people with cancer reduce anxiety by about 0.16 standard deviations (meta-analysis of 14 trials). Online support groups improved depression a little, on very low-quality evidence (Cochrane). We count 0.08 SD for a Support Line contact, 0.12 for a course of Buddies calls and 0.02 for online or print only. Each fades over a few months. Scaled by the site's WELLBYs per SD-year, trusted at 70%, with 60% not available elsewhere (NHS nurses, other helplines, Maggie's).
- **Welfare rights**: the £112m found is taken as a year's benefits. 60% is awarded, it lasts 1.5 years, and half wouldn't have been claimed otherwise. That gives about £5,100 a year per household, close to the £70 a week measured for a Macmillan-funded service in County Durham. It is valued on the log scale at claimants' consumption, 65% of the UK average per person (the poorest fifth of households spend 60% of the average). The taxpayers' cost is subtracted in the same branch. It is valued at national-accounts consumption, with a marginal cost of public funds of 1.2 and UK taxes taken as mildly progressive.
- **Grants**: £1.4m in grants of about £350, valued the same way for one year. This is audited money paid, so it isn't discounted for missing data.
- **Professionals**: 11,100 professionals, about 100 patients each. For 3% of those patients, care is better by 0.1 SD for half a year. 60% of that wouldn't have come from NHS training anyway.
- **Posts paid for by grants**: about half of the £56.6m of grants paid went on the first years of NHS posts committed before the new strategy, about £60,000 a post-year. Each post's patients get specialist care against none, at the same effect as above, for the 40% of posts that wouldn't have existed in those years without Macmillan.
- **System and communities**: people reached are spending over a cost per person. For system work that is about £1,000. The anchor is Improving the Cancer Journey's link officers, roughly £250-400 a person in Glasgow, with end-of-life care dearer. The Neighbourhood Transformation Funds' social investments are loans, not in this spending. For community grants it is about £300. Small wellbeing gains, with NHS partners' share of the credit and a year's wait counted.
- **Data, evidence and research**: counted as a cost of the services. Its value is in better services of the kind modelled.

### What drives the result

- **How much we count a programme whose results aren't measured**, a shared slider: every branch moves one for one with it.
- **How poor the claimants are** (45-90% of average consumption), **how long benefits last**, **how many would have claimed anyway** and **how much is awarded**: together they swing the result by up to two or three times either way.
- **How the taxes behind the benefits fall** (0.85-1.5 times proportional) and **the marginal cost of public funds** (1-1.5): they set how much of the claimants' gain the taxpayers' cost takes back.
- **The exchange rate between healthy years and income**, a shared slider.

### Biggest uncertainties

- What the £112m of "potential" benefits turns into: awards, their length, and how many would have been claimed without help.
- The effect of light support: the trials behind it tested structured programmes, mostly with women with breast cancer, not single calls or reading a forum.
- The system transformation work is new, and nothing is published yet on its reach or outcomes.

### What isn't modelled

Carers and families helped by the services; suicides averted; the energy advice team's savings; earlier presentation or better treatment adherence from information; advocacy on cancer plans; social investments repaid and reinvested; hospital admissions averted. Against them, NHS posts or services commissioners skip because Macmillan exists. Together these are put at about +5% (-3% to +15%) of the gross value, before the taxpayers' cost.

### How this model was checked

Built from the 2025 audited accounts and the annual report's service counts, then audited independently (rounds in audits/macmillan-cancer-support-charityevals/). Round 1 failed on how the taxpayers' cost of the benefits was valued: it is now in the welfare rights branch at national-accounts consumption with a marginal cost of public funds, as in the site's other model of public money. It also moved grants to a cash basis (−12%), anchored the claimants' consumption on UK data, re-anchored the system branch on Improving the Cancer Journey, counted grants as measured and gave the professionals a counterfactual. Round 2 passed; after it the posts that the grants paid for got a branch, the marginal cost of public funds became a shared fact (facts/public_funds.json, also read by Habitat for Humanity's awareness model), grants to individuals joined the cash cost and the unmodelled share moved onto the gross value; verification round 3 passed, and its three wording and small fixes (the posts branch's own missing-data step and wait) were checked in round 4.
