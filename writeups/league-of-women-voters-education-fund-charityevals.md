### Summary

The League of Women Voters Education Fund runs VOTE411.org, registers voters, brings voting-rights lawsuits and campaigns for fairer US elections. Our model of its year to June 2025 comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="league-of-women-voters-education-fund-charityevals">0.013</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. That is about a fifth of the site's global-health median and about half our figure for the Common Cause Education Fund, because most of the League's money goes to voter information, which we count as acting on democracy less directly than litigation. It is a very uncertain figure, built mostly on judgment.

### What the charity does

The League of Women Voters is a nonpartisan civic organisation founded in 1920, with more than 700 state and local Leagues. It has two national arms. The League of Women Voters of the United States is a 501(c)(4): it lobbies, and gifts to it aren't tax-deductible. The Education Fund is its 501(c)(3), and this model covers only the Education Fund.

In the year to June 2025 the Education Fund spent $11.3 million of its own money. Its accounts also record $5.4 million of donated legal services and advertising. Those are the donors' resources, not the Education Fund's spending, so we leave them out of the cost. Its programme spending, without the donated services, was:

- mission impact, $4.7 million: VOTE411.org (7.6 million users in the year), voter registration (482,000 voters registered) and $0.8 million of grants to state and local Leagues;
- communications, $2.0 million;
- advocacy, $0.7 million: lobbying and the League's litigation team (the lawsuits themselves are argued mostly by donated counsel);
- external affairs, council and convention, outcome and evaluation, and member services, $1.0 million together.

### How the impact happens

The work does good in two ways. First, it helps defend US democracy against a slide toward a hybrid regime. It does this directly through lawsuits, such as the 2025 suit that blocked the executive order requiring proof of citizenship to register, and less directly by informing and registering voters. Second, it campaigns for reforms within the democracy: citizens' redistricting commissions (People Powered Fair Maps), easier voting, abolishing the Electoral College, and DC statehood.

### How the model works

The model has four branches. Each counts what the whole field or movement achieved in a year, takes the Education Fund's share of the money behind it, and values better government as income.

- **Direct work against a slide (about 31% of the result).** This branch reads the same shared figures as our Common Cause, Brennan Center and Protect Democracy models. Forecasters give the US an 11% chance of losing its democracy rating before 2030, and a year of the whole pro-democracy field's work cuts that chance by about a twentieth. A slide would cost a share of democracy's long-run income gain, built up over 25 years. We judge 15% of the programme to be direct work. That is less than Common Cause's 35%, because the League's lawsuits are argued mostly by donated counsel, whose work isn't the League's spending.
- **Voter information and registration (about 32%).** This is 71% of the programme. We don't count it per user or per vote. We count it through what it changes: like the field's other turnout drives and civic education, it lowers the chance of a slide, at the shared weight of a fifth of a direct dollar. Its effect on who wins elections in ordinary times is a side effect, which we judge at a tenth of the branch. Nonpartisan turnout gains change little: universal mail voting, for example, changed neither party's share.
- **Redistricting reform (about 22%).** Since 2008, about 79 million Americans have come under commission-drawn maps, and state Leagues were in most of those coalitions. We judge 4% of the programme goes to this. That gives the Education Fund 2% of the movement's money.
- **Other reforms (about 14%).** Voting access, the Electoral College and DC statehood count through any change in who is elected or what is decided. We judge a dollar of this work worth a quarter of a dollar of the redistricting movement, as in our Common Cause model.

The side effects are shared figures. In the two slide branches they are democracy's value beyond income, less repression, and US democracy's effect on democracy abroad; the direct branch also has backlash. In the reform branches they are fairer elections valued beyond income, and the counter-measures that reforms provoke. With its next dollar at 52.5% of the past average, the result is <span data-figure="headline" data-model="league-of-women-voters-education-fund-charityevals">0.013</span>. The next-dollar figure reflects about six months of unrestricted reserves and two years of operating deficits.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts move the result most, then the missing-data discount, the next-dollar figure and the size of democracy's income gain. After those come the field's effect on the risk of a slide, and the weight of a voter-information dollar against a direct one.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether a year of the whole field's work changes the chance of a slide at all, and by how much. No study measures it.
- What voter information is worth. The fifth of a direct dollar is the field's shared judgment, not a measured effect, and it carries a third of the result.
- How the Education Fund's spending splits between the four kinds of work. Its accounts don't split it by issue, so all four shares are our judgments.

### What isn't modelled

- The lobbying of the League of Women Voters of the United States, the 501(c)(4).
- The value of the donated legal work as the Education Fund's own. It counts in the field's resources, so the law firms get their share of the credit.

### How this model was checked

An independent auditor reviews the model in rounds; the findings and fixes of each round are in audits/league-of-women-voters-education-fund-charityevals/.
