### What the charity does

The League of Women Voters is a nonpartisan civic organisation founded in 1920, with more than 700 state and local Leagues. It has two national arms. The League of Women Voters of the United States is a 501(c)(4): it lobbies, and gifts to it aren't tax-deductible. The Education Fund is its 501(c)(3), and this model covers only the Education Fund.

In the year to June 2025 the Education Fund spent $11.3 million of its own money. Its accounts also record $5.4 million of donated legal services and advertising. Those are the donors' resources, not the Education Fund's spending, so we leave them out of the cost. Its programme spending, without the donated services, was:

- mission impact, $4.7 million: VOTE411.org (7.6 million users in the year), voter registration (482,000 voters registered) and $0.8 million of grants to state and local Leagues;
- communications, $2.0 million;
- advocacy, $0.7 million: lobbying and the League's litigation team (the lawsuits themselves are argued mostly by donated counsel);
- external affairs, council and convention, outcome and evaluation, and member services, $1.0 million together.

### How the impact happens

The work does good in two ways. First, it helps defend US democracy against a slide toward a hybrid regime. It does this directly through lawsuits, such as the 2025 suit that blocked the executive order requiring proof of citizenship to register, and less directly by informing and registering voters. Second, it campaigns for reforms within the democracy: citizens' redistricting commissions (People Powered Fair Maps), easier voting, abolishing the Electoral College, and DC statehood.

### How the model works

The model has four branches. Each counts what the whole field or movement achieved in a year, takes the Education Fund's share of the money behind it, and values better government as income.

- **Direct work against a slide (about 36% of the result).** Forecasters give the US an 11% chance of losing its democracy rating before 2030, and a year of the whole pro-democracy field's work cuts that chance by about a twentieth. A slide would cost a share of democracy's long-run income gain, built up over 25 years. We judge 15% of the programme to be direct work: the League's lawsuits are argued mostly by donated counsel, whose work isn't the League's spending.
- **Voter information and registration (about 36%).** This is 73% of the programme. We don't count it per user or per vote. Like the field's other turnout drives and civic education, it counts through what it changes in elections, at the shared weight of a fifth of a direct dollar against a slide. We don't credit it a second time for changing who is elected: that is how it acts on a slide, and each dollar counts in one chain.
- **Redistricting reform (about 13%).** Since 2008, about 79 million Americans have come under commission-drawn maps, and state Leagues were in most of those coalitions. We judge 2% of the programme goes to this, mostly national support for state campaigns such as Ohio's in 2024. That gives the Education Fund 1% of the movement's money. Its redistricting lawsuits count with its other litigation, in the direct branch.
- **Other reforms (about 16%).** Voting access, the Electoral College and DC statehood count through any change in who is elected or what is decided. We judge a dollar of this work worth a quarter of a dollar of the redistricting movement.

The side effects are shared figures. In the two slide branches they are democracy's value beyond income, less repression, and US democracy's effect on democracy abroad; the direct branch also has backlash. In the reform branches they are fairer elections valued beyond income, and the counter-measures that reforms provoke. With its next dollar at 52.5% of the past average, the result is {{result}}. The next-dollar figure reflects about six months of unrestricted reserves and two years of operating deficits.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts and the missing-data discount move the result most, then the field's effect on the risk of a slide, then the size of democracy's income gain and the next-dollar figure. After those come the value of a healthy year against a doubled income, the chance of a slide by 2030, the discount rate and the redistricting gain share.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether a year of the whole field's work changes the chance of a slide at all, and by how much. No study measures it.
- What voter information is worth. The fifth of a direct dollar is the field's shared judgment, not a measured effect, and it carries a third of the result.
- How the Education Fund's spending splits between the four kinds of work. Its accounts don't split it by issue, so all four shares are our judgments.

### What isn't modelled

- The lobbying of the League of Women Voters of the United States, the 501(c)(4).
- The value of the donated legal work as the Education Fund's own. It counts in the field's resources, so the law firms get their share of the credit.

### How this model was checked

An independent auditor reviews the model in rounds; the findings and fixes of each round are in audits/league-of-women-voters-education-fund-charityevals/. Round 5 removed voter information's second credit, for who is elected.
