### Summary

The RepresentUs Education Fund explains corruption and threats to US democracy to large online audiences, and backs ballot measures for ranked-choice voting, independent redistricting commissions and anti-corruption laws. Our model of its 2024 work comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="representus-education-fund-charityevals">0.026</span> years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. That is about two fifths of the site's global-health median, close to our models of Common Cause and FairVote. It is a very uncertain figure, built mostly on judgment: how much a voting reform changes how well people are governed, and how the Education Fund's spending divides between its kinds of work.

### What the charity does

RepresentUs is a nonpartisan anti-corruption and democracy-reform group. It has two arms. Represent.Us is a 501(c)(4): it lobbies and runs campaigns, and gifts to it aren't tax-deductible. The Education Fund is its 501(c)(3), and gifts to it are deductible. This model covers only the Education Fund, which is most of the organisation: $7.8 million of the two arms' $8.1 million spending in 2024, counting the grant between them once. Its programmes in 2024, from its audited accounts:

- education and communications, $2.9 million: videos and social media reaching about 30 million viewers, a public-service campaign on AI deepfakes in seven swing states, and education on the ballot measures it backed;
- other projects, $1.2 million: ethics and accountability projects and the American Democracy Summit;
- grants to Represent.Us, $0.5 million: texts and calls for ballot measures, counted as the work they fund;
- research and planning, $0.06 million.

Management and fundraising cost another $3.0 million, a high share: total costs are 1.64 times programme costs. The Fund ended 2024 with a $5.3 million debt to Represent.Us and $0.4 million free to spend, so money is tight.

### How the impact happens

In 2024 RepresentUs backed 21 campaigns. Eight were to pass ranked-choice voting or other voting reforms, and three defended ranked-choice voting or open primaries (Alaska's repeal measure failed by fewer than 700 votes). Two were against gerrymandering: Los Angeles voted for an independent city commission, and Ohio's measure failed. Two were anti-corruption measures, in Maine and Ann Arbor. Some of its education aims directly at threats to democracy, such as the deepfake campaign.

### How the model works

The model has four branches. Each counts what the whole field or movement achieved in an average year, takes the Education Fund's share of the money behind it, and values better government as income. The branches use the same shared figures as our FairVote, Common Cause and Protect Democracy models, so credit for a shared win is divided the same way in each.

- **Ranked-choice voting (about a third of the result).** The US movement for ranked-choice voting added about 1.4 million people a year to ranked ballots over 2016-2025, spending about $19 million a year. We judge that the Education Fund spent 36% of its programme on this work, which makes it 14% of the movement's resources, counting the time of its volunteers, who sent 6.7 million texts and calls in 2024, among everyone else's. Ranked-choice voting is judged to bring 2% of democracy's long-run income gain, as in the FairVote model.
- **Redistricting (about a quarter).** About 79 million Americans have come under commission-drawn maps since 2008, with a movement spending about $22 million a year. The Education Fund's share is 3%. Each commission is judged to bring 1% of democracy's income gain, as in the Common Cause model.
- **Other reforms (about a fifth).** Anti-corruption laws, money in politics and ethics rules count through any change in who is elected or what is decided. We judge a dollar of this work worth a quarter of a dollar of the redistricting movement, as in the Common Cause model.
- **A slide averted (about a fifth).** Forecasters give the US an 11% chance of losing its democracy rating before 2030, and a year of the whole pro-democracy field's work cuts that chance by about a twentieth. We judge 20% of the programme to be direct work of this kind. That is a small share of the field's money, about 0.06%.

Each branch has side effects, each a shared figure: fairer elections valued beyond income, backlash and bans, and in the slide branch democracy's value beyond income, less repression and US democracy's effect abroad. With its next dollar at 49% of the past average, the result is <span data-figure="headline" data-model="representus-education-fund-charityevals">0.026</span>. The next dollar goes to new national work (pressing Congress on checks on power, from 2025) and the Fund changed its chief executive in 2025, but money is clearly short: its audited accounts say it can go on only if it raises unrestricted money and cuts costs.

### What drives the result

The years a won reform counts move the result most, then the missing-data discount, the size of democracy's income gain and the next-dollar figure. After those come the gain shares judged for redistricting and ranked-choice voting.

### Biggest uncertainties

- What a reform within a democracy is worth. The gain shares for ranked-choice voting and redistricting are judgments, anchored on studies of who is elected and of maps, not on measured effects on income.
- How the Education Fund's spending divides between its kinds of work. The accounts split it by activity (education, grants, projects), not by reform, so the split is our judgment from its campaign list and its restricted gifts.
- Whether a year of the whole field's work changes the chance of a slide at all. No study measures it.

### What isn't modelled

- Represent.Us's own spending ($0.3 million in 2024 beyond the $0.5 million grant it received from the Fund), because gifts to it aren't tax-deductible.
- Los Angeles's 2024 city commission, as a win of its own. The shared redistricting figures count state commissions and the money behind state campaigns, for every model alike; Los Angeles's measure was put on the ballot by the city council, not by a petition campaign the movement's money paid for, so the Fund's spending on it is credited at the state movement's rate.

### How this model was checked

An independent auditor reviews the model in rounds; the findings and fixes of each round are in audits/representus-education-fund-charityevals/.
