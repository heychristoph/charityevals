### Summary

The Iodine Global Network (IGN) helps governments and salt producers keep salt iodized, so that children are not born and raised short of iodine. Our model counts what its 2025 work achieved: **{{result}} years of healthy life per 1,000 dollars**. The figure is very uncertain: it rests on judgments of what each piece of work changed and on how much mild iodine deficiency harms a child's development.

### What the charity does

IGN, formerly ICCIDD, is a small organisation with a core team of seven and a network of 11 regional and 140 national coordinators, most of them working in governments, universities and hospitals. It spent US$1.32 million in 2025. It doesn't make salt or run programmes itself: it gives technical help to salt producers and inspectors, reviews countries' iodization laws and standards, builds and tests tools for checking how a country's programme works (its Program Assessment Tool, sentinel surveillance, a processed-food assessment), brings countries together in regional meetings, and publishes research.

### How the impact happens

The brain is most sensitive to iodine in the womb and in the first years of life. A child whose mother lacks iodine in pregnancy, or who lacks it as an infant, scores lower on cognitive tests and, later, earns less; deficiency also causes goitre. Iodized salt is the cheapest way to prevent this, but it only works if producers add enough iodine, the salt in shops and processed food is iodized, and governments keep the rules and check them. IGN's work is on those links.

### How the model works

- **Cost:** the average of IGN's 2024 and 2025 spending in 2025 prices, as the results of 2025 build on both years.
- **Value of a child protected:** 7.5 IQ points lost to iodine deficiency (a meta-analysis of trials and cohorts), halved because the deficiency at stake is mostly mild to moderate; 0.67% higher earnings per IQ point (GiveWell's figure) for 40 working years starting about 15 years after birth, shared within the household and discounted. Goitre averted (0.02 healthy years per child) and hyperthyroidism caused in older adults (a side effect, −0.001) are counted in healthy years.
- **Six pieces of work:**
  - **Lebanon:** the three main producers back to standard by early 2025; half the supply restored, half of the children reached otherwise deficient, the gain lasting about three years.
  - **Producer quality assurance:** small producers supplying 11.3 million people in Bolivia, a fifth of northern Argentina's 10 million, a tenth of Madagascar and 3% of Mozambique and Peru; a tenth of these households move to adequately iodized salt, the gain lasting about two years.
  - **Vietnam:** the Ministry of Health recommended keeping iodized salt compulsory in processed foods, which carry most of the salt people eat; the amending decree is still being drafted, so a 60% chance the final text keeps it and a 30% chance that, without IGN's evidence, the recommendation would have gone the other way (both single events), half the foods complying.
  - **Davao City:** a restarted salt task force with a multi-year enforcement plan; a twentieth of the city's births moved to adequate salt if the plan is carried out.
  - **Policy reviews and tools** in the 19 countries using IGN's toolkit (India, Germany, the Maldives, South Sudan, Bhutan, Cameroon, Sri Lanka, Armenia, Tanzania, Moldova and nine unnamed): about one new rule every two years the size of Moldova's requirement of iodized salt in bread (in force since 2011, re-issued in 2025 after IGN's processed-food assessment), which protects about 12,700 births a year.
  - **Regional advocacy** in Europe, South Asia and North America: about one such rule every four years.
- **Credit:** IGN gets 20% to 40% of each result, as UNICEF, WHO, ministries, universities and the producers themselves put in resources too; each result counts for the years a won change counts on the site, and is discounted by the shared missing-data slider, as no change in children's iodine status is measured.
- **The next dollar** is valued at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of the average: money is tight after aid cuts, but the 2025 accounts close with a surplus.

### What drives the result

The years a won change counts, the discount rate and the missing-data slider move the result most; of the model's own judgments, the new rules the policy reviews and tools lead to and how much milder deficiency costs in IQ.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Mild deficiency:** the meta-analysis's studies are mostly of moderate to severe deficiency; daily iodine for mildly deficient pregnant women in India and Thailand made no difference to their children's IQ. We take half the loss; a quarter or nine tenths are as defensible.
- **What a year's work moves:** no study measures how many children's iodine status changed because of IGN; the new rules and shares of births protected are our judgments from what the annual report describes.
- **Vietnam's decree:** the final text decides about a quarter of the result.

### How this model was checked

Audit rounds are listed in audits/iodine-global-network-charityevals/. Round 1 found three high, four medium and five low issues. The model now counts the regional advocacy in Europe, South Asia and North America and all 19 toolkit countries; asks its counterfactual rows only whether a change would have come within the years counted; counts illness in healthy years with no wait, and no deaths; caps producer support at two or three years; treats Vietnam's decree as a recommendation still to be decided; and counts the policy reviews in Moldova-sized wins rather than a share of all births. Round 2 found one high, two medium and four low issues: Moldova's bread rule dates from 2011 (the 2025 decree re-issued it), so the wins are now new rules of that size at one every two years for the reviews and tools and one every four for the advocacy; illness no longer takes the earnings trust row, and the hyperthyroidism harm is halved for work that restores or keeps intake.
