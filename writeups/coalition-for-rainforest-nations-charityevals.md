### Summary

The Coalition for Rainforest Nations (CfRN) works with rainforest countries in the UN climate talks and helps them get paid for cutting deforestation. Our model of its 2024 spending comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="coalition-for-rainforest-nations-charityevals">0.0003</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**.

- A small cut in the chance of a new pandemic, from tropical forest kept standing, is most of the good. It is the least grounded step in the model.
- The forest's carbon is worth little in healthy years, as everywhere on the site.
- Selling forest credits can do harm. A buyer that counts a credit against its own target can emit a tonne more. At our best guesses, the credits sold outnumber the real tonnes the money keeps, so the carbon is a small net loss.

The result runs from net harm to small gains. Both branches take the site's missing-data discount: no one measures the forest CfRN's work keeps.

### What the charity does

CfRN's founders proposed the idea of paying countries to reduce deforestation (REDD+), which Papua New Guinea and Costa Rica brought to the UN climate talks in 2004. It sits at the table in the talks as a recognised party group of its member countries. Its 2024 Impact Statement describes three kinds of work:

- **The climate talks.** At COP29 in 2024 it negotiated for the new climate-finance goal and for the completion of the carbon-market rules of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.
- **Data and training.** It trains national forestry teams to measure their forest and report their results to the UN. It worked with 15 countries in 2024, including Honduras, whose 6.8 million tonnes of results for 2016-2020 the UN verified that year.
- **National implementation.** It drafts carbon-market laws, builds registries and markets credits, in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Honduras, Mozambique and Suriname in 2024. Suriname offered the first 1.5 million forest credits under Article 6 that year.

In 2024 it spent $4.88 million: $4.05 million on its one reported programme, $0.80 million on management and $24,000 on fundraising (Form 990). The return says its statements were not audited.

### How the impact happens

Money paid for results gives a country a reason to keep its forest standing. CfRN's work helps that money flow in two ways:

1. **Results-based payments.** Donors such as Norway, Germany, the Green Climate Fund and the World Bank pay countries for verified cuts in deforestation. A country can only claim them once it can measure and report its forest. CfRN helps it do that.
2. **Carbon markets.** Under Article 6, a rainforest country can sell its results as credits to other countries and companies. CfRN helped write the rules and helps first sellers get ready.

The second way has a catch. A buyer that counts the credit against its own target can cut a tonne less at home. If the credit is a real extra tonne kept in the forest, the two cancel. If it isn't, the world emits more.

### How the model works

The [model](/coalition-for-rainforest-nations/coalition-for-rainforest-nations-charityevals) follows the site's conservation template, with two branches set against the same cost: all $4.05 million of programme spending plus overhead, $4.88 million in all. We judge that half the work serves each branch.

**What payments buy.** The one evaluation of a national programme like this is Norway's offer of $250 million to Guyana for 2010-2015. Roopsind et al. (2019) found it cut tree-cover loss by 35%, at $19.53 a tonne of CO₂. We then take:

- half the forest kept as kept for good (Guyana's loss rose after the payments ended);
- 20% of the clearing avoided as moved elsewhere;
- half of Guyana's effect for other countries' payments, many of which reward results years after the fact.

**Results-based payments.** Donors committed about $380 million a year from 2010 to 2021 (Forest Declaration Assessment 2022). Readiness grants and the forest countries' own budgets add about $2.60 for each dollar paid. CfRN's share of all those resources is about 0.15%.

**Carbon markets.** We judge the money paid for forest credits under Article 6 at $200 million a year once the market works, at $20 a credit, as in Hess's purchase from Guyana. The money keeps forest at the same rate as donors' payments. We judge that half the credits let buyers emit more. CfRN's share of the market's resources is about 0.3%. The market's size cancels out: a bigger market means more tonnes and a smaller share for CfRN.

The buyers' extra emissions are fossil fuel, so they carry smoke as well as heat. We count their smoke at 2.5% of the world average per tonne: buyers are rich countries and airlines, with cleaner plants and much fuel burnt at altitude.

**Pandemic risk.** The forest kept cuts the risk by the same shared figures as the site's other forest models.

**The next dollar.** The result is CfRN's share of an average year's resources, so the next dollar is valued at 64% of the average: CfRN has run deficits for two years and holds under a year of spending, and more money takes the same work to more countries, where no forest credit has yet been sold.

### What drives the result

The biggest swings are:

- how far Guyana's result holds for other countries' payments;
- how much of the pandemic risk from land use scales with forest loss;
- how much of the forest kept stays standing;
- the cost per tonne;
- how much smoke the buyers' extra emissions carry, and how many credits replace buyers' own cuts.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether payments for past results change behaviour.** Guyana's contract rewarded each year's restraint; most payments CfRN's countries claim reward results years old, against reference levels that may be set high.
- **How credits are used.** If buyers would have met their targets anyway, every non-additional credit adds a tonne. If they buy on top of their own cuts, there is no harm.
- **CfRN's share.** We credit it its share of all the money behind the forest kept. A reader who thinks its role in the talks and in getting countries paid is worth more than its spending can move the attribution rows.

### What isn't modelled

- The new climate-finance goal agreed at COP29 ($300 billion a year by 2035), of which forests get an unknown part.
- The integrity rules for Article 6 that CfRN pressed for, and countries' use of their new greenhouse-gas inventories for their national climate pledges.
- Donors shifting money from results-based grants to buying credits.

These sit in one row for effects not modelled, at zero net (−20% to +20%).
