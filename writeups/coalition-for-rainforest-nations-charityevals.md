### Summary

The Coalition for Rainforest Nations (CfRN) works with rainforest countries in the UN climate talks and helps them get paid for cutting deforestation. Our model of its 2024 spending comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**: a small net harm, close to zero. The result is mostly uncertainty: in our simulation it is harmful in about three draws of five.

- Nearly all of it is CfRN's small share of the climate-finance goal agreed at COP29. Some climate finance is aid moved from health, education and other development work into cutting emissions, which is worth far less in healthy years. At our best guesses the aid lost slightly outweighs the new aid for adaptation and the emissions cut, with the cleaner air that comes with them.
- Whether the goal's money is new aid decides the sign. If about half of it were new, the result would be zero; more, and it would be positive.
- The forest CfRN's work helps keep standing adds a little: a small cut in pandemic risk, and carbon, which is worth little in healthy years, as everywhere on the site.
- Selling forest credits can do harm too. A country that counts a credit against its own target can emit a tonne more. At our best guesses the credit sales are a small net harm.

Every branch takes the site's missing-data discount: no one measures what CfRN's work changes.

### What the charity does

CfRN's founders proposed the idea of paying countries to reduce deforestation (REDD+), which Papua New Guinea and Costa Rica brought to the UN climate talks in 2004. It sits at the table in the talks as a recognised party group of its member countries. Its 2024 Impact Statement describes three kinds of work:

- **The climate talks.** At COP29 in 2024 it negotiated for the new climate-finance goal ($300 billion a year for developing countries by 2035) and for the completion of the carbon-market rules of Article 6 of the Paris Agreement.
- **Data and training.** It trains national forestry teams to measure their forest and report their results to the UN. It worked with 15 countries in 2024, including Honduras, whose 6.8 million tonnes of results for 2016-2020 the UN verified that year.
- **National implementation.** It drafts carbon-market laws, builds registries and readies countries to sell credits: the Democratic Republic of Congo, Honduras, Mozambique and Suriname in 2024.

In 2024 it spent $4.88 million: $4.05 million on its one reported programme, $0.80 million on management and $24,000 on fundraising (Form 990). The return says its statements were not audited.

### How the impact happens

1. **The climate-finance goal.** More money for developing countries' climate projects, if donors deliver. Money for adaptation works like other aid. Money for cutting emissions buys tonnes. Money moved from other aid loses what that aid would have done.
2. **Results-based payments.** Donors such as Norway, Germany, the Green Climate Fund and the World Bank pay countries for verified cuts in deforestation. A country can only claim them once it can measure and report its forest; CfRN helps it do that. Money paid for results gives a country a reason to keep its forest standing.
3. **Carbon markets.** Under Article 6 a rainforest country can sell its results as credits. The money works like donors' payments. But a buyer that counts the credit against its own target can cut a tonne less at home: if the credit isn't a real extra tonne kept, the world emits more.

### How the model works

The [model](/coalition-for-rainforest-nations/coalition-for-rainforest-nations-charityevals) follows the site's conservation template, with three branches set against the same cost: all $4.05 million of programme spending plus overhead, $4.88 million in all. We judge that 45% of the work goes to measuring results, a tenth to the finance talks and the rest to carbon markets.

**The finance goal.** The goal raises the target from $100 billion to $300 billion a year by 2035. We take:

- half of that rise as not coming anyway: a new goal was required from a $100 billion floor, and the presidency's draft of 22 November 2024 already offered $250 billion;
- 15% of the rise in finance as the goal's own doing (the $100 billion goal was met two years late);
- 40% of the money as worth a grant (the goal counts loans and private money at face value), and half of it as delivered;
- the money in 2024 dollars (prices about a quarter higher by 2035);
- 35% for adaptation, valued as aid; the rest for cutting emissions, at $10 a tonne over the projects' 20-year lives (about three times the Green Climate Fund's own projection), with the smoke the fuel would have made;
- 40% as new aid (CARE found 7% to 48% of climate finance new, by its strict and weak tests). Of the rest, 40% is existing aid relabelled as climate finance, which changes nothing, and 60% is aid moved to new uses, which loses the value of that aid.

The win is counted like every won policy on the site: the advocates' shared part of a won climate policy (30%), the site's years a win counts, discounted to 2035, and one of the three years of talks per year of work. CfRN's share of the credit is its spending on the talks over all advocates' and support groups' spending on them: about half a percent, at our judgment of $80 million a year in all.

**What payments buy.** The one evaluation of a national programme like this is Norway's offer of $250 million to Guyana for 2010-2015. Roopsind et al. (2019) found it cut tree-cover loss by 35%, at $19.53 a tonne of CO₂, about $27 in 2024 prices. We then take:

- half the forest kept as kept for good (Guyana's loss rose after the payments ended);
- 20% of the clearing avoided as moved elsewhere;
- half of Guyana's effect for other countries' payments, many of which reward results years after the fact.

**Results-based payments.** Donors committed about $460 million a year (Forest Declaration Assessment 2022). Readiness and implementation support adds about $0.39 for each dollar paid. CfRN's share of that support is about {{row:rbf_attribution_share_shared}}; the donors who pay and the governments that protect the forest are credited through the mobiliser's share. We count {{row:rbf_counterfactual_adj}} of the payments as due to outside support at all, the site's shared figure for a won climate policy.

**Carbon markets.** CfRN's share of the support behind the forest-credit market is set the same way, about {{row:itmo_attribution_share_shared}}; the market's size cancels out, and the counterfactual is the same 30%. Credits sell at about $15. We judge that half go to countries, half of whose credits replace their own cuts at home, with the smoke those cuts would have removed. Other buyers (airlines, taxed companies, voluntary buyers) would otherwise have bought another credit or paid a tax; we count only the real cuts in the credits they would have bought, 12%.

**Pandemic risk.** The forest kept cuts the risk by the shared figures.

**The next dollar.** The result is a past average, so the next dollar is valued at 64% of it: CfRN has run deficits for two years and holds about two months of liquid reserves.

### What drives the result

The biggest swings are:

- the share of the goal's money that is new aid, and how much of the rest is only relabelled;
- the share for adaptation;
- the value of a dollar of aid;
- the cost per tonne of the mitigation, and the smoke it removes.

Each of these can turn the result from harm to good.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **Whether climate finance is new money.** If donors add it to their aid, the goal does good; if they move it from other aid, it mostly does harm.
- **CfRN's part in the finance goal.** It negotiated alongside many blocs and campaign groups. No one has measured what any one of them added.
- **Whether payments for past results change behaviour.** Guyana's contract rewarded each year's restraint; most payments CfRN's countries claim reward results years old.
- **How credits are used.** If countries would have met their targets anyway, every non-additional credit adds a tonne.

### What isn't modelled

- The integrity rules for Article 6 that CfRN pressed for, and countries' use of their new greenhouse-gas inventories for their national climate pledges.
- Donors shifting money from results-based grants to buying credits.

These sit in one row for effects not modelled, at zero net (−20% to +20%).

### How this model was checked

Built on 15 November 2026 from CfRN's 2024 Form 990 and Impact Statement. The first two rounds of the independent audit failed. After round 1 we added the climate-finance goal as its own branch, put Guyana's cost per tonne into 2024 prices and split credit buyers by what they would otherwise do. After round 2 we valued the goal's mitigation money in tonnes, counted the aid it moves from other uses, set CfRN's share from its own spending, and allowed for the goal coming anyway. Round 3 also failed: we then separated relabelled aid from aid moved, discounted the projects' tonnes over their lives, put the goal into 2024 prices, restated the cost per tonne on the Green Climate Fund's grant-equivalent basis, and made the effects not modelled count the right way round for a net harm. All rounds are in audits/coalition-for-rainforest-nations-charityevals/.
