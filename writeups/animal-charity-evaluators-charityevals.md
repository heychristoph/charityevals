### Summary

Animal Charity Evaluators (ACE) evaluates animal advocacy charities, recommends the ones it finds most effective, and passes donors' money to them. This model is of a gift for ACE's own work, not of the money that passes through it to other charities. It comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="animal-charity-evaluators-charityevals">0.0017</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**. Each dollar ACE spent led to about three dollars of giving it caused. That giving goes to animal charities, which at the site's animal weights do very little per dollar on the common scale, so ACE does too.

### What the charity does

ACE spent $6.58 million in its year to March 2025, by its audited accounts. Of that, $4.62 million was grants: donors' money passed on through its Recommended Charity Fund, gifts earmarked for a Recommended Charity, and its Movement Grants. Those grants are the recipients' cost, so they are taken out here. So is $242,000 of advertising Google donated. What is left, $1.72 million, is ACE's own spending: charity evaluations, Movement Grants grantmaking, fundraising for its Recommended Charities, and administration. Most of it is staff.

ACE says that in the same year it influenced at least $12.3 million of giving to its Recommended Charities. It puts $6.4 million of that down to its own work. It also awarded $1.2 million in Movement Grants to 32 small groups, of which it counts $0.84 million as money the groups would not otherwise have got.

### How the impact happens

ACE does no animal work itself. It finds charities it thinks do the most good, and donors give to them because of it. Some of that money is new giving. Some is giving that would have gone to other charities anyway. Its Movement Grants fund small and new groups, many of them in countries where animal advocacy has little funding.

### How the model works

The model starts from the $13.5 million of giving ACE traced in the year. It takes ACE's own estimate of the share it caused (54%), from its surveys of donors, grantmakers and charities. Then it applies the site's rule for an organisation's own survey: people who didn't answer are counted at half the influence of those who did. ACE publishes no response rates, so we judge that about 40% of the giving was covered. That leaves 38%, about $5.1 million.

Each dollar moved is valued at the charities it reached, read live from their own models on this site:

- **Recommended Charities.** These are the 11 ACE recommended in November 2024, which its February 2025 fund round paid. Eight have a figure on the rankings: The Humane League, Wild Animal Initiative, Shrimp Welfare Project, Sinergia Animal, Çiftlik Hayvanlarını Koruma Derneği, Dansk Vegetarisk Forening, Good Food Fund and Aquatic Life Institute. ACE publishes no split by charity, so they get equal weights. The other three take their average.
- **Movement Grants** go to small groups with no model here. They are valued at the animal-welfare charities on the rankings, with equal weights.

Half the money counts as new giving. The other half counts only for what it gains over an ordinary animal charity (below). A further dollar is assumed to do 36% as much as ACE's average dollar so far.

### What drives the result

- **The animal weights.** Everything ACE moves goes to animals, so the result scales with how much the site counts animal suffering against human health.
- **Its next-dollar figure (36%, 15–70%).** ACE holds about 1.3 years of its own costs, and says extra money would mostly go to advertising and events.
- **The non-response step.** Counting every donor at full influence would raise money moved by about two fifths.
- **What shifted money would otherwise have done** (below).

### Where we differ from the shared method

For shifted money, the site's rule asks what that money would otherwise have done, and values it at GiveDirectly. That fits donors who would otherwise give to global health. ACE's shifted money comes from donors who would have given to animals anyway. Valued against GiveDirectly, every dollar ACE shifts would count as a loss of about 4 healthy years per $1,000. That is a choice between causes its donors never faced. So the model keeps the shared rule's ratio of an ordinary charity to the best in its cause, about 13% at the defaults and moving with the same setting, and applies it to the animal-welfare charities on the rankings.

The charities' figures are ACE's own evaluations of them, because those are the figures the rankings show. This is the site's rule for every destination. It means the model is not an independent check of ACE's recommendations.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether ACE's own survey-based estimate of the giving it caused holds up. We could not read the full method: its report is not public.
- How the giving it influenced splits across its Recommended Charities. The Humane League's figure is ten times the others', so its share matters.
- Whether shifted animal money would otherwise have gone to an ordinary animal charity, or to a better or worse one.

### What isn't modelled

Side effects are one row, 10% (−10% to +30%) of the value. On the plus side: its reviews are used by funders and donors who don't report it, and its feedback helps evaluated charities. On the minus side: the staff time charities spend on its evaluations, and charities shaping their work to its criteria. ACE's research and public writing have no separate branch.
