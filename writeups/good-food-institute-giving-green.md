### Summary

The Good Food Institute (GFI) works to make alternative proteins (plant-based, cultivated and fermentation-made meat) cheap and good enough to replace some livestock, by funding and steering research, lobbying for public research money and supporting the industry. Giving Green estimates that GFI's work from 2016 to 2022 keeps a tonne of CO₂-equivalent out of the air for about $2.98, by slightly raising the chance of a world where alternative proteins take off. On our scale that is **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="good-food-institute-giving-green">0.051</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**: livestock tonnes avoid only heat deaths, which come decades from now and are discounted like any future good.

The model reproduces all 8 of Giving Green's calculated figures in its spreadsheet.

### What the charity does

GFI is a US-based network of nonprofits with affiliates in Asia, Europe, Israel, India and Latin America. It funds open-access research, maps the science that is missing, lobbies governments to fund alternative-protein research and to give new products a fair regulatory path, and helps companies and investors enter the field.

### How the impact happens

Giving Green's chain:

1. Two possible worlds, from Vivid Economics' modelling for ClimateWorks: one where alternative proteins get the research they need, one where only the market pays for it. Giving Green makes the first less optimistic about cultivated meat and puts the second halfway between, because governments already fund some research.
2. The gap in livestock production between them, summed until the two worlds converge (15 years in the best guess), times about 14 kg of CO₂e per kg of livestock (including land cleared for it): about 6 billion tonnes.
3. GFI's part: research, talent and public funding each raise the chance of the better world by about 12.5%, and GFI is credited with 3% and 1.5% of that work. Together about a 0.56% higher chance.
4. GFI's whole 2016–22 budget, $101 million, divided by its expected share of the 6 billion tonnes.

### How the model works

Giving Green's rows come first, ending at its bottom line in dollars per tonne, followed by its comparisons with an offset price and with its $1-a-tonne benchmark. Our steps follow:

- **Tonnes per dollar:** one over the cost per tonne, discounted for the years until the tonnes are avoided.
- **Tonnes without fuel smoke:** the shared climate rate, "Deaths caused by one more tonne of CO₂", counts deaths from heat and from the fine particles that come with burning fossil fuels. Livestock emissions are methane, nitrous oxide and CO₂ from cleared land, with no fuel smoke, so the model's row "Air pollution these tonnes cut, relative to an average fossil-fuel tonne" is 0 (0–0.05, our judgment: livestock chains burn some fuel, but making alternative proteins takes energy too), and the site values them for their heat deaths.
- **Staff pay:** with credit by share of resources on, staff's forgone pay counts as part of the cost.

The site turns these tonnes into years of healthy life through the heat deaths they avoid: 23% of "Deaths caused by one more tonne of CO₂", each valued by the age it comes at like every death on the site, discounted at "Next year's good counts less by" over "Years until the heat deaths from a tonne of CO₂".

### What drives the result

- **How long the gap between the two worlds lasts** (15 years; 5 to 20). Giving Green's guess, and it sets the livestock gap.
- **GFI's shares of the research and funding work** (3% and 1.5%), Giving Green's guesses.
- **Deaths caused by one more tonne of CO₂** and the discounting of heat deaths, values you set.

### Where we differ from Giving Green

- **Deaths valued by age.** Each death a tonne avoids is valued at GiveWell's moral weight for the age it comes at, over the ages of today's heat and air-pollution deaths (GBD 2023: most are of people over 50), about 30 and 24 healthy years, where the site used GiveWell's all-ages adult value (36.6) until late October 2026: the same rule as every death on the site.
- **A marginal dollar, not the past average.** Giving Green's figure is GFI's 2016–22 budget over its expected effect: an average. The next dollar does less; our judgment counts it at 50% (18% to 100%), one figure shared with every model on the site built on a past average (The Life You Can Save's leverage ratio, the other retrospective climate models). The evaluator view keeps 100%.
- **Tonnes become healthy years** through the shared climate rate. Giving Green stops at dollars per tonne.
- **Livestock tonnes count for their heat deaths only**, because they come with no fuel smoke.
- **Future deaths are discounted.** Like every model on the site, ours discounts future good at the shared rate.
- **The wait for the tonnes is discounted.** Giving Green counts a tonne the same whenever it is avoided; the livestock gap between Giving Green's scenarios is widest around 2050, so the tonnes come about 25 years after the 2016–22 spending (15 to 35, our judgment). Like every future benefit on the site, the tonnes are discounted for that wait at the shared rate, in both of the site's views, since Giving Green takes no view on discounting.
- **Staff pay:** with credit by share of resources, staff's forgone pay (our judgment for research and policy work) counts as an in-kind contribution.

### Biggest uncertainties

- Whether alternative proteins reach price and taste parity at all, and how much any one organisation changes that. Giving Green calls its own estimate low-confidence and uses it only as a rough plausibility check; its range runs from $0.68 to $192 a tonne.
- The model credits GFI's past spending with emissions after 2022, assuming no further work; it stands in for what a donation now buys.
- Methane forms ozone, which kills; those deaths aren't in the shared rate yet.

### What isn't modelled

Fewer animals raised for food, which matters on its own in our animal-welfare models; land freed for nature, which Giving Green counts in its biodiversity recommendation of GFI but doesn't quantify; and benefits for health and pandemic risk from less livestock.
