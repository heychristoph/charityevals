### Summary

Habitat for Humanity International (HFHI) funds and supports about 984 independent US affiliates that build homes with volunteers and sell them at appraised value, on zero-interest mortgages with down-payment help, to low-income families, and that repair low-income owners' homes. In the year to June 2025 HFHI spent $185 million on this work, against about 3,400 homes sold and 12,370 repairs across the network, and on disaster recovery after Hurricanes Helene and Milton. Our estimate of a dollar given to HFHI for its US work is **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals">0.003</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**.

That is very low for two reasons the model makes explicit. The families repay much of what their homes are worth, so only the net subsidy they receive counts, and the affiliates spend well over the sale price per home, so a dollar of the network's resources buys under half a dollar of subsidy; and they live in the US, where a dollar of better housing adds far less to a family's consumption than in a poor country, on the same log-income scale the site uses for cash transfers. And no controlled study measures what Habitat's homes or repairs do, so every branch takes the site's shared discount for missing data. This covers HFHI's US programme, 65% of its programme spending; its public awareness, education and advocacy (modelled separately; that model is held back until a review finds no high finding) and its international programme ([international model](/habitat-for-humanity/habitat-for-humanity-charityevals-international), <span data-figure="headline" data-model="habitat-for-humanity-charityevals-international">0.07</span>) are modelled separately.

### What the charity does

HFHI is the umbrella of the Habitat network. In the US, each affiliate is its own charity: it raises money locally, buys land, builds or rehabilitates homes with volunteers and the future owners' "sweat equity", and sells them to families earning about 30% to 80% of the area median income. Homes are sold at appraised value; the mortgage traditionally carries no interest, and part of the price is down-payment help or a forgivable second mortgage, repaid only if the family sells early. Affiliates also run critical-repair programmes for low-income owners, mostly older people.

HFHI's $185 million US programme (audited, FY2025; about $2 million of it US advocacy, taken out here and counted with the public awareness programme) is grants and house-building transfers to affiliates ($88 million), donated products such as appliances and materials ($32 million), and the staff, training, lending (Habitat Capital) and services behind the network ($64 million).

### How the impact happens

1. A family gets a home worth more than the present value of what it pays for it: that net subsidy is a transfer, enjoyed as better or cheaper housing over the years in the home.
2. A repair gives an owner a sound roof, furnace or ramp they would otherwise have done without or paid for.
3. HFHI's dollar buys a share of all of this, alongside the affiliates' own money, government grants and volunteers' time.

### How the model works

The model follows the site's template for social services in rich countries, with three branches and one credit step:

- **New homes.** The appraised price of a home ($180,000, Builder magazine's figure for Habitat's 3,370 closings in 2025), less the value of the buyer's mortgage payments (an interest-free loan priced at the 6.8% a 30-year mortgage cost over the year) and of the family's sweat-equity hours, gives the net subsidy: about $109,000 a home, for about four people. It is spread as an even yearly flow over the years in the home and valued per person on the site's log-consumption scale, at the buyers' consumption (about 35% of the US average per person, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics' spending by income fifth and Habitat families' larger households).
- **Repairs.** A net subsidy of about $8,000 a repair, spread over the repair's life, at the consumption of mostly older, low-income owners (the poorest fifth's spending, per person in a two-person home).
- **Disaster recovery.** About a tenth of the programme (a judgment: HFHI doesn't split it) went to clean-up and repairs after Hurricanes Helene and Milton; the households HFHI's own disaster dollars reached, at about $1,000 of help each, with a lower counterfactual (FEMA and other charities also help).
- **HFHI's share.** All the resources behind the year's homes and repairs: the affiliates' full cost (about 2.1 times the sale price, from the audited statements of Houston, Greater Sacramento and San Diego; repairs at 1.35 times the work) net of buyers' payments, volunteers' hours at the US value of volunteer time, and HFHI's own cost, without counting HFHI's transfers to affiliates twice. HFHI's share is about 15%.

Adjustments: a home is worth less to a family than the same money in cash (in-kind and illiquid), some families would have found other help, some default or sell early, and the counts are Habitat's own. Wellbeing and health beyond the money value are not counted in the chain (no controlled study measures them for Habitat); stability for children and falls averted by repairs are a positive share on top. Each branch is counted at the shared missing-data discount (half, by default): Habitat counts its closings and repairs, but the value each family gets rests on judgments and on research about housing assistance in general.

### What drives the result

- **How much we count a programme whose results aren't measured** (a site-wide slider): every branch takes it.
- **The value of a healthy year against a doubled income** (a site-wide slider): the result is all income.
- **The affiliates' full cost per home**, which sets HFHI's share.
- **How much a subsidy dollar is worth to a family against cash**, and **the share of the homes and repairs that wouldn't have happened anyway**.
- **What isn't modelled** (children's stability, falls averted), the mortgage's terms and the share of the price the family repays.

### Biggest uncertainties

- No national figures for the affiliates' full cost per home, their down-payment help or the subsidy per repair: three affiliates' audited statements are the anchor, all urban (a fourth, Greater New Haven, sells below market value and can't be compared).
- HFHI's share also rests on judgments about volunteers' hours.
- Benefits beyond money (children's stability and schooling, health) are measured only in uncontrolled surveys.

### What isn't modelled

HFHI's US policy work is modelled with its public awareness and education programme ($24.3 million), no longer as a share here. HFHI's international programme ($75.5 million) is modelled separately.

### How this model was checked

Three rounds of independent review, each by a fresh reviewer working from the model alone, re-checking the sources.

- **Round 1** (0 high, 7 medium, 9 low): one adjustment answered three questions (split into the in-kind value and, under delivery, defaults and early sales); disaster recovery after Hurricanes Helene and Milton became its own branch; the buyers' consumption now reads the Bureau of Labor Statistics' spending by income fifth (a new shared figure for the second fifth); sweat equity is netted out of the subsidy; the volunteer-hour value was re-sourced.
- **Round 2** (1 high, 3 medium, 6 low): the reviewer read two affiliates' audited statements (Houston, Greater Sacramento). The affiliates' full cost per home went from 1.35 to 1.7 times the sale price (the high finding); the interest-free loan was priced at the 8.02% accounting rate HFHI sets for affiliates' accounts, not at the site's time preference (since the rebuild, at the family's market alternative); homes are sold at appraised value, so the subsidy is the share the family doesn't repay; the home count is Builder magazine's closings.
- **Round 3** (0 high, 3 medium, 7 low): the summary was rewritten, and the share of the value families repay now covers Sacramento's model too (0.75, range 0.5 to 0.95). Left open: the affiliates' full cost rested on two small affiliates.
- **Rebuild, 4 November 2026**: under the site's new rule for missing data, each branch now takes the shared discount (Habitat measures none of its outcomes); and the advocacy share moved to the awareness model. Three rounds of independent review of the rebuild: round 1 (1 high, 2 medium, 7 low): the families' consumption had been moved to a household-survey base no other model of the family uses (moved back to the shared national-accounts figure); the affiliates' cost rested on two affiliates (San Diego's statements added: about 2.1 times the sale price); people per home now follow the homes; repairs got their own cost ratio, the loan is priced at the family's market alternative (6.8%), and the in-kind value moved into the chain. Round 2 (0 high, 1 medium, 5 low): the disaster households' consumption put on the same relative scale; the US advocacy cost booked in this line taken out (a shared figure the awareness model adds to its cost); ReStore volunteers out of the homes' resources. Round 3 (0 high, 1 medium, 4 low): the affiliates' cost ratio set between its sales-weighted and equal-weighted figures (2.1); explainers and drivers fixed. Left open: Builder magazine's closings have no second source (its page refuses our fetch), and the affiliates are all large urban ones.
