### Summary

All Hands and Hearts sends volunteers, led by its own staff, to disasters. In the US they muck out and sanitise flooded homes, clean smoke-damaged houses and repair homes. Abroad it rebuilds schools and funds relief through local partners. Its US work in the year to August 2025 comes to **<span data-figure="headline" data-model="all-hands-and-hearts-charityevals">0.003</span> years of healthy life per $1,000**, its work abroad to about 0.09 (a model of its own).

The US figure is far below the site's global-health charities. The help goes to American households, and the site's one income scale values a dollar of help to them at a small fraction of what it does for a family living on a few dollars a day. Volunteers' unpaid time and donated materials are also much of what the work takes, so All Hands and Hearts' cash gets about half the credit.

### What the charity does

The latest audited year runs to 31 August 2025. The accounts book $12.8 million of programme spending, $1.6 million of it donated goods and services, so $11.2 million in cash. They don't split it by country, and the Form 990 has no list of spending abroad. We judge that about 18% went abroad (10% to 30%). Both models read that one figure, so together they always cover all the spending.

- **US** ($9.2 million in cash). The Form 990 names three programmes: Hurricane Helene in western North Carolina ($2.7 million), Helene and Milton in Pasco County, Florida ($2.4 million) and the Los Angeles fires ($1.4 million). The Texas floods began in July 2025. Over the year the US teams did 800 muck and guts and 370 mould sanitations, and cleaned 170 smoke-damaged homes in Los Angeles. They also repaired homes, partly with contractors, but publish no count of them.
- **Abroad** (about $2.0 million). Bandevi School in Nepal, two bamboo-frame schools in the Philippines and 17 classrooms in Oaxaca, Mexico, were rebuilt or repaired. After the Myanmar earthquake, local partners delivered cash, water, shelter materials and hygiene supplies, then ran a water and hygiene programme. Together they reached 13,000 people.

### How the impact happens

The good is mostly the work itself. A household that would have had to pay a contractor, wait for another group or live in a mouldy house gets the job done. That is valued like money: the work's price, on the site's log-income scale at the household's own consumption. Repairs, and the schools abroad, are valued over the years they last. The relief in Myanmar is valued as goods, plus the few deaths that clean water and shelter avert after a disaster. The volunteers also gain some purpose and belonging, counted as a side effect.

### How the model works

- **Response work** (US). Each muck and gut, sanitation and smoke clean-up counts at a judged contractor's price: $6,000, $2,500 and $8,000. Help in kind counts at 80% of its price, and the clients' consumption is set a little above that of America's poorest fifth. This branch is about four-fifths of the US result.
- **Repairs** (US). An estimated 90 homes at $20,000 of repairs each, valued as housing over 15 years.
- **Volunteers' wellbeing.** The 2,526 volunteers gain a little life satisfaction for the year, discounted for those who would have volunteered elsewhere.
- **Credit.** All Hands and Hearts' cash is about half the resources behind the US work. The rest is the volunteers' unpaid hours (about 190,000, at the US value of a volunteer hour), donated goods and services, and partners.
- **Abroad.** Each school counts at its cost, spread over its pupils and its years of use, and only for the years it brought the school forward. The Myanmar relief is valued at $40 a person.
- **Missing data.** Every branch takes the site's missing-data step. The counts are All Hands and Hearts' own and rounded, and what the work is worth to those served is a judgment.

### What drives the result

The missing-data and income-to-health sliders move the US result most. Next come what others would have done anyway, the price of a muck and gut, how poor the clients are, and how many hours the volunteers gave. Abroad, the relief's value per person and the share of spending abroad matter most.

### Biggest uncertainties

- What the work is worth to those served. Contractors' prices vary widely, and some households would have done part of the work themselves.
- What others would have done anyway. Other voluntary groups do the same work, as do insurers and FEMA's assistance.
- How the spending splits between the US and abroad. The accounts don't say.

### What isn't modelled

The US model leaves out several benefits. Prompt muck-outs avert some mould-related illness. In Los Angeles, partner grants paid for cash vouchers, day-workers clearing burn zones and children's mental-health support. The first days of the Texas floods brought food, water and temporary housing. Small businesses were repaired and site supervisors trained. It also leaves out two harms: injuries to volunteers and work taken from local contractors. These are in one share of +15%, from −5% to +40%. Abroad, it leaves out pupils' learning and later earnings, lives saved in future earthquakes by resilient schools, the women trained as masons in Nepal and the diarrhoea the water work averts (+20%, from −5% to +50%).

### How this model was checked

Independent audits, kept in audits/all-hands-and-hearts-charityevals/ and audits/all-hands-and-hearts-charityevals-international/.
