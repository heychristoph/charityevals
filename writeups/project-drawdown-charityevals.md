### Summary

Project Drawdown researches which climate solutions matter most and helps funders, companies and investors put their money there. Our model values that advice by the money it changed in 2023-2025. Each dollar counts only for how much better its new use is than its old one. Against what Drawdown spent over those years, that comes to **{{result}} years of healthy life per $1,000**. A large part of it is foundation and family giving that follows its guidance, valued at the ranked climate charities' figures over an ordinary climate charity's. The result is very uncertain: nobody has counted the decisions it changed, so the money and Drawdown's part in it are our judgments.

### What the charity does

Project Drawdown spent $5.6 million in 2025, $4.4 million of it on programmes (Form 990). Its audited figures, $6.0 million and $4.7 million, add donated services. The tax return splits the programmes:

- **Science: $2.9 million (66%).** Research on climate solutions and Drawdown Explorer (2025), a free platform covering about 150 solutions, their costs and where they matter most. Its "emergency brake" work puts fast cuts in methane and other short-lived pollutants first. Drawdown Food covers food and farming emissions.
- **Drawdown Labs: $0.8 million (17%).** Work with companies (job-function action guides, training, the Drawdown Business Coalition) and the Drawdown Capital Coalition for impact investors and philanthropists.
- **Drawdown Stories: $0.4 million (8%).** Video and storytelling for the public: Drawdown's Neighborhood, the Global Solutions Diary, and SHIFT, a guide to effective personal action.
- **Communications: $0.3 million (8%).** Media, marketing and sharing the research.

### How the impact happens

Drawdown cuts no emissions itself. It changes where other people's climate money and effort go. The model has a branch for each kind of reader:

- **Funders.** Foundations and individuals gave $11.7-18.4 billion for climate mitigation in 2024 (ClimateWorks). We judge that 0.5% of a year's giving, about $75 million, follows a choice Drawdown's work changed. Its testimonials come from family foundations and ReFED's food-waste funders, but no funder names an amount.
- **Companies.** Corporate climate budgets that companies working with Drawdown Labs move from offsets and carbon accounting toward real cuts: $15 million a year (our judgment).
- **Investors.** Climate funds built on its list of solutions, such as Carbon Collective's ETF: $100 million a year. Buying climate shares instead of others changes very little, so each dollar is worth only a small gain.
- **The public.** About 3,000 people a year making a lasting change (diet, flights, driving, heating), worth half a tonne a year each.

### How the model works

This is a value-of-information model: money whose use changed × how much better the new use is.

Funders' giving would have gone to climate work anyway, so it is shifted money, valued by the site's one rule for spending that research or advice changed. A dollar at the climate charities on the rankings is worth their figure there, each with its own steps. Where it would have gone is an ordinary climate charity, which stands to them as GiveDirectly stands to the global-health charities. The giving counts once, in the year it is given, and only 70% of the gain is counted as real, since advice can point the wrong way or overstate the difference between options.

Companies' and investors' money is valued by the difference in tonnes per dollar between the new and old use. An ordinary corporate climate dollar is put at 0.01 tonnes (0.002 to 0.04), and Drawdown's advice makes it do 30% more. An invested dollar gains 0.0003 tonnes, since it comes back. These changes count for the shared years a won reform counts, after a year's wait.

Each branch then takes the share that wouldn't have happened anyway (40-60%) and Drawdown's share of the work behind the change. Other advisers and guides (ClimateWorks, Giving Green, Founders Pledge), grantees pitching to funders and consultants share the work; the funders, companies and fund managers who adopt a choice are not counted here but under the shared share for money and work it brings in. Donated services are others' resources: they are left out of the cost and lower Drawdown's share instead. Then come the shared evidence (70%) and missing-data steps and two side effects: other good not modelled, such as policy advice and research others cite (+15%), and companies using the partnership as cover (−3%). The total is divided by a typical year's spending in 2023-2025. The next dollar is valued at {{row:marginal_vs_past_average}} of that average. Drawdown holds about a year's cash, and new money goes to programmes not yet tried (Drawdown Capital, Explorer 2.0).

### What drives the result

Companies' money is the largest part of the result, about half; funders' giving is about a third, and investors' and the public's the rest. The biggest swings are how much giving follows its guidance, the shared multiple for an ordinary charity against the ranked ones, and Drawdown's share of the work behind a funder's choice. A shifted dollar is valued at the ranked climate charities' figures, and forest protection is among them.

### Biggest uncertainties

- **How much money it really moves.** Drawdown says its work has "helped guide billions of dollars", but that is a total since 2017 and includes money that would have gone the same way. No funder has published what it changed.
- **Whether its advice beats an ordinary climate charity.** The shared method values the money at the ranked climate charities' figures. Drawdown's rankings long favoured big, mature solutions that markets already fund.
- **Drawdown's part.** Funders weigh many advisers; its share is our judgment, from 20% to 70%.

### What isn't modelled

Its influence before 2023 is left out, along with its cost: the 2017 book and the funds and companies started because of it. The policy advice it gives governments has no named win, so it sits in the +15% for other good.

### How this model was checked

Independent audits are in audits/project-drawdown-charityevals/. Round 1 found the funders' giving valued by our own tonnes-per-dollar judgments; it now goes through the site's shared money-moved rows, which raised the result about eightfold. It also removed an evidence discount that repeated the missing-data step and timing reasons from a counterfactual that the years counted already cover. Donated services left the cost. Round 2 found the shifted giving read the climate charities without their own missing-data and age steps; the model now reads them as a money mover does, which lowered the result by about two-thirds. Before the last round the funders' branch moved to the site's new shared rule for spending research changed, with its evidence step (70%).
