## Drivers
1. `$discount_rate` (shared slider; 2.8–78.6). The benefits start about 27 years after a win and last 40 years.
2. `$win_years_counted` (shared slider; 2.9–41).
3. `@lead.paint_share_of_child_bll_lmic`: a judgment, 15% (4–40%; 3.6–35.8). It is the biggest own uncertainty and has no measurement. A blood-lead source-apportionment study (isotope ratios) in a LEEP country, or LEEP's planned estimate, would improve the model most.
4. `$missing_data_discount` (shared; 2.7–26.9).
5. `@lead.iq_points_per_ug_dl`: an estimate, 0.5 from Lanphear's pooled cohorts (4.0–21.5). It is sourced and verified. The model takes the median child's 5 µg/dL with a linear slope. A log-linear slope (6.9 points over ln(30/2.4), so 2.73 per ln unit) gives about 0.44 IQ points for a 15% cut, against the model's 0.375. This is within the range, so no finding.

Next come the next-dollar figure (sourced, 6.4–24), `cohorts_per_win_year` (a judgment with a sound steady-state argument), the credit share and the household multiplier.

Peers: 13.4 is 196× the global-health median of 112 models (0.0686), mostly direct-service and advocacy models of very different work. The row that explains the gap is reach. About 18.6M births a year across 14 countries (7.3M with Nigeria at half) are protected for 5 counted years, at an average cost of about $1.0M a year ($5.4M over 5.3 years). That gives roughly 0.05 uov per child-cohort, about 0.4 IQ points valued as earnings. LEEP's own testing supports the reach: measured market switches in Pakistan and Malawi, seven rules adopted. The gap comes from the evidence and the site's counting rules, not from a different programme, year or counting rule, so it is not a finding. (Audit-only note, never for site text.)
