That spread is honest: the word-reading effect is a null whose interval includes harm-level negatives, and the weight decides how much of it counts. The judged inputs on the path are anchored. All three point values (cost and the two counts) are the charity's own reported figures.

What would most improve the model: the November 2026 endline with comparison schools (a two-year effect on words and sounds, and the first P3 estimate), and a composite reading score or the subtask correlation from the charity's own data (finding 1).

## Peers
The result is ×152 the median of 143 global-health models. That median is dominated by health services and advocacy in rich countries, and the far-above gap is not an error. Within the same intervention and chain (cash-income template, a learning gain valued as later earnings through the same shared facts), `ln_gain_per_person` is 0.551, the same as Imagine Worldwide's (its own learning gain best guess is also 0.25 SD). The differences are cost per child and adjustments. Learning Alliance spent about $12 per pupil-year all-in, against Imagine's about $60 ($22.9M / 384,500), and its `total_adj` is 0.48 against 0.30, where Imagine's implementation and missing-data steps for children outside Malawi bite. Cost per pupil is the charity's own reported figure. It is low because a coach supports about 46 teachers and the government teachers teach the lessons. The effect is the charity's own controlled estimate. The gap therefore comes from evidence the model reads correctly, not from a different programme, year or counting rule. Per INV-15's retirement and the no-peer-comparison rule, this explanation belongs here, not in the summary.
